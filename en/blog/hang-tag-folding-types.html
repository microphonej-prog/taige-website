Folded Hang Tags: Bi-fold, Accordion, Multi-Panel and Card Formats

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 12, 2026

Folded hang tags — bi-fold, accordion or multi-panel — solve a simple problem: information that will not fit on one small card buys extra panels through folding. But folding adds creasing, folding, gluing or stitching steps, and panel layout, paper weight and hole placement all have to be recalculated. Get it wrong and the tag will not close squarely, the fold line will crack, or it will hang crooked.

This guide runs through four parts: when folding is worth it, how the four common structures compare, how to lay out panels across the fold, and the process points for creasing and hole placement — closing with grammage guidance and a five-step sampling check.

1. When a Folded Hang Tag Is Worth It

Folding suits brands whose content has outgrown a single card and who refuse to shrink type until it cannot be read. The test is simple: if the copy only fits below 6pt, or the tag must carry composition, care, several languages, brand story and a QR code at once, folding beats a longer single card.

  • Multiple languages: European and North American markets often need care and composition text in four to six languages, which no single card holds
  • Layered information: the cover keeps the brand and key visual, while the inner panels carry composition, sizes, care and origin, so detail appears only when opened
  • Combined functions: an inner panel can hold a slot or a small stapled sachet of spare buttons and thread, turning the tag into a booklet that ships with the garment
  • Substance: folded tags feel thicker and suit mid-to-high price points and gift-box lines, but look bulky on fine knits and underwear

2. How the Four Common Folding Structures Compare

Fix the structure before the size. What separates these options is not how many times the sheet folds but whether the tag lies flat when opened and squares up when closed — and that in turn decides the layout and how much content fits.

Bi-foldTwo sides, four panels in total, the cheapest option, opens flat and closes squareBrand story, front-and-back content, gift-box lines

3. Laying Out Panels Across the Fold

A folded tag is not a single card simply sliced in half. The fold consumes visual space, and any artwork near it risks being crushed into the crease, rubbed, or worn away once the tag is closed — so those zones must be avoided at layout stage.

  • Artwork across the fold: only structures that open flat, such as a bi-fold or saddle stitch, can carry a continuous image; accordion folds alternate direction and the halves will always misalign
  • Safe distance: keep text 3–5mm clear of the fold and images or solid blocks at least 5mm away, so the crease never runs through detail
  • Narrow the inner panel: on three-panel folds the panel folded innermost must be 1–2mm narrower than the outer ones, or the tag bulges and will not close square
  • Cover first: shoppers mostly see the cover and the area around the hole, so the brand name, key visual and main selling point belong on panel one
The cheapest habit is to fold before you finalise: make the tag at real size, fold it, and flip through it in your hand before approving artwork. A layout looks far better flat on screen, and only after closing it do you notice the fold eating half the cover.

4. Creasing, Hole Placement and Grain Direction

Folded tags must be creased before folding; folding a flat sheet directly cracks the fold, raises fibres or exposes the core. Hole placement and grain direction cannot be guessed either.

  • Creasing: coated and board stocks must be creased for a bi-fold; after lamination the crease matters even more, because the film cracks along the fold first
  • Hole placement: put the hole on the cover or outermost panel, at least 8–10mm from the fold; a hole straddling the crease saves space on paper but makes the inner panel curl once hung
  • Grain direction: a fold running parallel to the grain folds cleanly and resists cracking; folding across the grain shows up badly on thick board and laminated sheets
  • Combined with die-cutting: when a folded tag also uses a shaped die, the fold must sit inside the die's safe zone, otherwise it cannot be folded or it bites into the shaped edge

5. Matching Paper Weight to the Fold

Folded tags generally run one step lighter than single cards. Higher grammage means more stiffness but also more spring-back and edge cracking after folding, and more bulk overall. Choosing by fold type is safer than following single-card habits.

  • Bi-fold: 250–300g coated paper or board is the norm; above 350g, assess fold cracking and spring-back before committing
  • Accordion and multi-page: 200–250g folds more cleanly and stays flat when closed; too heavy and it springs open like a concertina
  • Insert card: 200–250g works, or switch material against the cover — soft-touch or recycled stock — to build a visible layer

6. Five Steps to Check a Folding Sample

  1. Fold centred: after closing, both edges must align; a centre offset visible to the eye fails
  2. Closes flat: no lifted edges or bulging, and it should not rock on a table
  3. Fold appearance: no cracking, exposed core or ink loss along the crease; on laminated tags check whether the film lifts
  4. Hole and attachment: fit the real cord or fastener, then confirm the fold does not pull on the hole and the tag hangs straight
  5. Durability and reference: run a repeated open-close and rub test, keep the approved sample and note paper, grammage, fold type and crease position
