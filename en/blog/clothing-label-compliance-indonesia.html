Indonesia Label Compliance: Customs, Regulator and Shelf Ask Different Questions

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 22, 2026

Indonesia is one of the fastest-growing apparel markets in Southeast Asia, and one of the least forgiving for a label that was designed for Europe and shipped unchanged. Customs reads the importer and origin lines. The regulator checks whether a regulated product carries SNI marking. Department stores and marketplaces check whether the care instructions can be understood in Bahasa Indonesia. None of the three cares how the hang tag looks. This guide lists what a label set for Indonesia has to contain, in the order a buyer will ask for it.

1. Three Checks, Three Sets of Documents

The most common mistake is expecting the single sewn-in care label to satisfy all three parties. An Indonesian label set normally has three parts: the sewn-in care label with composition, care and origin; the hang tag with brand, style and barcode; and the shipping documents with packing list, importer details and, where applicable, a copy of the SNI certificate. When a department store inspects, it cross-checks all three, and any mismatch means relabelling the lot.

2. SNI: Mandatory Only Where the Regulation Names It

SNI is the Indonesian National Standard (Standar Nasional Indonesia). For most apparel and trims it is voluntary, but for categories placed on the mandatory list, such as certain children's items, textiles and footwear, it becomes SNI wajib: the product must be certified and marked with the SNI logo and certificate number. There is only one reliable way to find out, and it is not a search engine: give the HS code and product description to your importer or customs broker and ask whether that line sits on the mandatory list. Never print the mark speculatively. An uncertified SNI logo is a worse finding than no logo at all.

  • Before the order, confirm with the importer whether the HS line is on the mandatory SNI list, and write the answer into the purchase order notes.
  • For certified categories, reserve a fixed print position for the certificate number on the care label reverse or hang tag back instead of adding a sticker later.
  • Writing 'no SNI mark' into the spec protects both sides: a mark you cannot legally use turns the lot into scrap.

3. Bahasa Indonesia: Which Lines the Consumer Must Understand

Indonesia's consumer protection law, UU No. 8/1999, requires product information to be provided in Indonesian and in a way the consumer can understand. In practice five lines get translated: composition (Komposisi), care (Cara pencucian), country of origin (Negara asal), importer (Diimpor oleh) and size (Ukuran). Bilingual Indonesian plus English is accepted. English alone is regularly rejected by store and marketplace reviewers, and Chinese, Japanese or Korean on their own count as no labelling at all.

Treat the five Indonesian lines as a second body text, not as small print on the hang tag edge. Store reviewers read the care label, and anything below about 1.5 mm cap height is judged unreadable.

4. Composition and Care: the Two Lines Behind Most Returns

List fibres one by one with names a local reader recognises: cotton as Katun, polyester as Poliester, elastane as Spandex or Elastane, each with a percentage. Put the blend tolerance into the spec, typically plus or minus 3 percent, and note that the permitted tolerance differs by market: for the European Union the fibre labelling rules sit in Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011. On care, print symbols and words together. Symbols alone are unfamiliar to many Indonesian shoppers, and words alone are hard to execute on the factory floor.

  • Use the Indonesian fibre names rather than English abbreviations alone; PE, PA and PU are routinely challenged in local inspections.
  • Base the care symbols on the ISO 3758 system, adopted in Indonesia as SNI ISO 3758, and add one Indonesian line under the symbols.
  • Write care more conservatively than for Europe: 30 °C, line dry in shade, no tumble drying. It visibly reduces complaints about shrinkage and cracked prints after washing.

5. Sizes and Weight: the 'All Size' Risk

Indonesian retail runs heavily on S to XXL, and many brands sell loose styles as All Size. For export and cross-border e-commerce, S/M/L on the size label is not enough: list measured dimensions in the spec and on the hang tag back, for example chest width 52 cm and body length 70 cm with a tolerance of plus or minus 1 cm. State fabric weight as 180 g/m² or 220 g/m² rather than gsm alone. For hijabs and long tunics, body and sleeve length must be given in cm; those are the two dimensions that drive the highest return rates locally.

6. A Label Table You Can Send to the Factory Today

Item Indonesian wording Where it goes
Country of origin Negara asal: Tiongkok Care label front or back; the carton must match
Importer Diimpor oleh: PT ... Care label reverse or hang tag back
Composition Komposisi: 95% Katun, 5% Spandex Care label front, cap height 1.5 mm or more
Care Cara pencucian: 30 °C, jangan diperas Care label, symbols plus words
Size Ukuran: M (chest 52 cm / length 70 cm) Size label plus hang tag back

7. Five Numbers to Fix Before the Order

  • Fibre percentages and tolerance, for example plus or minus 3 percent, written into the spec rather than left in an email.
  • Care label size and type height: 20 × 50 mm or 25 × 60 mm are common, with at least 1.5 mm text and one row of symbols.
  • Hang tag board weight: 300 to 350 g/m² normally, raised to 350 to 400 g/m² when foil stamping or embossing is used so the tag does not warp.
  • The Indonesian copy must be confirmed in writing by the importer, especially the importer name and address; one wrong letter can hold the shipment at customs.
  • Lead time: allow five to seven days each for label proofing and importer sign-off, and do not bury them inside the production window.

Indonesia rewards preparation. On the same garment, a supplier whose labels are right keeps earning repeat orders, while one whose labels are wrong stalls at the first shipment. Fix the three documents, the five Indonesian lines and the five numbers, and labelling stops being a risk and becomes a repeatable process.
