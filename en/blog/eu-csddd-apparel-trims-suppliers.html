CSDDD: A Trim Factory Is Not a Tier-3 Footnote, It Is Part of the Chain

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 22, 2026

Since the EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive, CSDDD or Directive (EU) 2024/1760, passed, legal teams at European brands and retailers have started doing something they rarely did before: moving trim suppliers off the purchasing list and onto the due diligence list. Hang tags, woven labels, care labels and packaging bags carry very low unit prices and now have to answer questions about human rights and environment. This article sets out how that actually lands on a trim factory, and how to sequence the preparation.

1. Why a Trim Factory Sits Inside the Chain

CSDDD requires in-scope EU companies to run due diligence across their chain of activities, which includes upstream manufacturing and processing. Trims have low unit prices, but brands order them directly, pay for them directly and specify them directly, so in practice they count as one of the upstream links the brand controls rather than a tier-3 supplier that has nothing to do with it. That is why a line has appeared in many recent RFQs: send your human rights and environmental policy, your latest audit report and your subcontractor list.

2. What It Asks the Buyer to Do, and Therefore You

At its core the directive is an inspectable process: identify risks, write a policy, take measures, track effectiveness, operate a grievance channel and report publicly. To prove it has done that, a brand can only ask upstream for evidence. What you receive is therefore not a declaration but a set of questions: how workers are recruited, how overtime is recorded, how chemicals are stored, how wastewater is handled, whether there is a grievance channel and who the work is subcontracted to. Suppliers who can answer stay on the list; those who cannot are rarely fined, they are quietly replaced.

3. The Four Documents Buyers Ask For First

  • 1. Human rights and environmental policy: one page is enough, but name the frameworks you follow (the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights, the ILO core conventions, REACH restrictions on chemicals) and sign it with a named owner.
  • 2. Latest social and quality audit: SMETA, BSCI, WRAP or a customer-specific audit, as long as it is under two years old and major findings have documented closure.
  • 3. Supply chain map: where the material comes from (paper, yarn, dye, metal parts) plus subcontractor names and addresses, ideally down to tier two.
  • 4. Grievance channel description: how workers and outsiders can raise an issue anonymously, who handles it and within what time. This one is underrated and it is the first thing an auditor samples.

These four documents do not need beautiful design, they need to reconcile. An auditor checks whether the subcontractors named in the policy match the supply chain map, and whether the response time promised by the grievance channel matches the actual log. Inconsistency is worse than absence, because it reads as concealment.

4. Contractual Assurances: Read Three Sentences Before Signing

The directive expects companies to use contractual assurances, where appropriate, as part of their measures. On a trim order that usually appears as an extra page behind the purchase contract. Only three sentences really need reading. First, does the assurance extend to your subcontractors, because an assurance that stops at your gate guarantees nothing. Second, what triggers a corrective action plan and who pays for it. Third, the termination clause: on a finding, can the customer walk away immediately or must it grant a remediation period. Those three sentences move your risk exposure more than the price terms do.

When you read that the supplier must ensure its subcontractors comply with the annex, ask three things first: how often the subcontractor list is updated, who maintains it, and whether the customer wants batch-by-batch notification. If you cannot answer, do not sign yet.

5. Traceability to the Lot Is Enough, If It Actually Reconciles

A trim factory does not need unit-level traceability. Three points that line up are enough for almost every audit: production lot, raw material lot and shipment lot. In practice, write the raw material lot on each production order, whether that is a paper roll number, a yarn lot or a dye bath number; put the same lot label on the outer packaging; and record the customer order number against that lot on the packing list. Then when a customer asks where the paper in a given batch came from, you can answer in ten minutes rather than three days.

  • Put the lot numbering rule into the work instruction instead of relying on one experienced operator's memory; it is the first thing to break when staff change.
  • Label material lots on arrival with the weight, for example 300 g/m² hang tag board or 120 g/m² care label tape, and the receipt date; those two facts are the most frequently requested during traceability checks.
  • For subcontracted steps, such as ultrasonic cutting of woven labels or hand stringing of hang tags, keep a record mapping the subcontractor to the lot; this is the gap auditors probe most often.

6. Timeline and a 90-Day Sequence

The directive entered into force in 2024, member states must transpose it into national law, and application is phased in. In 2025 the EU level saw simplification proposals affecting dates and thresholds. Do not plan around dates you read in a blog post; ask your European customer which phase they fall into and when their next questionnaire goes out. For a supplier, the operative fact is that the questionnaire arrives before the regulation does, and its deadline is usually two to four weeks.

Window What to do
Days 1 to 30 Assemble the four documents and make them reconcile: policy, audit, supply chain map, grievance channel. Fill gaps from existing records, never invent them.
Days 31 to 60 Run one internal drill: pick a batch of woven labels or hang tags and trace it from raw material lot to packing list, recording how long it took and where it stalled.
Days 61 to 90 Negotiate the contractual assurances with the customer: subcontractor notification frequency, remediation period and cost sharing. Settle all three before signing the annual contract.

One honest closing note: CSDDD does not add many direct legal duties for a trim factory. What it adds is the frequency of being asked to prove things. Rather than waiting for the questionnaire and scrambling, treat it as part of the quality system, because lot control, a subcontractor list and a grievance channel are things you should have anyway. Factories that move early gain something in the next round of RFQs: the customer knows you can withstand an inspection.
