Footwear & Bag Trim Labels: Shoe Labels, Woven Tags and Hang Tags

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 25, 2026

Footwear and bag trims look like a few small labels, yet the demands are tougher than on apparel: the product flexes, rubs, and meets sweat and rain, and the label has to keep up. This guide splits footwear and bag labelling into three layers — the material and origin marking required by law, brand identification such as tongue woven labels and leather patches, and retail elements such as hang tags and box labels — and closes with a material selection table and a sample approval checklist.

1. Three Layers of Requirements on Footwear and Bag Trims

  • Regulatory layer: material, origin and size marking that is mandatory or contractually demanded, where position and language both matter — get it wrong and you risk delisting or a customs hold
  • Brand layer: tongue labels, insole marks, leather patches and metal tags — these decide the first touch and the perceived grade
  • Retail layer: hang tags, box labels, dust bags, barcodes and price tickets — they shape in-store display and e-commerce inbound efficiency

2. EU Footwear Material Labelling: The Four Parts to Mark

Footwear sold in the EU must carry material information under Directive 94/11/EC, covering four parts: upper, lining and sock, and outer sole (some markets allow lining and sock to be combined). Marking may use words or the pictograms in the annex to the directive, and must be in the official language of the country of sale. The cheapest route is a combined label of pictograms plus multilingual text on the box — far less costly to change than a woven tongue label, and the first thing to revise when a market moves.

Part Typical marking Practical note
Upper Leather, coated leather, textile, etc. Must match the real product; for mixed builds mark the predominant material
Lining and sock Same list; some markets allow one combined entry The sock often carries the size too — keep it consistent with the box label
Outer sole Leather, rubber, plastic, textile, etc. For composite soles, mark the material covering the largest area
Country of origin Made in … / country of manufacture Often required separately by the destination country, usually on the sole or box
Note: Directive 94/11/EC covers material marking only; it does not replace origin marking, barcodes or the customer's own size requirements. In practice, keep regulatory items and brand items as two separate sets: when rules move, you only reprint labels and tags and leave the woven label plates untouched.

3. Common Footwear Trims: From Tongue Label to Box Label

  • Tongue label: usually a narrow woven label 20–40 mm wide, satin or plain weave; edges must stay soft, the position must not press on the instep, and width should be set from the real tongue width plus seam allowance
  • Insole mark: heat transfer or screen print applied straight to the sock; it takes sweat and constant rubbing, so run abrasion and perspiration tests before bulk
  • Size and side labels: small woven or printed marks that must line up with the tag and box label; parallel size systems are where errors happen most
  • Box label: the self-adhesive carrier for barcode, size, material and origin; cheapest to change, and therefore the first place to land a rule update or a customer revision
  • Hang tag: brand story, material notes and anti-counterfeit features; keep the string position clear of the try-on area and avoid pressure marks on the tongue

4. Bags and Leather Goods: Patches, Woven Labels and Hardware

Handbags and luggage have no EU-wide material labelling directive comparable to footwear, so material marking usually follows the customer, the retail platform and the destination market — but leather, coatings and metal parts remain subject to chemical and skin-contact rules, and that is where bag compliance really sits.

  • Leather patch: genuine, reconstituted or PU, finished by foil stamping, embossing or screen print; settle thickness and stitching first, because an over-thick patch shows through as stitch marks
  • Woven label: usually sewn into the lining or side seam and must match lining shrinkage, or it wrinkles after washing
  • Metal tags and hardware: parts in prolonged skin contact — buckles, rings, zip pulls — must respect nickel release limits; for the EU, confirm along REACH lines
  • Hang tag and dust bag: bag tags are usually larger and stiffer than apparel tags, so watch hole placement, string strength and transit creasing; dust bags are best in nonwoven or recyclable material
  • Bags entering the US: watch California Proposition 65 limits for lead and phthalates in leather, coatings and metal parts, and request material declarations early

5. Material and Process Comparison for Footwear and Bag Trims

Part Recommended process Durability point
Tongue (instep) Plain or satin woven label with soft edges Fold or hot-cut edges so nothing rubs the instep
Insole (high friction) Heat transfer or screen print with an abrasion-resistant coat Test abrasion and perspiration first, and confirm it survives shoe cleaning
Side and inner marks Narrow woven or printed labels Match upper shrinkage so the label is not stressed when the bond flexes
Leather patches Foil stamping, embossing or screen print Run a flex test: the foil layer must not crack or shed
Box and bag labels Self-adhesive: coated paper, synthetic paper or clear PET Choose face and adhesive for the humidity of storage and shipping; synthetic paper for sea freight

6. Sample Approval Checklist

  • Does the material marking match the real product, with all parts covered
  • Is the language the official one of the country of sale, and is a multilingual version needed
  • Woven label edge and thickness, and whether it feels harsh after folding
  • Are abrasion and perspiration results for the insole mark recorded with a retained sample
  • Label adhesion, humidity resistance and removability for returns
  • String strength and position, and whether size, barcode and box label all agree
