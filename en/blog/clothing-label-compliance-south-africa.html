South Africa Garment Labeling: CPA 68 of 2008, Care Labels and Import Rules

By TAIGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 9, 2026

South Africa is not a hard market to label for, but it is not a market where an English sticker is enough either. The Consumer Protection Act 68 of 2008 treats the label as part of the trade description: what the label says is what you promised the consumer. A wrong composition, a wrong origin or care wording that contradicts the symbols can all be handled as a misleading description.

This guide follows the order an importer or brand actually works in: what the law requires, which fields the label needs, how to word care instructions, how sizing and price tickets line up, packaging producer responsibility, and finally what has to be added when the same style also ships to the EU, Japan, Korea or Brazil.

1. CPA 68 of 2008: the label is the trade description

Section 24 requires product labelling without misleading descriptions, and requires the key information to be presented in a way the consumer can understand. In practice that means every field must be provable: composition backed by test reports or supplier data, origin consistent with the customs entry, and care wording that matches how the garment actually performs. On the technical side, the NRCS enforces compulsory specifications and the SANS standards set the test methods.

  • Composition must be true and verifiable. After a substitution or a blend change, an unchanged label is a misleading description.
  • Origin consistent with the customs entry. Regional wording such as 'made in Africa' is usually not enough.
  • Care wording must match measured performance: if the label says machine washable, there should be a wash test behind it.
  • Price is managed separately from labelling: a hang tag price that disagrees with the till is a pricing offence, a separate exposure.

2. What a South African label must carry

English is the working language of retail and customs, so English wording on the label is enough and a multilingual label is not required. The fields, however, cannot be dropped. These five are what importers and large retailers check line by line.

  • Fibre composition: percentages by weight, dominant fibre first, summing to 100%.
  • Care instructions: symbols plus English text, covering wash temperature, bleaching, drying, ironing and dry cleaning.
  • Size: the size system South African retail actually uses, with the body measurement in cm.
  • Origin and importer: country of manufacture and importer name and address, on the label or the packaging.
  • Traceability and style data: style, colour and batch codes that match the packing list and the retailer's system.

3. Care labelling: symbols plus plain English

South Africa uses the internationally recognised care symbols, in practice the ISO 3758 system, and retail expects a plain English line beside them. Writing 'cold wash, do not tumble dry' is safer than symbols alone, because in a consumer complaint the written instruction is the most direct evidence. Run one wash test in the customer's stated method, record shrinkage and appearance change, and keep the report in the technical file.

4. Sizes, measurements and price tickets

South African womenswear largely follows UK size numbers, menswear shirts are often sold by collar size in cm or as S-XXL, and childrenswear is marked by age or height. Price tickets are in rand (ZAR) and include 15% VAT; the hang tag price and the till price must agree. Fabric weight belongs in g/m² on the technical file and the hang tag, not squeezed onto the care label.

  • Womenswear: UK number sizes, with bust, waist and hip in cm.
  • Men's shirts: collar size in cm or S-XXL — giving both saves the most argument.
  • Childrenswear: height in cm or an age band, and keep an eye on small-part pull strength.

5. Packaging and producer responsibility for importers

Under the producer responsibility rules issued under the National Environmental Management: Waste Act 59 of 2008, South Africa applies registration and collection duties to the paper and packaging stream. Producers and importers above the threshold must join a producer responsibility organisation and report the volumes they place on the market. For a clothing brand the practical effect is that paper bags, hang tag board, cartons and poly bags must be countable, and suppliers must be able to declare material and g/m².

6. The same style to several markets: what the label adds

An English label is enough for South Africa, but not for the markets next to it. If the same shipment also goes to the EU, Japan, Korea or Brazil, one label per destination printed in the same run is the efficient route — and far more reliable than patching labels in the warehouse.

Destination What has to be added
EU, including Germany An EU responsible person under GPSR (EU) 2023/988 and fibre composition under (EU) No 1007/2011; in Germany the Pflegeetikett needs Faserzusammensetzung and EN ISO 3758 care symbols, with sizes in cm.
France and Spain Mandatory wording in French or Spanish — French under the Code de la consommation, Spanish under Real Decreto 928/1987 — with composición and care symbols.
Japan Japanese text under 家庭用品品質表示法 with 混用率, 洗濯表示 and 原産国; sizes in cm or 号.
Korea 취급 표시 with 혼용률 and 원산지 under 전기용품 및 생활용품 안전관리법, plus a manufacturer or importer field.
Brazil Portuguese etiqueta de conservação, composição and importer under the CDC (Lei 8.078/1990); sizes as PP/P/M/G/GG rather than S/M/L.

7. Five failures we see at retail quality checks

  • Symbols with no words: if the consumer cannot read the instruction, there is no instruction.
  • Percentages that do not match the test report, or a blend rounded the wrong way — 100% cotton on a garment with elastane.
  • Hang tag price not matching the till: a pricing offence, unrelated to label quality and still fined.
  • Blank importer field: the retailer cannot trace the responsible party and rejects the delivery.
  • Packaging without material and recyclability information: large retailers already list this as mandatory in their vendor manuals.
One rule: the South African hurdle is not language, it is provability. If composition, origin, care and price can each be backed by a document, the label stops being a route to claims.
