Heat Resistance and Ironing Compatibility for Garment Trims

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 26, 2026

Trims that pass every wash test can still fail at the ironing station: woven label edges stiffen and curl, care label print blisters, foil on a hang tag takes a shiny press mark, heat transfer labels whiten and lift at the edge. The cause is rarely poor quality — it is a mismatch between the trim's heat ceiling and the garment factory's pressing settings. This guide covers what ironing, pressing and steam setting do to each trim family, gives material-based temperature references, explains the three typical failures, and provides a pressing declaration sheet you can hand to the factory with your order.

Ironing is the last finishing step in garment making, and its temperature, pressure and dwell combine in a way that hits trims harder than a home wash: washing is heat plus mechanical action, pressing is heat plus sustained pressure. The safety margin is smaller than people assume, especially for anything containing thermoplastics — polyester, nylon, PVC or hot-melt adhesive.

1. Why Pressing Is Where Trims Fail

  • Dwell is underestimated: a hand iron touches for 3 to 5 seconds, while a press or finishing table often applies 10 to 20 seconds of continuous pressure, giving heat time to reach the adhesive layer and the fibre core
  • Worst possible location: the neck label sits centre-back and the care label on the side seam — exactly where pressing and steam setting pass
  • Hidden damage: many failures do not appear on the pressing day but after two or three washes, when the batch has long shipped
  • Parameters never travel: the label factory does not know the garment factory's pressing temperature, and the garment factory does not know the label's ceiling — both sides work from habit

2. Heat Reference by Trim Family

Trim Typical material Pressing reference Failure mode
Satin woven label Polyester warp and weft Keep below about 150°C and avoid direct pressing Edges stiffen, curl, surface glazes
Coated care label Polyester tape with resin-coated print Below roughly 140°C to 150°C; steam shortens contact time
Heat transfer label PU or PVC film with hot-melt adhesive Avoid both the post-press and any second pressing; PVC film is the most heat-sensitive
Hang tag with foil or UV Art, cotton or specialty paper with surface finishing Paper tolerates heat, but foil, UV and laminate take press marks and shiny spots
PVC, silicone or TPU badge Moulded soft PVC, silicone or TPU Silicone tolerates the most heat, PVC the least — they cannot share one pressing rule
Treat these numbers as a starting point: the real ceiling depends on film formulation, adhesive type and pressing pressure, and even a woven label can shift with a new yarn lot. The robust route is to obtain the factory's actual settings — temperature, pressure, dwell, steam — run a temperature-ladder sample against them, and only then fix the bulk specification.

3. Three Typical Failures and How to Handle Them

Failure one: stiffening, curling, glazed edges

Common on polyester satin labels. Polyester is thermoplastic: above its glass transition temperature and under pressure it takes a permanent set, which shows up as stiffness, lifted edges and a mirror gloss. Prevention and cure point the same way — lower the temperature, keep the iron off the label directly (use a cloth or press from the reverse), shorten the dwell, and if needed move to a heavier satin or a cotton woven label.

Failure two: colour migration, yellowing, shade shift

Pressing heat drives dye or ink colorants into neighbouring parts — a dark neck label leaves a faint mark on a white collar, a red care label leaves a ghost outline on pale fabric. Materials containing optical brighteners and phenolic antioxidants also yellow more readily under heat and moisture. Prevent it by running a migration test on dark labels (white cotton pressed against the label at the real temperature, then left 24 hours), avoiding stacking different colour families during pressing, and keeping pale-on-pale combinations.

Failure three: edge lift, blistering, delamination

This shows mainly on heat transfer labels, applied graphics and coated care labels. Hot-melt adhesive softens again at pressing temperature, so if the fabric is under tension or the steam pressure is high, the adhesive layer is pushed away from the edge; coated care labels can blister from the different thermal expansion of resin and substrate. The remedy: before bulk, run three home washes plus one pressing cycle against the real settings and confirm no edge lift or cracked print; at the garment factory, make transfers and applied graphics a no-press zone or finish from the reverse at low temperature.

4. Fix the Pressing Settings First, Then Choose the Trim

Most selection mistakes come from doing it in the wrong order: choose the label material, then ask the garment factory what temperature they press at. Reverse it — obtain the factory's real settings first (fibre content, pressing method, temperature range, pressure, dwell, steam, vacuum table) and choose material and process against them. The same care label can then be a coated version on a cotton T-shirt but a more heat-stable woven or printed label on a suit that needs high-temperature pressing.

5. How to Test: A Temperature Ladder

  • Prepare coupons: five or six sets from the bulk material, applied to the same fabric as the garment, fixed the same way as production — sewn or heat applied
  • Set the ladder: step down every 10°C from the declared temperature — 130, 140, 150, 160, 170°C, for example — at constant pressure and dwell
  • Watch four points: edge lift, surface gloss change, cracked or detached print, and bubbles at the bond line
  • Add a post-wash re-check: three washes following the care label, then repeat the same four observations — this is what filters out a build that looks fine on pressing day and fails after two washes
  • Keep the sample and records: archive the passing temperature step, material batch and date so a reorder reuses the same basis instead of repeating the trial

6. The Pressing Declaration to Send With Your Order

  • Garment fibre content and weight: cotton, poly-cotton, wool, with elastane
  • Pressing method: hand iron, press, steam setting, tunnel finishing
  • Temperature range, pressure, dwell per point, steam yes or no
  • Label positions and how they are handled during pressing: cloth, reverse, avoidance
  • Care instructions for washing and drying, used for the post-wash re-check
  • Whether dark labels were migration-tested and pale styles have a white reference cloth
