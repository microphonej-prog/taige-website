Spare Button Bags for Garments: Materials, Sizes and How They Fit Your Trims

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 27, 2026

A spare button bag — also called a spare-button pouch or replacement-button kit — is the small sachet packed with a garment: one or two matching buttons and a short length of thread, sometimes plus a fabric swatch and a care card. It never appears in a hang tag's main artwork, yet it is one of the first things a shopper touches after opening the packaging. Whether a lost button can be replaced at home, or a return is refused as incomplete, often depends on that little bag. For a brand it is also almost free label space: logo, size, fibre content and care instructions, seen the moment it is opened.

1. Why This Small Bag Deserves Real Attention

It solves three things at once: after-sales cost, brand touchpoint and packaging compliance. A few extra seconds sealing a sachet before shipment often avoids a full return and a one-star review. And with platform rules and childrenswear safety requirements getting more detailed, getting this small bag wrong costs more than looking cheap.

  • After-sales cost: a missing button is read as an incomplete product and, under cross-border e-commerce rules, can trigger a return or a bad review. One spare button in the bag lets most minor issues be solved in the customer's hands instead of a full round trip.
  • Brand touchpoint: hang tags are usually cut off and thrown away, while the button sachet often survives in a sewing kit or a drawer, so the buyer still recognises your logo on the next purchase. Small items outlive the main tag.
  • Packaging compliance: small plastic packaging and attachments on childrenswear have their own safety requirements. A bag without perforation or warning wording is both a hazard and grounds for rejecting a whole shipment.
  • Retail and dry cleaning: a spare set at the store settles a customer complaint on the spot, and dry cleaners routinely ask brands for replacement buttons they damaged during cleaning. Including a sachet takes load off the whole after-sales chain.

2. Four Common Formats: Materials, Specs and Where They Fit

There is no single standard for a spare button bag; the order should be content first, format second, printing last. The four formats below cover most orders, and the gap between fast fashion and premium is usually material thickness and print definition rather than cost.

Format Material and spec Best for Watch out for
Resealable bag (zip or self-adhesive) Transparent PE or CPE film, commonly around 50–80 microns, can be perforated and printed in one or two colours E-commerce, fast fashion, large volume Childrenswear needs perforation and warning text; below 50 microns bags tear easily in transit
Small kraft paper pouch 60–120 gsm kraft, glued or tuck flap, suited to one or two colour printing or blind embossing Mid-to-premium brands, eco-positioned ranges Weak moisture barrier: line the buttons with film or wax paper, and check how the flap survives repeated opening
Non-woven pouch 60–80 gsm non-woven, drawstring or glue closure, substantial feel, one-colour screen print Gift boxes, tailoring, premium gifting Higher cost and bulk; without a liner the buttons slide out, so add an inner bag
Paper card with window (or card plus clear bag) 250–350 gsm card, foil or emboss available, with a clear window or affixed bag Premium retail, gift box inserts, ranges that need display quality Hand assembly is required, so add labour and lead time; cost rises quickly with card size

3. Working Backwards from Contents to Size

The usual mistake is fixing a nice-looking size first and then filling it, which leaves an empty bag, buttons rattling in transit or a creased card. The right order is to list the contents, then add the clearance they need.

  • Buttons only: keep 4–6 mm clearance around the button diameter; typical finished sizes sit between 60×80 mm and 70×90 mm
  • With a card: add 3–5 mm per side; the smaller the card, the more important it is to check the minimum type size, or the copy turns into a smudge
  • Spare thread: 20–30 cm is enough; wound on a card it stays tidy and is less likely to tangle in transit. Pick the shade used most on that style
  • Fabric swatch: cut at least 2×2 cm and overlock or heat-seal the edges so fraying does not soil the bag; for dark fabrics add a layer of tissue to prevent rub-off
  • Match buttons to size: where button diameter varies by size, the spare must match the piece. Mixed sizes are among the most common and most overlooked after-sales complaints

4. Printing and Compliance: Small Area, Same Rules

Childrenswear: safety first, aesthetics second: small plastic packaging on children's garments sold in Europe and the USA generally needs perforation plus warning text stating the packaging is not a toy and should be kept from children. An error on a safety part cannot be sorted out by full inspection later — it has to be right at the sealing and bag-making stage. State the destination market and age group on the order so the factory lays it out to the right rules.
  • Keep copy aligned with the care label: fibre content, size and care symbols must not tell two stories, or customer service and platforms will default to the stricter one
  • Update languages together: when a new language is added, change hang tag, care label and button bag at the same time — changing one leaves a permanent inconsistency
  • Type size and contrast on a small area: keep body text at 6 pt or above, use a slightly heavier weight for reversed-out type, and remember that fine text smudges on film
  • Ink and substrate: for skin-contact garments and childrenswear prefer low-migration, low-odour inks, and write that requirement into the spec sheet
  • Add a material and recycling mark: stating PE or PP helps consumers sort waste, and EU requirements on packaging recyclability and material marking keep tightening, so being early saves a later reprint

5. Matching the Bag to Hang Tags, Woven Labels and Care Labels

The button bag is the most overlooked part of a trim package, yet it has to look like family with the hang tag, woven label and care label: one colour scheme, one typographic logic, one batch numbering convention. Customers do not compare line by line, but they spot incoherence at a glance.

  • Keep paper and colour consistent: a kraft tag with a kraft button bag, and because kraft board varies between batches, consider buying the year's requirement in one go
  • One bag per garment, allocated by ratio: shirts, suits and outerwear usually take two buttons, knitwear and small items one. Convert the size ratio into total quantities so you do not run short on large sizes and overstock small ones
  • Inside the bag or hung outside: most brands pack it inside (hard to lose, no effect on the garment's look). Premium lines hang it on the tag cord, but check the attachment strength and how it reads on display
  • Number for traceability: give the bag an internal code on the same convention as tags and care labels, so after-sales replacements and repeat orders line up and you never ship mismatched buttons from another batch
  • Work with the liner: place the sachet consistently between tissue paper and garment. Sachets loose in a carton corner are the ones most often missed and most likely to leave impressions

6. Six-Point Checklist Before Ordering

  • Finished size and material: length, width, thickness or grammage, clear or matte, perforated or not — in numbers
  • Contents and allocation: button type, colour and quantity; whether thread, card or swatch is included; and the split by size
  • Copy and languages: text, logo placement, minimum type size, language set — with an AI or PDF source file
  • Quantity and packing: bags per garment, total quantity, inner count such as 100 per bundle, and outer carton spec
  • Safety and compliance: destination market, age group, whether perforation and warning text are required, and whether small-part pull testing applies
  • MOQ, lead time and sampling: samples out in 3–7 days, bulk in 10–25 days, and both trial quantities and regular minimums are negotiable
