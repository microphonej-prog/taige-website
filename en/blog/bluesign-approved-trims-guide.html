A bluesign Logo Is Not the Same Thing as a Clean Woven Label

By TAGE Packaging · Updated November 28, 2026

More apparel brands now put a 'bluesign certified?' line in their supplier questionnaire, so trim mills have started printing the mark on sample cards. The catch is that bluesign issues three different levels of mark, referring to a chemical, a finished article, or a facility, and buyers usually just see a blue circle. This guide sets out what the bluesign SYSTEM actually manages, where a woven label and a ribbon genuinely touch chemistry, what each of the four common certificates proves, and which documents answer the question under EU, Japanese and Korean chemical law.

1. The bluesign SYSTEM Manages Inputs, Not Finished Looks

bluesign is built on input stream management: dyestuffs, auxiliaries, coatings and adhesives are screened before they enter the mill, against five criteria, resource productivity, consumer safety, water emission, air emission and occupational health and safety. It does not test the colourfastness of the finished woven label, and it is not a product quality mark. That is the most common misunderstanding. In practice the system issues three kinds of mark:

  • bluesign APPROVED applies to a single chemical, a dyestuff, auxiliary or coating, and is listed in the bluesign bluefinder database
  • bluesign PRODUCT means the article was made in a SYSTEM PARTNER facility using APPROVED chemistry, which is the only case where the article itself carries the mark
  • bluesign SYSTEM PARTNER is a facility-level status covering on-site management of water, air and worker safety, not a per-SKU pass

2. Three Places Where a Woven Label and a Ribbon Meet Chemistry

A 40 mm ribbon or woven label looks like yarn and lettering, but it passes through three chemistry-heavy steps. A buyer who only asks 'do you have bluesign?' has asked about the first one. To actually lower risk, split the three in the specification and ask for the matching chemical list, which is how we answer questionnaires.

  • Dyeing and sizing: dyestuffs and levelling agents for cone or skein dyeing, plus the sizing used before weaving, decide the AOX and COD load and, for childrenswear, the formaldehyde and aromatic amine risk
  • Softening and hand-feel finishing: silicone, softeners or hydrophilic auxiliaries migrate to the skin-contact face, which is where APEO and SVHC matter for next-to-skin and childrenswear
  • Coating and adhesion: ribbon back-coating, hot-melt film on woven labels and water-repellent finishes often carry PFAS or solvents, the most sensitive item in the REACH restriction list and the EU PFAS debate

3. What the Four Certificates Each Prove

System Proves Does not prove
bluesign APPROVED That chemical passed the five-criteria screen Whether the mill actually uses it, or in what dose
OEKO-TEX ECO PASSPORT Chemical-level conformity including MRSL and SVHC screening Migration or fastness of the finished label
ZDHC MRSL conformance The chemical inventory stays within limit values; Level 2 adds wastewater testing Recycled content, nor consumer safety of the article
GRS / RCS Recycled content and the transaction certificate chain Chemical risk in dyes, auxiliaries or coatings

Put that table into the questionnaire and most 'we are bluesign certified' answers collapse into one checkable sentence: which chemical, which certificate number, which facility. If the supplier can only send a logo image, the mark probably lives on a marketing page rather than in the purchasing file.

4. What Local Chemical Law Actually Requires You to Show

On the EU side the spine is REACH (EC) No 1907/2006: SVHC above 0.1 % triggers notification, the Annex XVII restriction list is revised every year, and the broad PFAS restriction proposal is still moving. In parallel, fibre composition on the care label is mandatory under Regulation 1007/2011, and chemical compliance never replaces that label.

France: the AGEC law phases in environmental disclosure, and the Code de la consommation prohibits misleading green claims, so a bluesign mark cannot stand in for specific data. Spain: Ley 7/2022 covers packaging and the plastic tax, while chemistry stays with REACH and Regulation (EU) 1007/2011 requires Spanish-language labelling with fibre composition. Japan: the Chemical Substances Control Law governs new substances, and the Household Goods Quality Labelling Act requires composition and handling information with care symbols per JIS L 0001; the label itself is not the declared article, but auxiliaries that migrate to skin fall under harmful-substance rules. Korea: K-REACH requires registration and tonnage reporting before import, the Electrical Appliances and Consumer Products Safety Control Act requires quality and handling marking, and KC certification covers only listed categories, so trims usually sit outside it, though brands still ask for the K-REACH registration number. China: GB 18401 sits alongside voluntary carbon disclosure under the dual-carbon policy, and new chemical substances need environmental management registration with the Ministry of Ecology and Environment.

5. The Four Documents an Audit Will Ask For

  • A chemical inventory with CAS numbers and use, mapped colour by colour; 'eco dyes' in four words is not an answer
  • On the certificate scan, check three things: number, expiry date, and the covered facility address, since a mismatch with the real production site is the most common defect
  • A wastewater report covering COD, AOX and heavy metals, sampled within the last 12 months, stating whether it is pre-treatment or at the discharge point
  • A line-by-line crosswalk against the buyer's MRSL; when a brand uses its own RSL, this is usually the slowest section of the whole questionnaire

6. Cost: the Certificate Fee Is Not the Big Number

The annual certification fee is fixed cost and lands at a few percent of the unit price on a low-value item like 40 mm ribbon. The expensive part is the chain reaction from switching chemistry: re-matching colour (a lab dip plus three or more bulk trials), re-testing fastness, client re-approval, and rescheduling. On a 200,000 m ribbon order in four colours, changing the whole dyestuff set usually consumes nearly all the gross margin on that line, which is why the practical route is to switch the high-risk items first (next-to-skin, childrenswear, water-repellent coatings) and keep the rest with test reports attached.

One line to remember: a certificate proves chemistry and process, not the piece of cloth. When a buyer asks whether the label is eco-friendly, the answer is a chemical inventory, a certificate number and a wastewater report, not a logo image.

7. The Order of Work, From Questionnaire to Bulk

  • Rank the SKUs by risk first: next-to-skin and childrenswear highest, then water-repellent coatings, then plain ribbons and labels
  • Then request the chemical inventory and certificates, and keep unsupported words off six-language packaging, for example 'PFAS-free' needs a third-party report behind it
  • Talk price last, and separate certificate upkeep from dye-switching cost so the buyer can see where the premium comes from
