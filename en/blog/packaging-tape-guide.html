Packing Tape Guide: BOPP, Acrylic, Kraft and Printed Tape

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 1, 2026

Packaging tape is the cheapest line on the packing list and the only component under continuous load for the whole journey. Whether a carton survives, and how clean it looks on opening, comes down to thickness, initial tack and whether the printed mark survives the friction.

This guide covers the four tapes in common use, how to read the numbers on a datasheet, what matters when printing a logo, and how tape has to match board, season and transport conditions.

Four tapes you will actually be quoted

Type Traits Best for
BOPP clear or printed Strong, cheap, printable; more than ninety percent of e-commerce volume
Water-based acrylic Low odour and quiet to unwind, good for branded gift boxes
Kraft paper tape Recyclable, writable and visually consistent; water-activated or high-tack versions
Filament tape Very high tensile strength for heavy goods, pallets and LCL bracing

Start from the board surface: laminated or recycled cartons carry a release layer or dust, and standard BOPP will not hold. Move to a high-tack or heavier gauge. This step matters far more than comparing price.

Reading the datasheet: thickness, initial tack, holding power

  • Thickness: 40-50 micrometres is the usual band, with 45 the e-commerce default. Every 5 micrometres removed visibly cuts tensile and puncture strength, and the saving comes back doubled in damage.
  • Initial tack: the grip at the moment of contact. It decides whether an automatic case sealer lays down first time, and whether the tape lifts in the cold.
  • Holding power: whether the bond creeps under sustained load. In humid sea freight and stacked pallets, holding power matters more than initial tack.
  • Peel adhesion and tensile strength: specify both for heavy, long-haul or multi-leg shipments, instead of shopping on price per roll.

Printed tape: three numbers decide whether the logo reads

  • Type height: below 8 mm the mark smears at a distance; keep the logo between 10 and 15 mm tall and reduce small type to the brand name and URL.
  • Repeat length: set it from the carton width you use most, otherwise every box cuts a logo in half and looks like a reject.
  • Contrast: kraft board is a yellow-brown, so white and light grey read best. On dark boxes invert to white, but choose an abrasion-resistant ink.
Water-based ink is cheap and scratches off, and after a long domestic trunk route usually half the logo is gone. Where budget allows, choose solvent-based or UV-cured ink, or move the logo to a side panel the tape does not cover.

Season and climate: lifted edges are rarely the tape's fault

In the rainy season and inside a sea container, humidity can pass 80 percent; once the board takes up moisture its surface strength drops and even good tape pulls the paper layer away. Winter in northern warehouses and cold stores is worse: below 5 degrees Celsius standard adhesive loses tack fast, looks stuck, then lifts two hours later.

The fix is simple: go one gauge heavier, move from a single centre seam to an H pattern (top and bottom centre seam plus one run each side), and check board moisture before packing. The H pattern uses about thirty percent more tape and usually cuts burst cartons more than a heavier gauge does.

Troubleshooting the usual complaints

  • Lifted edges: rule out surface dust, low temperature and thin adhesive coating in turn; it is rarely the same cause twice.
  • Stringing or snapping: usually low cohesive strength in the adhesive or a cold room. Switching to a higher holding-power grade solves it; the whole lot need not be rejected.
  • Adhesive transfer: glue left on the board after opening points to cheap hot melt. For gift boxes use water-based acrylic so the customer's hands stay clean.
  • Misregistered print: the repeat length does not match the carton width, or the sealer's knife is out of position. Fix the plate for the first, the machine for the second; they are different faults.
