Client-Supplied and Brand-Specified Trim Materials Guide

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 16, 2026

One type of garment trim order causes more disputes than any other: client-supplied and brand-specified materials. The client provides the woven labels, buttons or fabric, or insists on a particular brand of hook-and-loop tape or zip — and suddenly the responsibility boundary is blurred. Was the fault in the incoming material or in the processing? Who carries the loss rate? Does surplus material go back? If the contract is silent, production grinds to a halt. This guide turns client-supplied material management into practical steps.

It runs in four parts: distinguishing the three scenarios, then incoming inspection, loss and replenishment, segregation and traceability with settlement terms, and finally sampling and change control.

1. Separate the Three Scenarios First — Responsibility Differs Completely

ScenarioWhat the client providesRisk and key point
Client-supplied goodsFinished trims, fabric, paperFaulty incoming material is the client's responsibility, but you must inspect and record it on arrival; material lost beyond the agreed rate has to be replaced
Brand-specified materialBrand, model or nominated supplierSingle source, so lead time and price sit with someone else; write the equivalent option and the approval route into the order
Client-supplied tooling and artworkDies, foil plates, artworkNumber, store, maintain and return them; state which artwork revision governs and who pays for a revision

The three often mix in a single order: the client supplies the woven labels, specifies a zip brand and posts a die. Separating them on paper before quoting saves far more trouble than explaining later.

2. Incoming Inspection: Six Things Done on the Spot

  • Check the quantity: count by piece, roll and kilo, and note metres or yards on the rolls; if a count cannot be finished, sign the quantity as pending rather than signing it off and sorting it out later.
  • Appearance and damage: check roll ends, damp cartons, missing labels and mixed batches, and photograph anything unusual with a note on the same day.
  • Check the spec line by line: grammage, thickness, width, colour and model against the spec sheet — never waved through on the strength of last time.
  • Batch and colour variation: record the incoming batch number; when colour differs visibly between batches of the same material, compare on a small sample before committing to bulk.
  • Compatibility sample: confirm the match against the main fabric or component first — colour, stiffness, shrinkage and adhesion — rather than discovering it on the finished article.
  • Record and sign: one inspection sheet with quantity, batch, defect description and photos, signed by both sides; segregate the rejects and tell the client the same day rather than at scheduling time.

3. Loss Rates and Replenishment: Settle Them at Quotation Stage

  • Loss depends on the process: printing, die-cutting, foil stamping, weaving and stitching each have their own reasonable loss; stating the included allowance on the quotation is far cheaper than arguing after bulk.
  • Insufficient material stops the line: agree in advance on who carries the cost and how rescheduling works when client material runs short — as a rule the slot restarts from the day the replacement arrives.
  • Separate defect loss from set-up loss: waste caused by faulty incoming material is not the same as waste from machine set-up, colour matching and registration — the former counts against the client's material, the latter is normally the processor's.
  • Advise on the quantity to send: tell the client to add a safety margin on top of the reasonable loss, especially for ranges that will be reordered after the line is set — sending enough once saves a shipment and a stoppage.

4. Segregated Storage and Batch Traceability

  • Keep it in one place: store client material in its own area labelled with client name, incoming batch and date — mixing it with your own similar stock is the fastest way to lose track of where a fault came from.
  • First in, first out: client material often spans several production batches, so use the earliest arrival first, before surplus ages into discolouration or stiffness.
  • Trace both ways: a finished batch should point to the incoming material batch, and the material batch to every finished batch it went into — the only evidence both sides accept when something goes wrong.

5. Settlement and Terms: Cheaper in the Contract Than in a Dispute

  • Keep the free-issue material out of the invoice: client material must not be billed as part of the processing fee, and customs and accounting need it shown separately so the two never fail to reconcile.
  • Return of surplus and rejects: agree quantity, packing, who pays the freight and the deadline — usually a set number of days after the batch is completed — after which the processor may dispose of it.
  • IP and confidentiality: the client warrants the rights in supplied artwork, brand and design; the processor keeps drawings and process data confidential — both belong in the contract.

6. Sampling and Change Control: Never Skip Them with Free-Issue Material

  • Trial on a small sample first: before running client material in production, prove adhesion of print or foil, die-cutting behaviour and stitching on a small sample.
  • Re-confirm after a supplier or batch change: a different source of client material can shift colour, thickness and shrinkage, so the earlier signed sample no longer applies.
  • Keep samples with batch numbers: retain a physical sample of the client material with batch and date, plus the signed inspection sheet — on paper these beat any verbal explanation.
💡 Practical tip: free-issue disputes almost always fail on the same three things — nothing inspected, nothing recorded, no loss rate agreed. One incoming inspection sheet plus a loss and replenishment clause on the quotation costs next to nothing and keeps nine out of ten arguments outside the door.
