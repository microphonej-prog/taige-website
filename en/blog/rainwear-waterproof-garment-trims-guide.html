Waterproof and Rainwear Garment Trims: Care Labels, Neck Labels and Hang Tags

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 1, 2026

Trims for rainwear and waterproof garments — raincoats, shell jackets, ski wear, waterproof workwear, waterproof outerwear — do not follow the same logic as ordinary woven garments. The fabric carries a coating or a laminated membrane, so whether a label takes up water, whether stitching leaves needle holes, and whether a seam tape has been pierced all affect waterproof performance and comfort. This guide covers material selection for care labels and neck labels, how to keep stitching and seam sealing out of trouble, how to word the wash and care symbols, how to make a functional claim on a hang tag defensible, how to keep packaging dry, and the six items to state in an enquiry.

1. Three Ways Waterproof Fabric Complicates Trims

  • Absorption and drying: ordinary woven tape and cotton labels take up water, dry slowly against a laminated fabric and feel cold on the back when worn. Worse, a saturated label holds moisture locally and can leave tide marks and mildew after a sea crossing.
  • A needle hole is a hole. Stitch a label onto a shoulder or sleeve seam that has already been taped and the needle pierces the tape; if the thread itself wicks, water travels along the thread into the garment.
  • Seam sealing and heat: coatings and laminated membranes dislike high temperature and repeated abrasion. A stiff, thick label edge sitting on a fold will push out a pale crease within a few wears, and the seam tape lifts with it.

3. Stitching, Seam Sealing and Placement

  • Mark the sealed zones first, then choose the label position. Keep labels outside the tape — mid side seam or below the back neck in an untaped area — or make them hang free, and pin the position and tolerance down in the spec sheet.
  • Avoid needles where you can. Heat-transfer care labels, ultrasonic welding and local bonding eliminate needle holes entirely; sew a coated tape label only where long-term wash durability and hand feel demand it.
  • Thread matters too. Use polyester thread matched to the shell or a low-absorption thread rather than cotton; where sealing follows, the tape must fully cover both sides of the stitch line and no label should sit on top of the tape.
  • Finish the edges: hot or ultrasonic cutting is cleaner than a plain cut and reduces fraying and wicking. Keep thickness within what the shell can carry, and favour thinner stock in next-to-skin positions.

4. Wording the Wash and Care Symbols

  • Base the advice on the actual shell and coating, not on a template. Coated and laminated styles usually cannot take high-heat tumble drying or dry cleaning, bleach attacks the coating, and fabric softener blocks the breathable pores — say so in a line of text alongside the symbols.
  • Write maintenance advice, not just prohibitions. Lines such as wash with a technical detergent and tumble on low to restore water repellency, or reapply a durable water repellent periodically, cut complaints about fading performance considerably.
  • For multilingual markets, follow the language rules of the country of sale. Many EU states require the local official language, Canada requires English and French, and the symbol order and text should be proofed once rather than patched later with stickers.

5. Making a Functional Claim Defensible

  • Give numbers, not adjectives. Hydrostatic head, moisture vapour transmission and water repellency can be stated as method plus result — for example hydrostatic head 5000 mm and above, tested to ISO 811 — which is far more credible than super waterproof and much harder to challenge.
  • Keep every claim tied to a report. If a figure is printed, you should be able to produce a report for the same material or batch; without one, fall back to describing the construction — taped seams, coating, water repellent finish — and leave numbers out.
  • Do not place claims where they mislead. Keep performance statements away from the care symbols and print return instructions on a separate piece of card, so a customer does not read water repellent as fully waterproof — a frequent source of after-sales dispute.

6. Packaging and Shipping: Dry Beats Pretty

  • A waterproof garment carries a high unit value, so packaging has to survive the heat and humidity inside a container. The usual recipe is an outer PE or CPE zip bag with a header card, desiccant dosed to the carton volume, and a moisture barrier liner inside the carton.
  • Avoid packaging details that scuff the coating: keep stiff tags off the coated face, wrap metal fasteners and hard-edged fittings separately, and specify the folding method for rolled or folded packing, because a crushed crease is the hardest place to restore water repellency.

7. Six Items to Send With an Enquiry

  • Category and use: raincoat, shell jacket, ski wear or workwear, and whether it is worn outdoors long term — this sets the durability grade.
  • Shell construction: coating, laminated membrane or membrane-free water repellent fabric, plus whether seams are taped and the tape width and position.
  • Label plan: which carrier for the care label, where the neck label goes, and whether a needle-free method such as transfer or bonding is required.
  • Tags and claims: which performance figures to print, whether test reports exist, and whether a return card and brand story card go with them.
  • Packaging and logistics: unit packing format, pieces per carton, mode of transport by sea or air, and whether extra moisture protection is needed.
  • Target market and compliance: language requirements of the country of sale, restricted substance list, and whether test reports must travel with the goods.
💡 Tip: a large share of after-sales complaints on waterproof styles are not about the shell leaking — they are about water entering at the label, a pale crease pushed out by a label edge, or slow drying. All three show up at sampling: have the supplier sew a label onto a fully taped sample garment and run a spray test and a hand wash. That beats any specification sheet.
