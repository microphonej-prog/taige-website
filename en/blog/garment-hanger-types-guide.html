Garment Hanger Guide: Materials, Types, Logos and Hanging Shipments

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 9, 2026

A hanger is the last accessory a customer sees before the garment leaves the warehouse, and the easiest line on the brand's cost sheet to lose control of: the wrong material bulges and distorts the shoulder, too little load capacity snaps a coat hanger in two days, and a cheap logo process has already faded by the time it reaches the shop floor. More importantly, the hanger decides whether the shipment goes flat-packed or on hangers — a single decision that changes freight, re-finishing labour and how fast the store can put the product on display.

1. What a Hanger Actually Does in the Supply Chain

A hanger has to do four things at once: hold the garment on the correct shoulder line, carry the whole weight, carry the brand mark into the store, and — when shipping on hangers — keep the neckline and shoulder line undistorted. Those four jobs carry very different weight by category: a suit needs a wooden or wide-shoulder ABS hanger to support the shoulder, a knit needs flocked or soft-coated non-slip to stop it sliding and stretching, and underwear in an e-commerce parcel only needs a thin non-slip hanger that saves space. Buy hangers as a generic accessory and you usually end up reworking shoulder shape and breakage rates.

2. Five Common Hanger Materials Compared

Material Feel and positioning Cost and MOQ Best for
PP / PS plastic Entry level and versatile, glossy or matt Lowest, standard moulds, flexible MOQ E-commerce, wholesale, domestic lines
ABS plastic Good gloss and strength, clear and bright colours Medium, usually needs a mould Mid-market brands, retail display
Wood (beech / rubberwood / pine) Highest perceived value, grain and finish set the grade Highest, driven by timber and finishing Premium brands, suits and coats
Flocked Non-slip, thin and space-saving, soft to touch Medium to high Lingerie, knits, loungewear, boutiques
Metal (aluminium / stainless) Slim and rigid, plating and anodised colour possible Medium to high, moves with metal prices Formalwear, hotels, concept stores

Beyond material, two parameters are easily overlooked: hook type and shoulder construction. A swivel hook lets the store turn the garment quickly, a fixed hook resists pulling better; a shoulder-contoured hanger supports the shape of a suit or coat, while a flat shoulder suits knits and T-shirts. When buying for a mixed range, keep the store display consistent — one hanger across a line is worth more visually than a bigger budget spent.

3. Matching Hanger Type to Garment

  • Top hangers: the shoulder width should match the garment's shoulder width — too narrow pushes a peak into the shoulder line, too wide and it slides off.
  • Trouser and skirt hangers: clamping force must hold without leaving teeth marks — use soft or coated jaws — and hanging trousers at the waistband keeps the shape better than hanging them by the hem.
  • Suit hangers: contoured shoulders with a trouser bar; heavier fabrics need a wider shoulder and thicker body to avoid shoulder collapse in long storage.
  • Children's and baby hangers: moulded to childrenswear shoulder widths, with no sharp corners or burrs, and small parts meeting the destination pull requirements.
  • Lingerie and bra hangers: non-slip and width-matched so straps are not stretched out of shape; go thin where display density is high.

4. Surface Finish, LOGO and Odour Control

There are four common ways to put a brand mark on a hanger: silk-screen or pad printing is cheapest and suits large solid areas; hot foil and heat transfer look better but demand adhesion on a curved surface; laser engraving never fades and suits wood and ABS; soft TPU or silicone badges give depth and often match the brand label visually. At acceptance, focus on abrasion and colour loss — a non-woven rub and a tape peel test are more reliable than a visual check. Odour matters too: plastic hangers with a high recycled content can smell pungent inside a sealed container, so export orders should state the virgin-material ratio and any environmental requirements (such as phthalate limits), and the contract should define how odour is judged and what happens if it fails.

5. Shipping on Hangers: Hangers, Hanger Bags and Hanging Cartons

Flat packing saves freight but the store has to press and re-merchandise on arrival; hanging costs more to ship but lets the store hang the goods straight out of the box and cuts re-finishing. Three factors decide it: category (suits, coats and eveningwear almost always hang), channel (own stores and department stores usually want hanging, platform warehouses and e-commerce usually want flat), and container volume utilisation. For hanging, hanger, hanger bag and hanging carton form one system: the bag length must cover the garment and leave the hook hole in the right place, and the bar spacing inside the carton must match the hanger thickness or the garments crush and crease in transit. Many hanging orders also expect hangers to be recovered in store or returned with the next shipment, so settle the hanger recovery plan when you quote.

6. Acceptance and Quote Request Checklist

  • Load: static and drop tests at the heaviest garment weight, recording where it breaks and the breakage rate.
  • Appearance: no burrs, no flash, no sink marks, colour matched to the approved colour card.
  • Marking: abrasion and colour-loss tests (non-woven rub plus tape peel); laser engraving must be consistent in depth.
  • Odour: no smell after 24 hours sealed in the box; export orders should state the virgin-material ratio and plasticiser limits.
  • Packaging and hanging: bag type and length, hook-hole position, bar spacing in the hanging carton and pieces per carton.
