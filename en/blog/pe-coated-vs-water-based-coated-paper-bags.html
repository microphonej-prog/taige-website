A Paper Bag's Recyclability Hangs on a Coating Thinner Than a Hair

By TAGE Packaging · Updated November 26, 2026

Two carrier bags can be made from the same 90 g/m² coated board and still end up in completely different places: one goes into a paper mill's pulper, the other into an incinerator. The difference is not the paper but the 8 to 25 grams of coating between them. A paper bag needs moisture resistance, grease resistance and enough strength to carry a purchase, which means something has to be laminated onto it. Whether that something is a PE extrusion layer or a water-based dispersion coating decides whether the bag is feedstock or waste at the recycling end. We make custom paper bags and paper-based packaging in Dongguan, and this guide sets out the physical difference between the two processes, how recyclability is actually judged, the trade-offs on cost and sea freight, and the wording that will not come back as a complaint.

1. Two Ways to Make Paper Hold Back Water and Grease

The first route is extrusion lamination: molten polyethylene is pressed into a film at around 300 °C and laid directly onto the paper, cooling into a continuous plastic layer, usually at 10 to 30 g/m². That is a physical barrier, and it gives the best moisture resistance and heat-sealing behaviour, which is why handles and bag bottoms are usually built this way. The second route is aqueous dispersion coating: an acrylic or styrene-acrylic dispersion is applied to the paper and dried down to a 3 to 8 g/m² dry coat, where the particles coalesce into a continuous film under a coalescing agent. There is no plastic film, and the fibre stays exposed beneath the coating.

2. Why PE Takes the Bag Out of the Fibre Stream

A paper mill's pulper is built for fibre: add water, agitate, screen, deink. Polyethylene does not disperse in water; it breaks into flakes that hang on screens and pipework, which is why PE-laminated paper is usually not recoverable in a standard mill and goes either to a specialist plant with a film-removal line or down to energy recovery. The industry works with a common threshold for how much non-fibre content still counts as recyclable: below 5% non-fibre is treated as compatible with standard recycling, 5% to 10% needs case-by-case assessment, and above 10% essentially needs a different outlet. That number is the key to coating weight. A 90 g/m² paper with 20 g/m² of PE is roughly 18% non-fibre, well outside the threshold.

Buyers ask whether a bag is recyclable. The question that produces an answer is how much non-fibre content it carries, whether a de-inking or film-removal line exists, and where the repulping test report is. The first gets a verbal assurance; the second goes into the tech pack.

3. What a Dispersion Coating Is, and What It Is Not

Three chemistries dominate: acrylic, which gives the best grease holdout and film formation; styrene-acrylic, which costs less and resists water better; and polyurethane dispersions, which flex well and suit a bag mouth that opens and closes repeatedly. Their shared advantage is low add-on: a 3 to 8 g/m² dry coat keeps non-fibre content usually below 4%, and the coating disperses with the fibre in the pulper. The limits are just as clear.

  • Moisture resistance is below PE. A dispersion coating handles grease and short contact with water droplets, but not sustained high humidity, and 60 days inside a sea container is sustained. The fibre still takes up moisture, which is where South-East Asian and Middle Eastern orders most often run into trouble.
  • Heat sealing is weaker. A dispersion coating can be heat sealed, but the seal strength is below PE, so a heavy-duty carrier bag usually still needs PE or a paper-twist handle at the joint.
  • Grease holdout depends on additives. The higher the kit rating, the more complex the formulation, and it has to be a PFAS-free system: the EU already restricts C9-C14 perfluorocarboxylic acids under REACH Annex XVII, and fluorinated grease barriers are being phased out of food and textile packaging item by item. Ask for the report, not the assurance.

4. The Three Tests That Settle It

A supplier saying a bag is recyclable is an opinion; a test report is a fact. These three cover different questions, and missing any one leaves a blind spot. The last row is not a test at all but the assumption most often used in place of one.

  • Repulping and disintegration (ISO 5263-1): the sample is broken down in water and you watch whether the coating disperses with the fibre or leaves flakes. This is the best predictor of what a standard mill will actually do.
  • Grease resistance (TAPPI T 559, the kit test): drops of oil at graded surface tensions are timed to give a rating from 1 to 12, which decides whether the bag can touch oily goods directly.
  • Recyclability standard (ISO 18604): it defines the terms and requirements for recovery by material recycling, which is how you check that buyer and supplier mean the same thing by recyclable.
  • The common misreading: EN 13432 is a composting standard, not a recycling one. It requires 90% disintegration within 12 weeks and 90% biodegradation within six months, which is a completely different route from paper recycling and cannot be used to prove a bag recyclable.

5. Where Each One Wins on Cost and on Freight

Price is not just cost per tonne of coating. A dispersion coating usually costs more per kilogram than PE resin, but the add-on is a third to a fifth of the PE weight, so the difference per bag often lands in single-digit percentages. Three things move it more. The first is minimum order quantity: dispersion coating needs a dedicated coating line and drying curve, and setup and sampling cost that cannot be amortised over a small run pushes unit price up directly. The second is printing: dispersion surfaces are more sensitive to ink adhesion, and a heavy solid print often needs a primer. The third is weight, because a lower non-fibre share means a lighter shipment at the same piece count, which saves money whether freight is charged by cubic metre or by kilogram, and the EU recovery fee is charged by weight and material as well.

Coating type Typical add-on In the paper stream? Where it wins
PE extrusion lamination 10-30 g/m² usually not, needs a film-removal line moisture barrier, heat sealing, heavy loads
Acrylic dispersion 3-8 g/m² dry yes, non-fibre share usually under 4% grease holdout, repulpable, light
Polyurethane dispersion 4-10 g/m² dry yes, at a higher cost flex resistance for a bag mouth that opens repeatedly

6. Wording That Survives a Compliance Check

100% recyclable paper bag is the sentence most often sent back in this trade, and not because the bag cannot do it. The claim simply carries no conditions: in which country's collection system, with which coating, at what non-fibre content. Markets also police green wording differently. France relies on the Code de la consommation and the AGEC law, and expects the Triman mark with the info-tri sorting notice; Spain applies Real Decreto 1055/2022 together with Ley 7/2022; Japan treats an exaggerated environmental claim as a misleading representation under its Act against Unjustifiable Premiums and Misleading Representations; Korea handles it through the Act on Fair Labelling and Advertising; China maps to the GB/T 16716 series and the labelling requirements of GB/T 5296.4. Where the bag also carries an origin, fibre-composition or quality marking, the labelling duty of the destination country lands on top of the packaging rule. Rather than one absolute sentence, write three specific things.

  • State the conditions: for example, 96% paper base with a 4% aqueous coating, repulpable in a standard mill per ISO 5263-1, sorted under the French Triman scheme.
  • Narrow the scope to compatible with the local collection system rather than recyclable anywhere.
  • Keep compostable and recyclable apart. Do not put EN 13432 and a recyclable mark on the same bag, because they describe two different routes.
