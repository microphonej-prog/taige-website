Double-Sided Hang Tags: Splitting Content, Registration and Paper Choice

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 20, 2026

A double-sided hang tag carries printed content on both the front and the back: the front handles branding and visual impact, while the back handles the information that must travel with the garment — fibre content, care, barcode, origin and size. It solves two real problems: nowhere left to put the information, and a blank back that makes an otherwise good tag look cheap.

One point first: double-sided printing is not simply running the single-sided artwork twice. A second pass means the sheet goes through printing, drying and registration again, so ink build-up, paper movement and the front-to-back position all shift. If the specification is vague, you will spend the sampling stage going back and forth.

1. When Double-Sided Printing Is Worth It

  • Compliance information has nowhere to go: fibre content, care symbols, origin and importer details already fill the care label — moving part of it to the tag back gives breathing room
  • The brand wants to tell a story: a short brand note, material story, care advice or multilingual copy will never fit on one side
  • The tag has to carry a code: a retail barcode, traceability QR code or membership entry on the back keeps the front clean
  • The tag does double duty: warranty card, care card, size chart or gift-note card — the back is there to be written on

2. Splitting Content Between Front and Back

A useful rule: keep subtracting on the front, keep adding on the back. Fewer elements on the front read as stronger, and everything that must travel with the product belongs on the back.

SideWhat to put thereWatch out for
FrontBrand logo, collection name, key visual, the finishing that matters (foil, embossing)Fewer elements, more white space — that is what reads as quality
BackFibre content and care, origin, size, barcode or QR, service and after-sales detailsBody text 5 pt or larger; avoid hairline and light-grey text on dark blocks
Both sidesString-hole position, die-cut outline and a consistent brand colour across bothDraw the hole into the artwork from the start — do not leave the printer to guess

3. Registration Tolerance: How Much Misalignment Is Too Much

Registration is the relative position of the artwork on the two sides. Standard hang tags are fine at an image-position tolerance of ±1.0 mm. If the design demands strict front-to-back symmetry — a colour band running through both sides, a hole punched through both, or mirrored elements — ask for ±0.5 mm and hold one backlit check at the sampling stage. Pure text does not need tight registration, as long as it clears the hole and stays inside the die-cut line.

4. Paper and Show-Through: Where Double-Sided Tags Fail

Show-through is artwork bleeding visually to the other side. A solid dark block on one side with pale text on the other is the single most complained-about combination on double-sided tags.

  • Grammage: 300–400 g coated or art card is the norm; below 250 g, keep solid dark blocks off the reverse
  • Opacity: double-coated card is more opaque; recycled and kraft papers are naturally translucent and suit lighter artwork
  • Design fix: pair a dark side with dark text, or leave a white panel on the reverse and set the text inside it
  • Production fix: print a white base layer under the ink when needed — slightly more cost, far less show-through

5. Lamination: Does Laminating Both Sides Feel Slippery

  • Gloss both sides: slick and highly reflective, sheets slide when stacked — fine for retail displays that want maximum shine
  • Matte both sides: the safer choice — scratch-resistant, fingerprint-friendly, and forgiving on dark artwork
  • Finishing one side, varnish the other: when the front carries foil, embossing or spot UV, keep the back to an overall varnish — cheaper and no interference
  • If the back must be written on, skip the lamination: price marking, handwritten sizes and gift notes will not take on a laminated surface

6. Cost and Lead Time: Where the Extra Comes From

  • Double the press work: a second pass adds set-up and running time, and the smaller the run, the more visible that is in the unit price
  • Tighter registration means more waste: on strict-alignment jobs, agree the spoilage allowance when you quote
  • Laminating or finishing both sides adds material and labour at once — usually the main driver of the extra cost
  • Lead time: sampling at 3–7 days and bulk at 10–25 days still holds, but allow 2–3 extra days when double-sided printing stacks with several finishing steps
