Double-Sided Hang Tags: Splitting Content, Registration and Paper Choice

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 20, 2026

A double-sided hang tag carries printed content on both the front and the back: the front handles branding and visual impact, while the back handles the information that must travel with the garment — fibre content, care, barcode, origin and size. It solves two real problems: nowhere left to put the information, and a blank back that makes an otherwise good tag look cheap.

One point first: double-sided printing is not simply running the single-sided artwork twice. A second pass means the sheet goes through printing, drying and registration again, so ink build-up, paper movement and the front-to-back position all shift. If the specification is vague, you will spend the sampling stage going back and forth.

1. When Double-Sided Printing Is Worth It

  • Compliance information has nowhere to go: fibre content, care symbols, origin and importer details already fill the care label — moving part of it to the tag back gives breathing room
  • The brand wants to tell a story: a short brand note, material story, care advice or multilingual copy will never fit on one side
  • The tag has to carry a code: a retail barcode, traceability QR code or membership entry on the back keeps the front clean
  • The tag does double duty: warranty card, care card, size chart or gift-note card — the back is there to be written on

2. Splitting Content Between Front and Back

A useful rule: keep subtracting on the front, keep adding on the back. Fewer elements on the front read as stronger, and everything that must travel with the product belongs on the back.

SideWhat to put thereWatch out for
FrontBrand logo, collection name, key visual, the finishing that matters (foil, embossing)Fewer elements, more white space — that is what reads as quality
BackFibre content and care, origin, size, barcode or QR, service and after-sales detailsBody text 5 pt or larger; avoid hairline and light-grey text on dark blocks
Both sidesString-hole position, die-cut outline and a consistent brand colour across bothDraw the hole into the artwork from the start — do not leave the printer to guess

3. Registration Tolerance: How Much Misalignment Is Too Much

Registration is the relative position of the artwork on the two sides. Standard hang tags are fine at an image-position tolerance of ±1.0 mm. If the design demands strict front-to-back symmetry — a colour band running through both sides, a hole punched through both, or mirrored elements — ask for ±0.5 mm and hold one backlit check at the sampling stage. Pure text does not need tight registration, as long as it clears the hole and stays inside the die-cut line.

4. Paper and Show-Through: Where Double-Sided Tags Fail

Show-through is artwork bleeding visually to the other side. A solid dark block on one side with pale text on the other is the single most complained-about combination on double-sided tags.

  • Grammage: 300–400 g coated or art card is the norm; below 250 g, keep solid dark blocks off the reverse
  • Opacity: double-coated card is more opaque; recycled and kraft papers are naturally translucent and suit lighter artwork
  • Design fix: pair a dark side with dark text, or leave a white panel on the reverse and set the text inside it
  • Production fix: print a white base layer under the ink when needed — slightly more cost, far less show-through

5. Lamination: Does Laminating Both Sides Feel Slippery

  • Gloss both sides: slick and highly reflective, sheets slide when stacked — fine for retail displays that want maximum shine
  • Matte both sides: the safer choice — scratch-resistant, fingerprint-friendly, and forgiving on dark artwork
  • Finishing one side, varnish the other: when the front carries foil, embossing or spot UV, keep the back to an overall varnish — cheaper and no interference
  • If the back must be written on, skip the lamination: price marking, handwritten sizes and gift notes will not take on a laminated surface

6. Cost and Lead Time: Where the Extra Comes From

  • Double the press work: a second pass adds set-up and running time, and the smaller the run, the more visible that is in the unit price
  • Tighter registration means more waste: on strict-alignment jobs, agree the spoilage allowance when you quote
  • Laminating or finishing both sides adds material and labour at once — usually the main driver of the extra cost
  • Lead time: sampling at 3–7 days and bulk at 10–25 days still holds, but allow 2–3 extra days when double-sided printing stacks with several finishing steps

8. Grammage and Show-Through: Combinations That Work

The first thing to fail on a double-sided tag is not registration but show-through: a large dark panel on the front greys out the copy behind it, or a flooded back makes the shadows of the front image go muddy. The test is simple — hold the finished tag against a light and see whether the back content reads as a shape from the front. How the Second Side Gets Printed: One Pass or Two

There are two ways to print the back, with different cost and risk. Perfecting prints both sides in one pass: registration is the most stable and the schedule is shortest, but the sheet takes ink on both sides at once and carries more moisture, so thin stock stretches and dark floods on the back can set off. Two passes mean printing the front, letting it dry and running the sheet again: each side dries separately, ink is more stable and heavy coverage or multi-colour builds are safer, at the cost of a second set-up charge and 1–2 days, with registration held by gripper and guides rather than a single pass, so the tolerance is slightly wider.

Two passes pay off when the front carries a large spot colour or foil, when the back has small barcodes and fine text, or when the stock is under 250 gsm. Conversely, with light backgrounds on both sides, simple content and runs under 5,000, one pass is the better buy. When you request a quote, state whether "double-sided" means one pass or two — the difference can reach 15–25%, and it is one of the usual reasons two quotes for the same spec do not line up.

9. Barcodes and QR Codes on the Back: Position, Quiet Zone, Variable Data

Cross-border e-commerce and retail channels almost always want a barcode on the tag, and the barcode is the element most often scrapped by layout alone: printed perfectly, it still will not scan if the quiet zone is trimmed away or the symbol sits over the punched hole. EAN-13 at 100% magnification is 37.29 × 25.93 mm and needs at least 3.5 mm of quiet zone on each side; bar height should not fall below 13 mm, and the symbol should stay 3 mm or more from the trim edge and the hole. For QR codes use at least 12 × 12 mm with a 2 mm quiet zone, and avoid reversed codes (light modules on a dark ground) — phones under shop lighting often fail on them.

Care and Composition on the Back: A Hang Tag Is Not a Durable Label

The back of a double-sided tag is often used as a stand-in for the care label, and there is a boundary here: a hang tag is not a durable label. EU Regulation 1007/2011 requires textile fibre composition to sit on a durable label that stays with the product, and care symbols follow EN ISO 3758; the tag may carry the same information for selling purposes but cannot replace the sewn-in label. In practice, print composition, origin and care symbols on the back for retail display, and still sew a durable label into the side seam or back neck. For Japan, note the Household Goods Quality Labelling Act requirements on composition and the labelling responsible party; Korea requires fibre blend percentages and manufacturer information.

10. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the minimum order quantity for double-sided tags?

Double-sided printing does not raise the MOQ by itself, but it changes how cost is spread. Plain two-sided work usually starts at 1,000–3,000 tags; adding foil, embossing or a custom die pushes it to 3,000–5,000, because each of those steps carries a plate and set-up charge that dominates the unit price on short runs.

What does sampling cost — and is it credited against the bulk order?

Digital samples are priced per piece and cheap; physical samples with foil, embossing or a custom die are charged by process. The usual practice is to credit the sample fee against the bulk order, but it must be stated on the quotation — if it is not written, it does not exist. Sampling takes 3–7 days, plus 2–3 days when double-sided printing is combined with several finishes.

How much misregistration is a reject?

Registration is normally held to ±0.3–0.5 mm. Where a shape or colour band must continue across the tag — a front band running onto the back, for example — ±0.2 mm is achievable, but it slows the press and raises waste, so the price moves with it. Measure from the same reference edge, inspect at AQL 2.5, and state the reference edge in the spec sheet: without it, two people can measure the same tag and get different numbers.

Does laminating both sides make the tag slippery?

Both faces can be laminated, but gloss on both gets slippery — tagging guns and automatic packers start to slide and double-feed. Prefer gloss front and matte back, or matte on both; if the back carries barcodes and care information, matte is the practical choice because reflections raise the failure rate for scanners and phones. Lamination also softens the perceived stiffness slightly, so if a crisp hand is the goal, skip the film and use calendering instead.

What is the lead time — can a rush order jump the queue?

Sampling takes 3–7 days and bulk production 10–25 days, plus 2–3 days when several finishes stack on top of double-sided printing — that is the industry norm. Whether a rush order can jump the queue depends on press scheduling and whether the paper is in stock: specialty and imported stock often needs 5–15 days to arrive, which is longer than the printing itself. When the date is tight, confirm paper availability first; chasing the printer is the slower route.
