HS Codes and Customs Guide for Garment Trims and Packaging

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 15, 2026

The HS code of a garment trim decides its duty rate, the regulatory conditions that apply and whether extra certificates are needed. Hang tags, woven labels, care labels, poly bags and paper bags all look like small accessories, yet they can fall into different chapters: the same label is classified differently in paper, textile and plastic; the same goods shipped on a garment or on their own may be treated differently. A wrong code means at best a duty adjustment, at worst a customs hold.

This guide follows the order classification logic, key variables, document checklist and free-trade agreement benefits, covering the issues that come up most often when shipping trims.

1. Why You Cannot Guess the HS Code

HS codes are six digits internationally, with each country adding its own subdivisions. The first six digits are universal; the rest are national, so the same product extends into different suffixes in China, the EU, the US and Japan. One wrong digit at declaration can move a line from zero duty to a double-digit rate, or wrongly trigger anti-dumping or quota controls.

For buyers, the code also governs whether a certificate of origin can be used, whether an FTA preferential rate applies, and whether the importing customs will demand composition testing or environmental declarations.

2. Classification Logic for Common Trims

The table below shows the chapter or heading trims usually fall under, for internal judgement and for briefing your broker. Final classification follows the customs tariff in force and the customs ruling — the same product can shift heading with composition, process or packing.

💡 Tip: Trims often ship as a coordinated kit with the garment. If hang tags, woven labels and poly bags share one carton declared under a single description, confirm with your broker whether they must be declared as separate lines — mixed declarations are where classification disputes most often start.

3. Four Variables That Drive Classification

  • Material — paper, textile, plastic, metal and leather sit in different chapters; this is the first test.
  • Printed or not — printing frequently moves the same material into a different heading.
  • Retail packing — rolls, bulk and retail-ready packs can classify differently.
  • Function — anti-static, flame-retardant or chip-enabled trims can trigger extra regulatory conditions.

4. Customs Document Checklist and Common Errors

A standard export set includes the commercial invoice (description, quantity, unit price, total, trade term), the packing list (packages, gross and net weight, volume), the declaration elements (material, composition, specification, process, brand), the contract and, where relevant, a certificate of origin. The declaration elements are the most overlooked item and the one that most affects classification.

  • Writing only labels or tags without stating paper, textile or plastic — the most common cause of inspection.
  • Omitted fibre content on woven labels, leaving customs unable to decide between cotton and man-made.
  • Mixing quantity units (pieces, rolls, dozens, kilograms) so figures do not reconcile with the packing list.
  • Invoice and declared values differ, or sampling and plate fees are left out entirely.

5. Certificates of Origin and FTA Preferential Rates

Trims made in China and shipped to RCEP members such as ASEAN, Australia, New Zealand, Korea and Japan, or to other FTA markets, can normally claim a preferential rate with a certificate of origin. Three conditions apply: the heading must appear in the agreement schedule; the origin rule must be met (wholly obtained or regional value content); and the certificate type must match the destination (general CO, RCEP certificate, or the dedicated China-Australia / China-Korea forms).

For markets without an FTA with China, such as the EU and the US, focus instead on MFN rates, anti-dumping measures and the importing country's labelling and environmental rules (packaging recyclability, chemical restrictions). These do not change the HS code but they do affect a clean release.

6. Checklist to Agree With Your Trim Supplier

  • Ask for the suggested HS code and declaration elements on the quotation, not just a product name.
  • Confirm fibre percentages on woven and care labels for origin-rule assessment.
  • Request composition tests and REACH/CPSIA declarations up front to avoid document requests at destination.
  • For mixed shipments, box by material and label each carton so lines can be declared separately.
  • Over time build your own trim code list including HS codes, and reuse it on every reorder.
