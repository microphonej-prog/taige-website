Hat, Scarf and Glove Trims Guide: Fibre Labels, Sizing and Gift Boxes

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 23, 2026

Hats, scarves and gloves are the classic winter accessories, and their trims requirements differ most from garment work: the buyer cannot try them on, fibre content matters above all else, sizing runs on head circumference and palm circumference, and a large share is bought as a gift. A care label that misstates the fibre blend on a wool scarf generates returns and complaints faster than on a garment. This guide covers where to sew neck labels, fibre and care labels, how to write size labels, how to hang tags, and how to configure gift boxes and e-commerce bags.

The guide works through category characteristics, hats, scarves and wraps, gloves, tags and attachment, then packaging, and closes with a specification table and a five-step acceptance list you can copy into your quote request.

1. Four Things That Set Accessory Trims Apart

  • No fitting, so fibre content replaces feel: the buyer cannot touch or try the item, so the fibre label carries the value judgement. Wool, cashmere, acrylic and viscose percentages must match the actual blend and be stated as ratios, for example 30% cashmere and 70% wool; a wrong figure is mislabelling.
  • Sizing runs on circumference: hats go by head circumference such as 54–56 cm and 56–58 cm, gloves by palm circumference and hand length, and scarves are mostly one size. Printing centimetres and S/M/L together reduces customer service questions.
  • A high gift share: accessories dominate holiday gifting, so gift boxes, ribbon and price-free brand cards are in greater demand than for garments. The tag must share one visual system with the box, or the unboxing reads as two different brands.
  • Worn against skin: a scarf sits on the neck and a hat on the forehead, so label placement and material matter more than on outerwear. For wool and cashmere, use soft woven labels or heat transfer to avoid a scratchy hard edge.

2. Hats: Brand Label, Care Label and Head Size

  • Sew the brand label at back centre or inside the strap: knit hats suit a soft woven or heat-transfer label at back centre, the least visible spot; on a cap with an adjustable strap, sew the label inside the strap rather than on the sweatband for better wear.
  • Put the care label inside at the left: never at the front of the forehead, where it becomes noticeable within ten minutes. On wool hats, state clearly whether dry cleaning is required or machine washing is acceptable.
  • Size labels state the head range: the common split is 54–56 cm and 56–58 cm, and adjustable styles should list the adjustment range. For kids, pairing age group with head circumference is more convincing than the vague term children's size.
  • Print and stitching on a structured cap: the sweatband can take print or a woven label, but stitches must not pierce the crown and leave needle holes, which let rain through and distort the shape.

3. Scarves and Wraps: Sewing a Long Label Without Showing It

  • Sew one third in from the short edge or along a lengthwise fold: that spot hides under itself once the scarf is knotted and folds away cleanly. Never sew into the fringe or tassels, which will pull loose within a couple of washes.
  • Narrow tape suits a long piece: a 15–20 mm tape sewn lengthwise rolls less than a wide label. When fibre, care and origin all have to fit, use a two-ply narrow tape rather than one large patch.
  • Put dimensions on the label: length by width in centimetres is basic information for a scarf, and wool or cashmere pieces should state dry cleaning and ironing instructions to avoid claims after a mishandled wash.
  • A corner woven label is cheap branding real estate: a small woven logo tab at one short edge looks better than repeating the logo on the tag, but keep contrast in mind: never put a pale tonal tab on a pale scarf.

4. Gloves: Sizing, Marking and Inner Labels

  • Size by palm circumference and hand length: adult ranges typically run 6.5 to 9 inches or S to XL, with children sized by age. Printing the measuring method, the loop around the palm excluding the thumb, noticeably cuts size exchanges.
  • Keep the inner label away from the thumb crotch: inside the cuff is the safe position. Leather gloves should use heat transfer or a woven label, and stitching must not pierce the leather in a way that starts a tear.
  • Mark function and compliance: touchscreen compatibility, warmth rating and water-repellent finish all deserve a line on the tag. Kids gloves must avoid detachable small parts and meet the target market's pull-force and sharp-edge rules.
  • Never break up a pair: retail commonly joins the two gloves with a tag gun or a hanging card, and counts are reconciled by pair at shipping. A single missing glove cannot even be processed as a return.

5. Tags and Attachment: Cord, Fastener or Pin

  • Use thin cotton or elastic cord on knits: thread it through the card and then the fabric. Avoid metal pins: the hole and any burr snag the yarn, and one snag pulls a whole run.
  • Match the fastener to the material: scarves suit plastic fasteners or branded woven tape, while leather gloves and belts look better with a plastic fastener or thin leather cord. Any metal part needs nickel release testing confirmed.
  • Layer the information: keep the front to brand and one selling line, and put material, care, origin and barcode on the back. On gift styles, replace dense text on the back with care icons, which photograph better.
  • Separate compliance from pricing: do not print prices on the tag; use in-store tickets. For the EU, mark the responsible person and local-language information under GPSR, and confirm the customer's template before finalising artwork.

6. Packaging: Unit Bags, Peg Cards and Multi-Piece Boxes

  • Let unit bags breathe: wool and cashmere accessories suffer when sealed, so punch holes in OPP zip bags or paper bags, or use a micro-perforated film, and add desiccant for long sea freight to prevent trapped odour and mildew.
  • Run both retail and e-commerce packaging: retail wants a peg card for display, e-commerce a tamper-evident bag with a brand card. Label information must match between the two, or stock counting gets muddled.
  • Ratio the winter three-piece set: hat, scarf and glove boxes are where pieces go missing. State pieces per set, colour and size ratios on the packing list, and keep tissue and tag visuals consistent with the box.
  • Cartons and fibre declarations: carton marks must match the tag brand name, and accessories containing wool, down or other animal fibres may face quarantine or fibre declaration requirements at destination, so confirm the document list with the customer before shipping.

8. Sampling and Acceptance: Five Checks

  1. Wear test: keep the hat on for ten minutes to check the forehead, wrap the scarf once to check the label does not scrape, and make a fist in the gloves to confirm no hard edge at the thumb crotch
  2. Reconcile the fibre label: check every line against the material issue sheet, so blend ratios, origin and care symbols match reality
  3. Wash and pilling: wash five times using the care method stated on the label, then check label edge fraying, scarf pilling and colour migration on leather styles
  4. Attachment assembly: card the goods the way they will ship and confirm the cord or fastener does not snag, the barcode scans first time, and the card does not cover the main brand visual
  5. Sets and packing: reconcile pieces per set, colour and size ratios and box layering against the packing list, and confirm the replacement rule for shortages
💡 Tip: on accessories the failures are rarely about printing — they are a label sewn into the fringe and a fibre ratio that does not match the material sheet.
