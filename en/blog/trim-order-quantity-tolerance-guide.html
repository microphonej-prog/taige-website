Trims Order Quantity Tolerance: Over-Runs, Shortfalls, Spares and Reorders

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 28, 2026

Quantity is where trims orders most often disagree: the PO says 10,000 hang tags and 10,300 arrive - is that an over-run? Two hundred short - must they be replaced? Printing overs, weaving waste, cutting and sampling all make the delivered quantity differ from the order, and the industry works with sensible bands. What those bands are, how to write them into the contract, and who absorbs the excess or the shortfall is where receiving turns argumentative. This guide covers the sources of the gap, tolerance wording, spare rates and reorder rules.

1. Where the Gap Comes From: Four Sources

  • Printing and die-cutting overs: set-up and paper waste push actual output above or below the order
  • Weaving and cutting waste: woven and care labels lose material at weaving, dyeing, cutting and folding
  • Sampling: AQL inspection removes pieces; agree whether opened cartons still count as delivered
  • Counting errors by colour and size: sorting by colour-way and kitting is where counts slip

2. How Much Tolerance: Bands and Wording

Tolerance is not something a supplier invents; it is negotiated around process waste and minimum machine batches. Writing the tolerance for each item type into the order confirmation beats arguing at receiving. The bands below are common in the trade, but the process and the number of colour-ways still decide.

Item Usual tolerance Notes
Hang tags (print and die-cut) about ±3% to ±5% Shaped dies, foiling and embossing add their own overs
Woven labels (woven and jacquard) about ±5% to ±10% Weaving and cutting waste is higher, worse with many colours or shades
Care labels (tape and coated) about ±5% to ±10% Sold by piece or by roll, piece counts drift a little more
Bags (poly and paper) about ±3% to ±5% Printing and bag-making overs run together; whole-carton counts are easy to check

Three points are worth spelling out: whether tolerance applies per item or to the whole order; how the out-of-tolerance portion is handled; and whether split deliveries are allowed to make up the total. Get those in writing and receiving has a basis.

3. Handling Over-Runs and Shortfalls

  • Over-run within tolerance: normally received and invoiced as delivered, with the unit price for the excess agreed up front
  • Beyond tolerance: the buyer may receive and pay only the ordered quantity; the supplier handles the rest
  • Shortfall within tolerance: not normally a breach, settled on the quantity received
  • Beyond tolerance: check the impact on production first, top up within a deadline if possible, otherwise apply the contract remedy
  • Split deliveries: fix the number of shipments and each arrival date, or the last batch becomes the argument

4. Setting the Spare Rate: More Is Not Better

A spare rate insures the sewing line; it is not free volume. Setting it by item and process usually costs less than adding a flat ten per cent: printed hang tags and care labels often sit at 1% to 3%, woven labels and hand-assembled trims at 2% to 5%. Pack spares separately and label them as spares so they can be counted apart from the main batch.

5. Colour-Ways and Size Break: The Real Threshold

Ten thousand tags split into four colours of 2,500 is a different cost structure from ten thousand in one colour. Printing, foiling and weaving all need a new set-up per colour, so the minimum per colour-way is the real threshold. Confirm it before deciding whether to merge colour-ways or move to digital printing.

  • The closer each colour-way minimum is to the overall MOQ, the closer the price is to bulk
  • With many colours and small runs, digital printing or smart imposition can win
  • If size labels are packed by size, agree the minimum per size too

6. Reorders: When You Can Skip the Plate Cost

  • Same artwork, material and process usually need no new tooling, but a minimum still applies
  • A very small top-up may be priced at the minimum or carry a set-up charge
  • Any change to artwork or material is a new order, with fresh tolerance and minimums
  • If spares are needed long term, order them with the original PO rather than topping up piecemeal later

7. Six Things Your Quantity Sheet Should State

  1. Ordered quantity and allowed tolerance per item
  2. Whether colour-ways or sizes apply, and the minimum per combination
  3. Spare rate, and whether spares are packed and labelled separately
  4. Whose count governs delivery, and how it is verified
  5. How over-runs and shortfalls are handled and settled
  6. Minimum reorder quantity, and whether set-up or tooling is charged
💡 A practical habit: put ordered quantity, tolerance, minimum per colour-way and spare rate on a single quantity sheet confirmed with the artwork. Agree the counting rule up front and receiving needs no favours.
