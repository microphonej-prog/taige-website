Silicone, PVC and TPU Labels: How to Choose the Right Rubber Label

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 12, 2026

Silicone labels and PVC rubber labels are the most common 3D trims on activewear, kidswear, bags and footwear. They stand apart from woven and printed labels through thickness, hand feel and relief. But silicone, PVC and TPU differ sharply in weather resistance, eco profile and cost — the wrong choice leads to cracking, colour loss or rejected shipments.

This guide walks through material, process, attachment, design and MOQ, then compliance, so you can specify a silicone or rubber label correctly the first time.

1. What Silicone and Rubber Labels Actually Are

Silicone labels are moulded from silicone rubber into 3D letters or motifs, typically 1–3mm thick. They are soft against the skin and resist heat, washing and chlorine, which makes them standard on activewear, yoga wear, swimwear and kidswear, as well as cord ends and zip pulls.

Rubber labels usually means PVC rubber labels: PVC paste is poured into a mould and cured. Colours are saturated, large areas are easy, and the cost sits below silicone — but PVC contains chlorine and is hard to recycle, so brands with eco claims tend to avoid it.

TPU labels are the fastest-growing third option: thermoplastic polyurethane, elastic, abrasion-resistant and halogen-free, bondable to fabric by heat press or high-frequency welding, more recyclable than PVC and priced between PVC and silicone.

3. Processes: Moulding and Surface Effects

  • Compression moulding — the workhorse for both silicone and PVC; relief depth comes from the engraved mould, usually copper or steel.
  • Liquid silicone rubber injection (LSR) — high precision and minimal flash for small, detailed motifs, with higher tooling investment.
  • Multi-colour and two-tone — built up by repeated moulding or dispensing; more colours mean more steps, cost and lead time.
  • Surface finishing — matte, sandblasted, gloss, screen printing, spray coating or laser engraving; gloss shows scratches, matte ages better.
  • Adhesive backing and pre-punched holes — 3M adhesive, stitching holes or cord holes must be decided before the mould is cut; they cannot be added afterwards.

4. Choosing the Attachment Method

  • Sewing — the strongest option; allow a 2–3mm margin and enough hole positions. Dense stitching tears the material, so silicone needs extra thickness allowance.
  • Heat press and HF welding — TPU or adhesive-backed labels bond straight to fabric, but temperature and pressure must be trialled first to avoid scorching synthetics.
  • Cord and hardware — for cord ends, zip pulls and tag accessories; hole diameter must match cord gauge, and rounded corners greatly reduce wear and breakage.
  • Adhesive only — fine for bags and footwear that are rarely washed; for garments that go through repeated laundering, adhesive alone is not enough.

5. Design and Sampling Points

  • Keep line width at 0.3mm or more and minimum character height at 1.5–2mm; finer strokes blur in the mould and lose detail on the finished label.
  • Avoid needle-sharp corners — moulds cannot reproduce a true point, and rounded transitions are both more stable and kinder to skin.
  • Thickness drives the 3D look and feel, with 1–2mm the common range; too thick hinders stitching and comfort, too thin loses the relief.
  • Size tolerance is usually held at ±0.2–0.3mm. Colour can only come close to Pantone; silicone and PVC render colour very differently from paper printing, so compare shades under the same light source.
  • Sign off a physical sample before cutting the mould: confirm feel, thickness, colour and attachment in the final material, then scale up.

6. MOQ, Lead Time and Compliance

Silicone and rubber labels both carry a one-off tooling cost, so the MOQ depends on size and mould structure: the more complex and larger the design, the higher the tooling investment. A trial order to test the market before scaling up is usually the sensible route. Sampling runs 3–7 days and bulk production 10–25 days; add a week of buffer when colour matching and safety testing are involved. Unit pricing depends on material, thickness, number of colours and quantity band.

On compliance, children's products need extra care: US CPSIA caps phthalates in children's items at 0.1%, so if you choose PVC, insist on a phthalate-free formulation and third-party test reports. For the EU, watch the REACH SVHC and restricted substances lists. For products in prolonged skin contact, ask for food-grade silicone testing (FDA 21 CFR or LFGB). If sustainability is your angle, note that silicone is halogen-free but hard to recycle, so recyclability claims fit TPU better.

💡 Tip: feel, colour and thickness differences between silicone and PVC can only be judged in the hand. Before ordering, request a physical sample from the same production batch, scratch it with a fingernail, stretch it repeatedly, then run a home wash and dry cycle — only scale up once there is no cracking, whitening or colour loss.
