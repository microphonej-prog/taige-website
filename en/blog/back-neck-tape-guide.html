Back Neck Tape: Four Jobs, Eight Numbers and Three Sewing Methods

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 14, 2026

Back neck tape is the narrow strip that covers the seam allowance at the back of the neck. It never appears in a lookbook and never becomes a selling point, yet it answers four questions at once: the overlock seam must not scratch, the neck label must not sit directly on skin, the neckline must not grow in the wash, and the brand needs a second place to be read. Choose the wrong material for a 15 mm strip and all four fail together.

1. Four jobs served by one strip

  • It covers the overlock: the back-neck seam sits against the nape and is the first place customers report scratching, so the tape covers it end to end.
  • It isolates the neck label: the label is stitched to the tape instead of the shell, which reduces corner curl and redness after washing.
  • It stabilises the neckline: this is where knitwear distorts first, and a low-shrinkage twill tape acts as a dimensional insurance policy.
  • It carries brand information: putting composition, size and origin here gets read far more often than hiding it in a side-seam label.

2. Three materials and where each belongs

  • Polyester twill tape: the default. Densely woven, dimensionally stable, wash resistant, and on the firm side. Right for T-shirts, sweatshirts and outerwear where the neckline needs body.
  • Knit tape or jersey binding: elastic and comfortable on skin, right for knit dresses, underwear and childrenswear. The weakness is dimensional stability, so it has to be matched to the shell's shrinkage.
  • Satin ribbon: high lustre for eveningwear, silk and premium womenswear. It snags easily, so confirm the edge is hot-cut.
  • Cotton twill tape: supports a natural-fibre story for organic cotton and natural-positioned lines. Shrinkage runs higher, so pre-shrink or add cut length.

3. Eight parameters that must be written as numbers

Parameter Typical range Why it becomes a claim
Width 12–20 mm, 15 mm usual Too narrow and the overlock shows; too wide and it peeks past the shoulder seam
Thickness 0.3–0.5 mm Too thick and it ridges through the neckline; too thin and it stops stabilising
Weight 60–120 g/m² Weight drives hand feel and cost together; leave it out and buyers compare price only
Shrinkage 2% or less after washing Neckline growth and puckering both start here; tape and shell must shrink the same way
Colour tolerance ΔE 1.0 or less The tape sits right next to the shell, so any drift is magnified
Stitch density 10–12 stitches per 3 cm Too sparse and the seam floats; too dense and the knit pulls into a wave
Recovery 90% or more after 30% extension Elastic-bound necklines that do not recover wave after two wears
Cut direction State bias cut or straight cut Two cut directions on one style means one edge puckers and the other stays flat

4. Three sewing methods and the trap in each

Single-needle binding is the default: fold the tape over the seam allowance and run one row of topstitch through all three layers. It is fast, and the price is that the stitch must catch the bottom layer, or the seam allowance shows the moment the neck is turned. Double-fold binding belongs to mid and high price points: the tape edges are folded before wrapping, giving a clean edge at the cost of thickness, and the neckline reads firmer. Overlock-and-topstitch is the cheapest and suits underwear and childrenswear, but the overlock edge fuzzes after about five washes, which matters if the garment has to pass a sharp-edge review.

5. Hand feel, labelling and compliance

The neck tape is a part that sits against skin for hours, so two layers have to be checked together. On feel: do not buy flatness with a stiffening finish, because that finish turns on you after the first wash, and specify hot-cut or laser-cut edges to cut down on fuzzing friction. On compliance: textiles in skin contact should reach OEKO-TEX Standard 100 Class II, dyestuffs and auxiliaries must meet the azo dye and formaldehyde limits in Annex XVII of REACH Regulation 1907/2006, and childrenswear adds the safety rules of whichever market it enters.

6. How to write the line so it does not come back three times

A line that works reads like this: back neck tape, polyester twill, 15 mm wide by 0.4 mm thick, 60 g/m², natural white, shrinkage 2% or less, hot-cut edge, dyed to match the shell within ΔE 1.0, single-needle binding with one row of topstitch at 10–12 stitches per 3 cm. Material, five numbers, edge finish, colour relationship and sewing method in one sentence. Leave any of them out and the factory fills the gap with the cheapest option, which is rarely the one you wanted.

7. The three most common reasons a neck tape gets rejected

First is waviness: tape and shell shrink in different directions and one wash turns the neck into a wave. Second is a hard edge: a stiffening finish bought a flat look that hardens further in the wash and presses on the neck within two hours of wear. Third is exposed seam allowance: the topstitch missed the bottom layer, so a pale line appears as soon as the neck is turned. None of the three is a design problem; all three are specification problems, and writing the numbers above costs almost nothing while removing the defect.
