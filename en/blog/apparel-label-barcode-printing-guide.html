Barcode Printing for Apparel Labels: Three Routes, Thermal Options and Ribbon Choice

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 11, 2026

There are only two ways to get a barcode onto an apparel label: print it into the hang tag or care label, or print it on site with a barcode printer. Pre-printing suits fixed content and large volumes; on-site printing suits variable data, short runs and late changes. The real difficulty is not which route to take but whether the code scans and whether it is still readable after washing: direct thermal labels fade with heat and light, and a care label barcode printed with the wrong ribbon turns into a grey smear after two or three home laundries. This guide covers the three sources of barcodes, direct thermal versus thermal transfer, wax to resin ribbons, matching resolution to barcode grade, and what to write into the order.

1. Three Routes for Barcodes on Labels

  • Pre-printed into the hang tag or care label: fixed content, large volumes and the lowest unit cost, ideal for barcode hang tags, price tickets and fixed codes on care labels; the price is that any change means reprinting the whole run
  • Adhesive labels printed on site: direct thermal or thermal transfer, applied to the back of the hang tag, the bag or the carton; the practical route for variable data such as FNSKU codes, batch numbers and carton marks that change at short notice
  • Heat transfer labels: pre-printed by the trims supplier and pressed onto the fabric or care label; the best wash durability, for body-contact positions and for codes and size marks that must survive laundering

4. Printer and Resolution: If It Does Not Scan, It Is Not Passed

  • Match resolution to bar width: 203 dpi suits 1D codes of 10 mil (0.25 mm) and above plus shipping labels; 300 dpi suits hang tag and care label codes of 5 to 7.5 mil; 600 dpi is for very small symbols and dense 2D codes
  • Put a barcode grade in the spec: 1D codes are graded A to F under ISO/IEC 15416 and 2D codes under ISO/IEC 15415; retail and warehouse customers commonly require grade C or better, and falling below that clearly raises the risk of rejection and returns
  • Do not trim the quiet zone: an insufficient quiet zone on either side of the code is the most common hidden cause of scan failures on site; when laying several labels across a small sheet, keep each quiet zone intact
  • Dial in darkness and speed: too dark a setting bleeds the edges and distorts bar width, too light loses contrast; run a darkness ladder to find the optimum and lock the settings into the device so they do not drift between shifts

5. Why Care Label Barcodes Fail: Five Common Causes

  • Wrong ribbon: a wax or wax-resin ribbon on a nylon or coated care label can shed on the very first wash
  • Wrong substrate: standard card and uncoated tape do not hold the ink; a coated care label, PET or nylon gives the code something to grip
  • Too little energy: the code looks black but the layer sits on the surface and rubs off; a tape peel test finds this faster than a wash test
  • Wash conditions out of range: fabric softener, chlorine bleach, washes above 60 degrees and tumble drying all accelerate fading; any wash-cycle claim must be validated against the customer's actual conditions with the method stated
  • Folding and pressing: sewing the code into a fold or letting the iron touch it cracks or transfers the layer, and the finished garment no longer scans

6. Six On-Site Printing Faults and How to Prevent Them

  • Smearing and ghosting: usually excessive darkness, too slow a speed or carbon build-up on the printhead — clean the head first, then reduce darkness
  • Missing lines and white dots: a dead element in the printhead or a creased ribbon — print a test pattern to locate it and rebalance ribbon tension
  • Looks fine, will not scan: usually a trimmed quiet zone or weak contrast; use reversed-out codes on black and coloured codes with care
  • Labels lifting off: PE and PP are low surface energy materials and need high-tack or aggressive adhesives, with surface treatment if necessary
  • Static and dust: common in dry workshops and a source of white dots and feed drift; humidification or static eliminators fix most of it
  • Ribbon and label mismatch: a width that does not fit or an inward-outward ribbon loaded the wrong way simply prints nothing; check the spec and the wound side before running

7. Five Things to Write into the Order and the Acceptance Check

  1. Substrate spec: hang tag card, synthetic paper, PET, nylon or coated care label, with grammage and thickness stated, so print behaviour does not shift between batches
  2. Ribbon type and grade: wax, wax-resin or resin, ideally with a specific grade; never change ribbons mid-order
  3. Resolution and grade: state 203 or 300 dpi and the grade required, for example grade C or better for 1D codes
  4. Wash requirement and method: number of washes, temperature, whether softener or bleach is used, line dry or tumble dry — validated against the customer's real laundry conditions
  5. Samples and sampling rule: keep a barcode reference sample and a print test strip from the first run, and agree how many pieces to scan (say 20 per batch, three scans each) and what happens when a code fails
