Woven Labels vs Embroidery: Feel, Cost & When to Use Each

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated August 21, 2026

Woven labels and embroidery are both common ways to brand garments, but they differ greatly in how they are made, how they feel and what they cost — choose wrong and the look misses your brand positioning, or the budget blows out. This article compares them across five dimensions so you pick the right process.

1. How Each Is Made

A woven label is created directly on a jacquard loom with yarns — the text and artwork are literally woven. An embroidery label is stitched onto fabric or tape by an embroidery machine, producing a raised, dimensional design.

  • Woven: thin, flat and smooth on both sides
  • Embroidery: layered threads, strong 3D feel and a crafted look; the back usually needs a lining
  • Woven excels at fine small type and tonal gradients; embroidery suits bold logos and large lettering

2. Feel & Durability Compared

ItemWovenEmbroidery
Thickness / flatnessThin and flatThick and dimensional
FeelSmooth against skinTextured
Wash resistanceGood (satin/polyester best)Good, but watch for loose stitches
Abrasion resistanceFairStrong
DetailFine text and lines possibleSmall type blurs; best for large shapes

For skin-contact spots like necklines and side seams, woven labels win — thin and non-irritating. For display areas like jacket chests and cap fronts, the dimensional feel of embroidery carries the brand better.

3. Cost & MOQ Compared

Woven label cost is driven by loom time and color count; MOQ is typically 1,000+ pcs and unit price drops with volume. Embroidery is priced by stitch count and stitched area — flexible and workable in small batches, but cost climbs fast with dense, large designs.

  • Woven: best for large volumes and repeat orders, with an edge in fine detail
  • Embroidery: suits small batches and mid-to-high price points; strong with big logos and bold lines
  • Both get cheaper per unit at higher volumes — confirm with sample quotes

5. Using Them Together

Established brands often mix both: a woven main label at the neckline for comfort, embroidery on the chest or back to reinforce brand memory, and printed tape care labels — each plays to its strengths and total cost stays under control.

💡 Tip: small embroidered lettering (under 10mm high) tends to blur — always sample first; for woven labels, confirm yarn colors match your Pantone references. Sampling usually takes 3–7 days.

7. Setting the Spec: Density, Thread Colors and Minimum Letter Height

Once the process is chosen, the spec parameters decide the outcome. Woven labels hinge on density and fold type; embroidery hinges on stitch count and backing. Both have a minimum letter height below which type simply blurs. Put these three items on the sample sheet and rework rates drop noticeably.

Spec itemWoven labelEmbroidery label
Minimum letter heightAbout 1.5–2 mm; smaller and you get floats and letters mergingAbout 3–4 mm; stitch pitch and thread weight set legibility, and small type forces high stitch counts
Color count4–8 colors is typical; each extra color adds loom time and plate costChosen from a thread chart; color count mainly affects thread-change time, not the digitizing logic
Edge finishingHot cut, ultrasonic cut, end fold / center fold / miter fold; a fold consumes 3–5 mm of marginLaser or ultrasonic cut; the back needs paper backing, hot-melt film or a water-soluble backing, otherwise thread ends scratch skin
Hand feelThin, flat and smooth; can weave in size and care informationDimensional and slightly stiff; large embroidered areas affect stretch and drape

8. Incoming Inspection: Five Checks You Can Finish in Ten Minutes

Labels are low-value items, but one wrong batch can delay an entire apparel shipment. Run these five checks on arrival; it costs far less than fixing it later.

  1. Compare with the sealed sample: text, typeface, proportions and color, item by item — no "close enough"
  2. Edges: are folds aligned, any fraying, and do hot-cut edges show yellowing or a hard rim
  3. Back side: on woven labels check for floats; on embroidery check for exposed backing and loose thread ends
  4. Color: compare pieces within the lot, viewing dark and light shades separately; across lots, the sealed sample is the reference
  5. Quantity and packing: pack by size and color, mark it on the carton label so repeat orders can match the original plates

9. Four Design Mistakes That Cause Most Rework

  • Logo lines too fine: detail below the minimum letter height cannot be woven — it must be enlarged or simplified, and the later you find out the more it costs
  • Fine reversed-out type: white thread on a dark ground fuzzes up with abrasion; avoid fine knockouts on skin-contact areas
  • Type under the fold: a fold eats 3–5 mm, so text too close to the edge disappears into it and fails inspection outright
  • Ignoring fabric stretch: embroidery on stretch fabric moves with the cloth, and excessive density causes puckering and post-wash distortion

10. Do Not Use Embroidery as Your Care Label

A common misconception: "the composition is embroidered on the chest, so we do not need a care label." The US FTC Care Labeling Rule (16 CFR Part 423) requires care instructions to be permanently attached and legible; embroidery rubs against skin and few brands would stitch full composition and care text onto the chest anyway. The compliant split is simple: embroidery carries brand identity, the care label (woven or printed) carries composition, origin and care instructions.

For the EU, GPSR (EU) 2023/988 adds traceability information and an EU responsible person — content that also needs a label readable over the garment's life. Treating "brand label + care label" as one design system rather than two loose items saves a lot of late re-versioning and re-labelling.

11. How Brands Usually Combine Woven Labels and Embroidery

In practice it is rarely either-or. The most common pairing is a woven main label plus embroidered chest logo: the main label must be thin, survive repeated washing and carry size and composition — a woven label handles that. Chest and cap fronts are the visual focus, where the dimensional feel of embroidery supports a higher price point. Another option is a woven label with an embroidered border, well suited to small light-luxury ranges.

On cost, woven labels are the steadier choice for large repeat orders; embroidery is more flexible for small, multi-style, fast-turnaround ranges because no loom plate is needed. Tell your supplier both the annual volume and the number of styles, and you will usually get a better structured quote than from a single-style inquiry.

12. Five Steps From Brand Positioning to a Locked Specification

Here is the earlier material condensed into an executable checklist. Work through these five steps and quoting, sampling and bulk inspection all run smoother — and you avoid the worst schedule killer: discovering after sampling that the price point does not work.

  1. Fix the price point and channel: fast fashion cares about unit cost and lead time, accessible luxury about feel and detail, sportswear about wash resistance and stretch. Write down the retail price and the channel before discussing any process.
  2. Decide placement and size: thin woven labels for skin-contact spots such as back neck and side seam; embroidery for display areas like chest and cap front. Once placement is fixed, size and fold type have a basis.
  3. Set letter height and color count: shrink the logo to actual size and check that the finest line still sits above the minimum height. Choose colors by brand palette — if four do the job, do not pay for eight.
  4. Settle edges and back: fold type drives the sewing operation and the look, while back finishing drives feel and complaints — for next-to-skin pieces the back must be finished or thread ends scratch.
  5. Sample and wash-test: keep a sealed sample, run 3–5 washes per ISO 6330 or AATCC 135, grade color fastness to ISO 105-C06, and check distortion and fuzzing before moving to bulk.

None of these steps adds cost, and together they block most rework. The fifth matters most: skipping the wash test at sampling pushes the risk onto the consumer, and resolving a single complaint usually costs more than a whole label batch.

13. Woven Label Sizes and Fold Types at a Glance

Size and fold decide where the label goes and how it is sewn. Main labels commonly run from 30×50 mm to 40×70 mm, adjusted to the logo proportions and text length. Fold type is the more overlooked of the two, yet it drives the sewing operation directly.

Fold typeHow it is sewnWhere it belongs
Straight cutSewn on one side only; the other sits flat or is edge-stitchedInside positions that must stay flat, such as back neck and waistband
End foldBoth ends folded once, with the seam passing through the folded edgeSide seams and hems; stable when the label is long
Center foldFolded in half and caught in the seam, readable from both sidesSmall-to-medium main labels and styles needing information on both sides
Miter foldCorners folded at an angle for a clean, fray-free edgeHigher-priced garments and raw-edge necklines

Size labels usually run in the same batch as the main label, on the same tape and fold, with only the size numbers changed. Treating sizes as one plate with different content saves duplicate plate work — but since "S/M/L" and "90/100/110" differ in length, check the readable area on a center fold against the longest size before fixing the dimension.

Once the fold is approved, write it into the spec sheet and keep the sealed sample. On a repeat order, cite that spec rather than saying "same as last time" — a new account handler at the supplier means verbal descriptions drift, and lead time pays for it.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do MOQs differ between woven labels and embroidery?

Woven labels start around 1,000 pieces per spec and color; embroidery can go down to a few hundred, though high stitch counts and large areas raise the unit price clearly. The closer you fill a full plate, the more room there is on price.

How is the sampling fee calculated?

Woven labels need a loom plate, so the fee usually includes plate cost; embroidery only needs digitizing, which is cheaper. In most cases the sample fee is credited against the bulk order — confirm beforehand whether plate cost and freight are included.

Can one garment use both woven labels and embroidery?

Yes, and it is very common. Use a woven main label for wash resistance and complete information, and embroidery on display areas for perceived quality. Align the color standards between them, or one garment ends up showing two different "brand colors".

How do I prevent distortion or puckering after washing?

Embroidery on stretch fabric is the usual culprit: excessive density, no backing and too-hot washing all cause puckering. Counter it by lowering stitch density, adding a fusible backing, stating water temperature and care on the label, and running a wash test at sampling.

What should I watch for on lead times and repeat orders?

Standard samples take 3–7 working days; bulk runs 10–15 days depending on quantity and capacity, and peak season needs more. If the fabric or color changes on a repeat, always re-approve the plates — producing straight from old plates is the most common source of color deviation.
