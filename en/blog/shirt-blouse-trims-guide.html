Shirt and Blouse Trims Guide: Neck Labels, Collar Sizes and Packing Boards

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 24, 2026

Shirts and blouses are among the most trim-sensitive categories: the fabric is flat, the lines are clean, and the thickness of a neck label, the weight of a tag fastener or the stiffness of a packing board all show up in the first seconds of unboxing. At the same time shirts are washed often, frequently given wrinkle-free finishes, and exported into several sizing systems, so trims have to be specified more finely than on casualwear.

This guide works through four blocks — neck label and size label, care label, hang tag with fastener, packaging — and closes with a specification sheet you can quote from and a five-step sampling check, so brands and factories can lock shirt trims in one pass.

1. Four Requirements Shirts Place on Trims

  • Flatness: the collar and placket are flat surfaces where any bulge or curl is magnified, so label thickness drives the visual verdict
  • Wash and wear: shirts are machine-washed and worn often, so neck labels and care labels must survive repeated abrasion and laundering
  • It must carry a sizing system: shirts often use collar sizes (39, 15.5), and the size label has less room than on other garments, so plan the layout early
  • Light weight: shirts are light, so a heavy fastener or tag pulls at the collar yarn and can leave press marks on the fabric in transit

2. Neck Label and Size Label: Position, Size, Collar Size

Woven shirts usually take a light satin woven label, or a tagless heat-transfer label. On premium or pale lightweight styles, tagless is preferable to avoid show-through and pressure. Size can be woven into the same label as the brand, or sewn as a separate label on one side of the collar stand; the first saves labour but locks the size slots in advance, the second is more flexible on size changes and lighter on stock.

  • Typical size: a neck label of 20–30mm wide and 15–25mm high works well; keep letter height at 1.2mm or above or woven strokes blur
  • Placement: 5–8mm below the centre back neckline, clear of the collar stand stitching; a separate size label sits on one side of the stand so it does not rub the nape
  • Collar size wording: for Europe and the US the label often carries collar size (cm or inches) plus the generic size — keep it identical on the care label, hang tag and product page

3. Care Label: Machine Wash, Wrinkle-Free, Dark Shades

Shirt care labels are mostly nylon or polyester woven tape: washable, abrasion-resistant and legible. Paper or coated composite labels cost less but fuzz and fade after repeated machine washing. If a brand applies a wrinkle-free or resin finish, the care wording must match that finish, or customers following the label will still get creases and complain.

  • Match the base to the shade: dark shirts look better with a dark base and light type, pale shirts with a white base and dark type; the base must not show through
  • Symbols plus text: base the label on ISO 3758 symbols with one line of local-language text; the symbols travel across markets, the text is grouped per market
  • Attachment: shirt care labels are often sewn with the neck label into a side seam or inside the collar stand; avoid catching the structural stitching, which makes the corner flip out

4. Hang Tag and Fastener: Light and Mark-Free

Shirt hang tags run small — commonly 45×85mm or 50×90mm — on 300–400gsm coated or speciality stock, with soft-touch lamination or spot UV where texture matters. For attachment, cotton cord or a fine loop suits shirts better than hardware; plastic fasteners should be the slim gauge. Keep weight and thickness down and the collar will not distort, nor will the fabric take press marks in transit.

  • Information layout: brand and line on the front; composition, origin, barcode, size and price on the back, with blank space for a price sticker
  • Two-tag approach: a main tag for brand image and a second for fabric and finish (wrinkle-free, easy care) reads better than one crowded tag
  • Hole and fastener: punch 3–4mm; an eyelet with cotton cord looks neatest, or a thin loop with a slim plastic fastener — avoid heavy metal parts

5. Packaging: Board, Collar Support and Folding

Shirt packaging is the most under-specified part of the trim set and the one that most affects repeat purchase and gifting. It has to survive export transport and still arrive with a crisp collar and clean folds.

  • Packing board: typically 350–450gsm white board, sized to the fold and tag position; die-cut windows work for display — avoid boards that are too soft
  • Collar support and fold: include plastic or paper collar stays; a three-fold suits retail in a clear bag, a five-fold packs tighter for e-commerce and container shipping
  • Crease and moisture control: avoid over-vacuuming, which sets hard creases; add moisture control in the carton, and keep pale shirts away from dark bags and printed surfaces

7. Five-Step Sample Approval

  1. Sew it for real: stitch the labels onto the actual fabric and check thickness, hand and show-through — do not judge from flat samples
  2. Before and after wash: run one machine wash and dry cycle per the care label and compare colour, curling and legibility
  3. Full assembly: fit tags, fasteners, board and garment together and check whether the fastener marks the collar or the board presses the shape
  4. Transit simulation: pack into the export carton and run a short stacking or drop check to confirm tags stay flat and the collar holds
  5. Seal the sample: sign off and retain the approved sample as the reference for bulk inspection and repeat orders
💡 Tip: the riskiest part of shirt trims is not printing but consistency of size and composition. Any mismatch between neck label, care label, hang tag and the product page can get a shipment rejected. Keep one comparison sheet for all four and update them together.
