Wastewater in Woven Label Dyeing: The Numbers an Audit Checks

By TAGE Packaging · Updated November 28, 2026

A woven label uses almost no electricity and emits nothing at the press, which makes it look cleaner than a poly bag. But most of a woven label's weight is dyed yarn, and dyeing a kilogram of yarn consumes tens of litres of water. The environmental score of this trim is decided at the dyehouse discharge point, not at the printing press. That is why brand questionnaires have shifted from which ink do you use to does your dyehouse file a ZDHC report.

This piece skips the carbon slogans and handles four things: which process steps generate the wastewater, the four documents a buyer's audit asks for, how to read a report, and whether solution-dyed yarn is worth switching to.

1. Which process steps produce the water

Water use on a dye line concentrates in four steps: pretreatment (scouring, bleaching), dyeing itself, soaping and rinsing to remove unfixed dye, and fixation or softening. A conventional overflow or jigger machine runs a liquor ratio of roughly 1:8 to 1:15, meaning one kilogram of yarn against eight to fifteen kilograms of water; with rinsing, the line lands in the 80–150 litres per kilogram range. The real figure depends on machine type, shade depth and how many times the shade is corrected.

The cost logic in one line: one shade correction equals one more full bath. Fixing colour at the sampling stage is far cheaper than installing water reuse at the dyehouse.

2. The four documents a buyer's audit asks for

First, ZDHC MRSL conformance for the input chemistry (current MRSL v3.1), proving the dyes and auxiliaries contain no restricted substances. Second, a test report against the ZDHC Wastewater Guidelines (current v2.2), sampled at the final discharge point rather than a workshop drain. Third, laboratory accreditation to ISO/IEC 17025; without it the report is not accepted in brand systems. Fourth, the upload record: a ClearStream report must be findable on the ZDHC Gateway, and sending a PDF is not the same thing.

Two more systems usually come along: GOTS requires wet-processing units to treat effluent and meet its own limits for COD, BOD, AOX and heavy metals, while bluesign system partnership audits input chemistry and discharge together. Note that the ZDHC and GOTS limit tables are not the same numbers: one report saying pass may only pass one of the two. A report whose header does not state the standard and its version cannot be read.

A five-second test: if the front page lacks the standard name, its version, the sampling point and the lab accreditation number, send it back. That beats comparing limits line by line, because a report you cannot read is usually a report that does not pass.

4. The biggest water lever: solution-dyed yarn

Solution-dyed yarn puts the pigment into the polymer melt before extrusion. The dye bath and the soaping step disappear, water use for that yarn drops to near zero, and light fastness is usually better than vat dyeing. The trade-offs are three: a minimum order per colour, often in the hundreds of kilograms; a palette limited to what the spinner stocks; and a longer lead time than dyeing in-house. For stable core colours it is currently the cheapest way to cut water.

To claim both recycled content and water savings, use solution-dyed rPET yarn — but the recycled claim needs a GRS or RCS transaction certificate chain. A spinner's self-declaration will not survive a brand review. The usual error with these double claims is turning the yarn is recycled into the label is recycled, without allowing for weaving, dyeing and packing losses in between.

6. Five things a supplier should hand over unprompted

  • ① The dyehouse ZDHC Gateway ID, so the buyer can look it up instead of asking for it.
  • ② A wastewater report under 12 months old, with a photo of the sampling point and the lab's accreditation page.
  • ③ The chemical inventory — dyes, auxiliaries, fixing agents — with MRSL conformance statements.
  • ④ Shade corrections per lot and the actual liquor ratio: the most direct self-reported predictor of water use.
  • ⑤ If solution-dyed or rPET yarn is used, the spinner's GRS or RCS transaction certificates mapped to batch numbers.

Cost and MOQ belong in the same sentence: woven labels typically start at 3,000–5,000 pieces per style, and changing yarn — including a new solution-dyed shade — or changing the weave means a new sample. The sample fee is credited against bulk; the lost production slot is not. The real cost of an eco requirement in this category is not the yarn price but the fact that a new colour means a new yarn lot, which is why most brands end up moving core colours to solution-dyed yarn and leaving seasonal shades in the dye bath.
