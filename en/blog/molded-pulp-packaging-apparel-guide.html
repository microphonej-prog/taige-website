Molded Pulp Packaging: Why One Tool Decides Whether It Pays

By TAGE Packaging &nbsp;·&nbsp; Updated November 24, 2026

Molded pulp looks like the easy win of apparel packaging: paper-based, plastic-free and photogenic in an unboxing. Then the tooling quote arrives and the project stops. The material is ordinary — recycled board or sugarcane bagasse — but the process is water and fibre pressed into a shape, and the numbers that decide cost live in the tool, not in the pulp. This guide covers the two forming routes, wall thickness and load, tooling and minimum quantity, moisture in transit, and the certification wording that keeps a compostable claim legal.

1. Choose the Route First: Wet-Press or Dry-Press

Wet-press forms and squeezes the water out inside the tool. The result is a smoother surface, a tight dimensional tolerance, higher density, and walls down to 1.5 mm, which suits gift-box inserts that are seen up close. The price is higher tooling and drying energy. Dry-press, often called thermoformed fibre, forms the shape first and then presses it hot: the surface keeps a screen texture, the hand is coarser, cushioning is actually better, the tool is cheaper, and walls usually land between 2.5 mm and 3.0 mm.

A simple split: if the insert will be held and inspected by the customer, needs colour or has to follow a curved product surface, use wet-press. If its job is protection and replacing plastic, use dry-press. Dry-press will never quite look premium up close, and wet-press for a pure cushion overpays for tooling nobody sees.

2. Wall Thickness, Ribs and Load

Thickness is not a case of more is better; it has to match the load. Under 500 g, 1.5 mm is enough. Inserts usually run at 2.0 mm. A 3 to 5 kg product needs 2.5 to 3.0 mm with longitudinal ribs on the loaded face. Ribs raise the section's moment of inertia, and at equal material a ribbed wall can carry more than twice as much. Do not inspect thickness at acceptance, inspect the drop: ISTA 1A for a single package and ISTA 3A for a parcel, ten drops each, then open and check the product and the insert for cracks.

3. Tooling and Minimum Quantity: The Real Barrier

Molded pulp tooling comes as aluminium or resin. Aluminium holds tighter tolerances, lasts far longer and suits production, but the tool costs more. Resin is cheap and short-lived, which makes it the right choice for prototypes and small test runs. Minimum quantity usually lands between 3,000 and 10,000 pieces, depending on the tool type and how complex the geometry is. Change the geometry and the tool has to be cut again, which is why the insert design must be frozen at the sampling stage rather than fine-tuned just before production. Tooling is quoted per structure and per tool material; there is no general price list.

4. Moisture and Mould: The Sea Freight Gap

Molded pulp leaves the factory at 8 to 12 % moisture, and wet-press parts run higher. A 40-foot container spends 30 to 45 days at sea, and the day-night temperature swing pushes the relative humidity inside past 80 % repeatedly. The pulp starts absorbing water and mould has the conditions it needs by day seven to ten. The answer is a combination: dry the parts properly before packing, bag the inserts with a desiccant inside, hang container desiccants, and keep pallets at least 10 cm off the floor. Doing only one of these is usually the same as doing none.

5. Certification and Claims: Compostable Is Not Compostable Anywhere

EN 13432 and ASTM D6400 certify degradability under industrial composting conditions, at roughly 58 °C and within 180 days. Home composting is a separate certification. That makes an unqualified claim of 100 % biodegradable a high-risk statement in Europe: the EU packaging regulation (EU) 2025/40 now folds recyclability and labelling into its requirements, and national scrutiny of environmental claims keeps tightening. The practical approach is to state the conditions: industrially compostable, according to EN 13432, in a dedicated facility. This line appears on the packaging label, so like fibre content it is wording that legal should read.

6. When It Pays, and When It Does Not

It pays when three things are true at once: annual volume amortises the tool, the plastic insert has a real reason to go (regulation, brand policy, a customer requirement), and the packaging is part of the sell. It does not pay when a style runs under 1,000 pieces, when the pack cost exceeds a fifth of the product's unit cost, when the design needs to be transparent or extremely thin, or when styles change faster than the tool's life. Divide the tooling cost by the expected three-year volume, add the unit price and compare it with the plastic option; the answer is usually obvious.
