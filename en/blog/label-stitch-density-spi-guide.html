Label and Trim Stitch Density: How SPI Decides If It Survives the Wash

By TAIGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 10, 2026

Stitch density is the number of stitches per inch (SPI) along a seam, and on garment trims it decides something more concrete than appearance: whether the label is still attached after thirty washes. A woven main label, a care label folded into a side seam and an elastic waistband run through the same machine, but they do not need the same SPI. Too few stitches and the label lifts at the corners; too many and the needle perforates the tape until the seam tears along a line of holes.

1. Why SPI matters more than the thread brand

Thread gets the discussion, but SPI is what distributes the load. On 1 cm of seam at 8 SPI you have roughly three stitch points holding the label; at 14 SPI you have five and a half, and the load per stitch falls. The price is perforation: every needle penetration weakens the woven tape a little, and above about 14 SPI on a 20 mm polyester care label the holes start to merge under tension. The practical band for most trims is 10–12 SPI.

2. Typical stitch density by attachment point

Attachment point SPI Stitch type What to write in the spec
Woven main label, neck seam 10–12 Lockstitch 301 1 mm from the edge, backstitch at both ends
Care label, side seam 10–12 Lockstitch or 3-thread overlock Fold tucked in, 2–3 mm fold clearance
Hem on jersey 12–14 Twin-needle coverstitch Must recover stretch, never lockstitch
Elastic waistband 10–12 Zigzag or coverstitch Stitch must stretch with the elastic
Button, loop tape 8–10 Lockstitch plus bar tack Bar tack 8–10 mm, aligned to load direction

Put SPI, seam allowance in mm and thread ticket on the same line. European buyers usually write “10–12 stitches per inch (about 4–4.7 per cm)” because the purchase order travels to a factory working in centimetres: one inch is 25.4 mm, so 10 SPI is 3.94 stitches per cm, not exactly 4. Say which unit you mean and the factory does not have to guess.

3. The three failure modes you will actually see

  • Corner lift: at 8 SPI or below, the corner peels up within ten washes. It reads as poor quality even when the fabric is fine.
  • Needle cutting: above 14 SPI on tight-woven polyester tape, the needle severs warp yarns and the label tears along the stitch line.
  • Puckering: too high an SPI for the thread tension pulls the seam and the label curls over the seam allowance.
  • Abrasion at the fold: a care label folded twice needs 2–3 mm between the fold and the stitch line, or the fold wears through first.

4. Durability is a compliance question, not a preference

In the EU, Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011 requires fibre composition on a label permanently attached to the garment, and care instructions that stay readable for the life of the product. A care label that survives five washes meets the rule on day one and fails it a month later. The General Product Safety Regulation (GPSR, (EU) 2023/988) also requires a responsible person in the EU and traceability information that remains present, so the stitch holding the composition holds your compliance evidence too. In the US, the FTC care labelling rule (16 CFR Part 423) asks for instructions that stay legible for the life of the garment, and AATCC test methods are the usual way to prove it.

5. How to verify it in the sample room

  • Count stitches over 25 mm at three points per seam with a pick glass; record the average, not the best section.
  • Pull test: 5 kg static load for 30 seconds on the label; no stitch rupture and no more than 2 mm seam opening.
  • Wash test: five cycles at 40 °C following EN ISO 6330 or AATCC 135, then re-count SPI and compare legibility with the original.
  • Fold clearance: 2–3 mm between fold and stitch line on double-folded care labels.

6. One line on the purchase order

A specification that survives the factory handover reads like this: “Main label: 10–12 SPI, lockstitch 301, ticket 120 polyester thread, 1 mm from the edge, 2–3 mm fold clearance, 5 kg pull test.” Add the wash reference (EN ISO 6330, 40 °C) and the acceptance rule (no more than 2 mm seam opening). Nothing about SPI is expensive — the difference between 8 and 12 stitches per inch is seconds per hundred pieces — but it is the cheapest way to stop label complaints.

Rule of thumb: when a customer returns a garment because the label came off, the cause is nearly always SPI or fold clearance, not thread. Ask the sewing room for the SPI count before you change thread supplier.

FAQ

Is a higher SPI always stronger? No. Above 14 SPI on woven polyester tape, needle cutting reduces strength faster than extra stitches add it. 10–12 SPI is the sweet spot for most trims.

Does SPI change the price? Marginally. It adds seconds per garment, not material. The real cost driver is whether the factory has to re-set the machine, so keep SPI consistent across a style.

What about stretch fabrics? Use 12–14 SPI with a stitch that stretches — coverstitch or a narrow zigzag — otherwise the seam fails before the label does.
