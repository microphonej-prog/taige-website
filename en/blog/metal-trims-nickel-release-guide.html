Nickel Release in Metal Trims: Choosing Buckles, Rivets and Eyelets

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 18, 2026

Nickel release is the compliance line metal trims most often trip over when exporting to Europe: the rules do not ban nickel, they cap how much nickel a part in prolonged skin contact may release. When metal buckles, rivets, eyelets, tag pins or metal badges fail in bulk testing, the batch usually needs a changed plating process or rework. This guide explains where nickel risk comes from, how to avoid it through material and plating choices, which tests apply, and what to write into the spec sheet.

1. The Rule: Not Whether Nickel Is Present, but How Much It Releases

EU REACH sets a nickel release limit for metal parts in prolonged skin contact — commonly stated as 0.2 µg/cm² per week — with stricter limits for pierced-body parts. The trigger is the part in prolonged contact with skin, not the garment as a whole: exposed buttons, rivets, zip pulls, metal tag pins, eyelets and belt buckles typically count, while metal hidden inside a lining or pocket generally does not. Compliance therefore runs in two steps: decide which parts are in prolonged skin contact, then test release for those parts.

2. Nickel Risk by Metal Trim, and the Alternatives

Part or material Where the risk comes from Common mitigation
Plated zinc alloy (buckles, badges) Nickel in the plating layer, released after wear or sweat corrosion Switch to a nickel-free plating system, or thicken the coating and test after abrasion
Brass parts (rivets, eyelets) Nickel carried in alloy impurities and the surface treatment Specify low-nickel stock, state the plating formula, request test reports
Stainless steel 304 / 316 Contains nickel, but the dense structure keeps release low in most cases A common first choice; still sample and test by batch for the record
Metal tag pins and hooks Direct contact with neck and wrist skin Use nickel-free or tin-plated metal, or switch to plastic or resin tag pins
Plastic or resin alternatives No metal nickel release risk Watch load capacity and weather resistance; best for decorative parts

3. How Testing Works: Method and Timing

The usual route is a third-party lab: sample the finished part and test nickel release (the EN 1811 series), with simulated wear and corrosion pre-treatment (EN 12472) to reproduce a worst case after months of wearing. The test object is the physical part, not a material certificate — which is where many teams slip: the same material with different plating batches can produce different results. Test once at the sampling stage to set a baseline, retest after any process or supplier change, and sample by batch in production.

4. Five Things to Write Into the Spec Sheet

  1. List every part that counts as prolonged skin contact instead of leaving it to the supplier.
  2. Base material grade and plating system, including whether it is nickel-free — not just the colour word silver.
  3. Ask for part-level test reports stating the method, test date and batch traceability.
  4. A change-control clause: any change of base material, plating supplier or process must be notified in advance and retested.
  5. Treat childrenswear as a separate line item: migrated-element and mechanical safety rules are stricter, so design to the strictest destination standard.

5. Four Common Misconceptions

  • Stainless steel is nickel-free: 304 and 316 both contain nickel; release is usually low, but keep test records.
  • Thicker plating is safer: thickness delays wear, but a nickel-containing system still releases once worn — the plating system is what matters.
  • One report is enough forever: a report covers a specific material, process and batch — switching suppliers means retesting.
  • Judging the part without the position: the same buckle on an exposed neckline and hidden inside a lining are judged differently — planning the position removes half the risk.
💡 Tip: nickel release is a finished-part metric, and a material certificate does not replace a part test. If your buyer simply says compliant with REACH, confirm three things: which parts are in scope, which limit applies, and whether abrasion pre-treatment (EN 12472) is required. This article is practical guidance; current regulations, destination rules and your buyer's technical file prevail.
