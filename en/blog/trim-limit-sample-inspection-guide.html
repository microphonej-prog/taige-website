Limit Samples for Trim Inspection: How to Set Visual Defect Standards

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 26, 2026

The argument at trim inspection is rarely about whether a defect exists, but about whether it counts and at what level. A float on a woven label, a slight misregistration on a tag, an uneven coating on a care label: the supplier calls it normal, the brand calls it unacceptable, and the discussion goes in circles. The fix is not a longer standard but a set of limit samples — three physical pieces on the table that both sides judge against. This guide covers how limit samples differ from a golden sample, which trims need them most, how to create them on site, how they combine with AQL sampling, and how to store and use them when a dispute arises.

1. Why a Written Standard Still Leaves Room to Argue

  • Adjectives do not measure: slight, visible and acceptable mean different things to different people. The same float reads as minor to a buyer and fatal to a designer
  • Position is hard to describe: no fraying on the edge — front or back, both sides, within 2 mm or 5 mm of the seam? Without a physical example it is interpretation
  • The viewer's conditions vary: light changes how colour and appearance read. The same comparison can pass under daylight, fail under fluorescent or flip inside a light box, so limit samples must be agreed under the same viewing conditions on both sides

2. Limit Sample, Golden Sample and Reject Sample

Sample Purpose How many
Golden sample The target: colour, dimensions, hand feel and make-up, signed by both sides with a copy each Two to three
Limit sample The worst acceptable level: judged physically, anything worse is rejected and anything better passes Two to three: client, factory and, if needed, a third party
Reject sample A clearly out-of-spec piece: new operators and inspectors see at a glance that this is not acceptable, which text cannot achieve One to two

3. Which Trims Need Them Most

Trim Most-debated defects What the limit sample fixes
Woven labels Float length, skipped picks, edge fraying, letter clarity Millimetres of float allowed, number of skipped picks tolerated, whether fraying sits inside the fold
Hang tags Misregistration, foil flaking, die-cut burrs, crease depth, colour shift Millimetres of offset accepted, maximum flaked area, and the magnification used to judge burrs
Care labels Dotty print, uneven stroke weight, coating bubbles, cut size variation Whether small text is legible at reading distance, bubble size limit, dimensional tolerance
Packaging bags Seal offset, print misalignment, opening tension, thickness feel Seal width and offset limits, print distance from the edge, one piece per thickness

4. How to Build a Usable Set

  • Step one, work from real pieces: do not draft from imagination; ask the factory to lay out first articles, trial pieces and past problem pieces and go through them one by one
  • Step two, pick the worst acceptable piece: that becomes the limit sample. It is the worst piece you would still accept, not the average and not the best
  • Step three, agree and sign on site: brand, buyer and factory QC in the same room, same light, same distance, same magnification, instead of couriering samples and disagreeing by mail
  • Step four, make three copies and seal them: label each with the trim, the defect type, the grade accepted, the date and the signatures, one set per party
  • Step five, put it in the documents: the limit sample number belongs in the spec sheet and the purchase order. Physical samples alone are useless once the account manager or inspector changes

5. Combining Limit Samples with AQL Sampling

The limit sample defines what counts as a defect; the AQL plan defines how much you inspect and how many you tolerate. You need both. An AQL of 2.5 without limit samples means each inspector judges differently; limit samples without a sampling plan leave you inspecting everything. The practical loop is: draw the sample per AQL, grade each piece against the limit sample as critical, major or minor, then apply the accept and reject numbers to release or rework the lot.

  • Write tiers such as zero tolerance for critical, AQL 2.5 for major and AQL 4.0 for minor into the spec sheet, pair them with the limit samples, and judgement stops being a negotiation
  • Reworked lots should be re-judged against the same set by a different inspector, so no one audits their own work

6. Storage, Updates and Disputes

  • Limit samples age: paper yellows, plastics embrittle, fabric gathers dust. Review every 12 months, and remake them immediately when the material, process or supplier changes
  • In a dispute the sealed sample governs: the piece with matching numbers held by each side is the strongest evidence. Photos sent by chat are affected by light and phone processing and only support the case
  • Reference the sample number in the claim clause: one line that judgement follows LS-2026-03 beats three hundred words of description
  • Keep a working set on the inspection bench: limit samples belong next to the QC table, not locked in a drawer; standards that are visible get applied
You can start in three steps: take the trim that generates the most rework or complaints, lay out recent good pieces and borderline pieces, pick the worst one you would still accept, label it, sign it and make three copies. That beats a ten-page specification at ending arguments.
