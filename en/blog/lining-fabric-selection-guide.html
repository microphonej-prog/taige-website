Garment Lining Guide: Taffeta, Acetate, Cupro and Knit Linings

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 4, 2026

The garment lining is the invisible part that decides how a piece of clothing feels the moment you put it on. Sandwiched between the shell fabric and the body, it delivers slip, coverage, structure and protection — a modest share of the cost, yet it drives wearing comfort, post-wash shape and return rates. Choosing the wrong lining usually shows up as heat build-up, static, puckering and shrinkage after washing, or colour transfer onto a dark shell.

This guide covers five parts: what a lining has to do, six common lining fabrics compared, working backwards from the garment type, the specifications to agree before ordering, and how the lining relates to fibre content on the care label — ending with a spec list you can copy straight into a quotation request.

1. Start With What the Lining Has to Do

Choosing a lining is not about finding the cheapest cloth; it starts with deciding which jobs that garment actually asks the lining to do. The four duties carry different weight, and the answer changes accordingly.

  • Slip: a low-friction inner face so the garment slides on and off without clinging — wool coats and knit jackets depend on this most.
  • Coverage: when the shell is light or translucent, the lining does the opacity work, usually in the same shade or one step lighter.
  • Structure: it supports the suit front, the waistband and the coat shoulder so the fabric springs back instead of collapsing.
  • Protection: it cuts friction between the fabric and the body or outerwear, reducing pilling and shine, and keeps skin off coarse seam allowances.

2. Six Common Lining Fabrics Compared

Lining type Handle and sheen Breathability and static After washing Typical use
Polyester taffeta (190T/210T) Smooth, matte, the cost baseline Average, prone to static Low shrinkage, wash-fast, stable Trenchs, jackets, down linings, casual trousers
Nylon taffeta Softer, smoother, better drape Below average, obvious static High strength, creases easily Sports jackets, outdoor, down
Acetate Silk-like, soft sheen Medium Dry clean or cool gentle wash Suits, coats, eveningwear
Cupro Closest to silk, excellent drape Good, skin-friendly Shrinks easily, needs gentle care Premium suits, dresses
Knit lining (warp or weft) Stretch, soft against the skin Good Stretches with the shell Knitwear, yoga and activewear, kidswear
Satin High sheen, decorative Average Snags easily Dresses, eveningwear, decorative panels

The conclusion compresses into one line: where the care method allows it, polyester taffeta is the value baseline; for a premium hand, go acetate or cupro; for stretch and comfort, choose a knit lining. Whichever you pick, the lining and the shell must share the same care level — otherwise the care label cannot be made consistent.

3. Work Backwards From the Garment Type

  • Suits and coats: cupro or acetate in a medium-to-heavy weight; check colour fastness, fraying and press marks.
  • Trenchs, jackets and down: polyester or nylon taffeta; watch shrinkage, static and down-proofing.
  • Dresses and skirts: follow the care level — washable styles take knit or taffeta lining, dry-clean styles can use acetate or satin.
  • Sport and yoga: warp-knit lining with elastane; demand four-way stretch, moisture wicking and colour retention.
  • Kidswear: choose skin-friendly, soft materials, avoid stiff static-prone polyester, and watch fluorescent whitening agents and formaldehyde.

4. Six Specifications to Agree Before Ordering

Complaints about linings are rarely about whether the fabric works — they are about inconsistency between batches. Put these six items in the spec sheet at sampling stage and most of the arguing later simply disappears.

  • Weight and density: confirm both gsm and T (threads per inch); 190T and 210T are the usual dividing lines.
  • Shrinkage: wash alongside the shell — hold woven linings within ±3%, and widen and state the limit for knits.
  • Colour fastness: washing change and staining ≥3-4, rubbing dry and wet ≥3-4; on dark linings watch staining first.
  • Static and handle: if an antistatic finish is used, state how many washes it survives; confirm handle on a physical swatch, not a photo.
  • Fraying and seam slippage: hot-cut, cold-cut and woven edges behave very differently — run a seam-slippage test before sewing.
  • Shade and lot variation: keep one dye lot per order where possible, and compare first and last piece swatches.
💡 Tip: the lining must match the shell's care level. If the shell is washable, the lining cannot be dry-clean-only acetate; conversely, a dry-clean suit lined in washable taffeta still has to carry the dry-clean symbol — the strictest rule wins and the comfort gain is lost.

5. Does the Lining Belong on the Care Label?

Most markets mandate the shell fibre content and do not require the lining to be listed separately. When the lining is a large share of the garment, or the customer is a European brand with strict composition rules, list it alongside. The safer route: set the care symbols by the stricter of shell and lining, and keep the lining spec in the trims sheet — so you never end up with a washable care label and a lining that puckers at the first wash.
