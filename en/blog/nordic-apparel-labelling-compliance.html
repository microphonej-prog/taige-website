Nordic Apparel Labelling: Swedish, Danish, Finnish and Norwegian Text plus the Nordic Swan

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 5, 2026

The four Nordic countries hold fewer than thirty million people between them, yet they are among Europe's highest-ticket apparel markets, with some of the most generous returns policies. Chinese suppliers rarely lose orders here over print quality; the damage comes from three things: consumer information without a local language, packaging never registered under the national producer responsibility scheme, and a customer holding the Nordic Swan Ecolabel while the trims arrive with no chemical declaration. This article separates the three and ends with a pre-shipment checklist.

1. One EU Rule Set, Four Ways of Applying It

Sweden, Denmark and Finland are EU member states, so Regulation 1007/2011 applies directly: statutory fibre names, composition in descending order by weight, non-textile parts listed separately. Norway is not an EU member, only part of the European Economic Area, and textile labelling there is governed by national rules with Norwegian as the statutory language. Treating Norway as an EU country is the most common first mistake on Nordic orders.

The real divergence sits on a second layer: consumer information. The EU regulation governs fibre composition only; the language of everything else is set by national consumer protection law. Sweden, Denmark and Norway each require their own language, while Finland is officially bilingual in Finnish and Swedish, and orders destined for Swedish-speaking retail usually need both. One care label serving four countries rarely carries the right language mix, which is why Nordic orders put so much weight on carton segregation.

💡 Tip: when one shipment covers all four countries, the cheapest route is a care label carrying Finnish and Swedish, hang tags and packaging segregated by country, and adhesive over-labels for the Danish or Norwegian wording. Count the cartons per country first, then decide between over-labelling and separate print runs.

3. Packaging Producer Responsibility: Four Schemes, Four Registrations

The Nordic packaging producer responsibility schemes were among the first in Europe, and each country runs its own: Näringslivets Producentansvar in Sweden, Rinki in Finland, Dansk Producentansvar in Denmark and Grønt Punkt Norge in Norway. Fees follow material and weight, declared separately for paper, plastic and metal, with plastic usually the most expensive per kilo. Registration is not a one-off: new materials or a changed packaging structure mean an updated declaration.

The obligation falls on whoever first places the packaging on that national market, normally the brand or importer. But if you quote DDP straight to a retailer, that step can land on you, and the fee becomes your cost. Put three things in the contract before signing: who registers, whose account declares, and who pays.

  • Material declaration: hang-tag board, poly bags, paper bags and cartons are counted separately; mixed-material packs are declared under the dominant material with a note
  • Weight declaration: unit packaging net weight times quantity, never an estimate from carton gross weight — sampling checks catch it
  • Marking: some retailers require the recycling mark and material code on-pack; confirm the client template before delivery instead of designing your own

4. The Nordic Swan: Not Law, Yet a Gate on Orders

The Nordic Swan is a voluntary ecolabel under the Nordic Council of Ministers, with a dedicated standard for textiles. It sets limits on formaldehyde, azo dyes, alkylphenol ethoxylates (APEO), heavy metals and colour fastness, and requires supply-chain traceability. It is not mandatory law, but several large Nordic retail groups have written it into purchasing policy, which makes it behave like one for Chinese suppliers.

Once a client carries the Swan, trims stop being a matter of looks: inks, adhesives, woven-label yarns and care-label coatings all need chemical declarations and test reports, and material changes must be notified. In practice inks and glues are where orders stall — hang-tag laminating adhesive, self-adhesive label glue and water-based bag glue are the three items where even a composition sheet is often unavailable.

💡 Tip: brands holding the Swan usually keep their trims list as a whitelist. Before switching yarn, ink or adhesive, a supplier should ask one question — does this change need notification? — which costs far less than retrofitting documents or reworking a batch.

5. Seven Checks Before the Goods Leave

Turn these seven points into a one-page checklist filed with the packing list. When a Nordic client files a claim, the first question is how the goods were verified at the time; a signed sheet saves far more effort than an explanation afterwards.

  1. Fibre names in the statutory local language, composition in descending weight order, blend percentages summing to 100
  2. Consumer information in the right national language; for Finland, confirm whether Finnish and Swedish are both needed
  3. Country of origin plus manufacturer or importer name and address, consistent with the entity registered under the packaging EPR scheme
  4. Care symbols follow ISO 3758 and do not contradict the wording — for example a machine-wash symbol next to hand-wash text
  5. Packaging EPR registration number, material and weight declarations confirmed, with the responsible declarant named in the contract
  6. For Swan or other ecolabel orders: chemical declarations and test reports for inks, adhesives and yarns still valid
  7. Cartons per country, over-labelling plan and label language stated carton by carton on the packing list, so the warehouse does not have to guess
