Garment Size Labels: EN 13402 Rules, Size Codes and Conversion

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 14, 2026

A size label is the most-read label on a garment and the one most often filled in from memory. EN 13402 replaced that habit in Europe with a single rule: a size designation describes body dimensions in centimetres, not the measurements of the garment. Keep that distinction and the label becomes a specification you can defend; lose it and every fit complaint starts at the label.

1. What EN 13402 actually standardises

The standard comes in three parts. Part 1 fixes the vocabulary and the size pictogram, a human figure carrying four body positions: height, bust or chest, waist and hip. Part 2 assigns a primary and a secondary dimension to each garment type. Part 3 gives the body measurement tables and the size codes derived from them. None of the three describes ease, fit or garment measurements; together they describe the body a garment is intended for.

2. The control dimension decides the code

Part 2 gives each garment type a control dimension, and the size code is derived from that dimension alone. Adult upper garments follow bust or chest girth; men's trousers follow waist girth; women's trousers and skirts follow hip girth; shirts follow collar size; children's garments follow height. One garment carries several dimensions, but only one decides the code, and the purchase order has to say which.

Garment type Control dimension Example code
Women's tops and dresses Bust girth 38 (bust 88 cm)
Men's jackets and knitwear Chest girth 50 (chest 100 cm)
Men's trousers Waist girth 82
Women's trousers and skirts Hip girth 97
Shirts Collar size 40
Children's garments Height 104

3. One code step is four centimetres

In the adult tables each step in the code equals 4 cm of the control dimension, which is why women's codes run 34, 36, 38 and men's 46, 48, 50. Children's sizes step by 6 cm of height, so 92, 98, 104 follow in order. That arithmetic is what lets two suppliers compare codes at all: if both follow EN 13402, code 40 means the same body. A tag that says only S, M or L states relative size, not body size, and on cross-border orders that gap is one of the commonest causes of a remake.

4. Why conversion charts drift

A conversion chart is commercial convenience, not a standard. UK and US women's codes have no legal basis: the same 38 dress may sell as a 10 in one market and a 6 in another, and one brand can shift its own chart between seasons. Japan works in 号 under JIS L 4005 (size 9 equals an 83 cm bust), Korea in 44, 55, 66 and 77 under KS K 0050 (66 equals a 90 cm bust), China in height plus bust under GB/T 1335 (160/84A). One rule survives all of this: print the original body dimension beside any converted code.

5. Printing a size label that holds up

Woven or printed both work as a material, but the size is read after friction and washing, so a woven label or a heat transfer holds up better; a printed label has to be proven legible after 30 washes. Place it at the centre back neck and keep it separate from the side seam care label: size is selling information, the care label is compliance information, and merging them flattens the wash symbols until nobody can read them. Go by legibility at a glance: cap height under 2 mm disappears on dark fabric.

6. Is the size legally required? Check per market

This differs by market, so settle the destination before quoting. In the EU the textile labelling regulation (EU) 1007/2011 mandates fibre composition only; size designation rests on EN 13402 and on retailer requirements. Japan requires fibre content, care symbols and a labeller's name under the Household Goods Quality Labeling Act, with size bands applied by the trade along JIS L 4005. Korea requires quality marking, including fibre content, care warnings, manufacturer and origin, with sizes following the KS K 0050 series. US federal law has no size statute, but fibre content and care must meet 16 CFR 303.

A purchase order that says size 38 and nothing else has not specified anything. Write the control dimension and its tolerance into the order, agreed per body point (for example ±1 cm at bust and ±0.5 cm at length), otherwise the label and the spec sheet can both be correct and still contradict each other.

7. Six checks before the size labels are ordered

  1. The control dimension and code arithmetic are agreed with the buyer in writing
  2. The original body dimension is printed beside any converted code
  3. Size label and care label are separate, with legible type
  4. A 30-wash legibility test is done, or a sealed reference sample is kept
  5. The destination market's labelling duties are checked: fibre, care, origin, language
  6. Size range and packing ratio match, so no mixed-size mispacking
