Woven Label Production: From Yarn to Finished Label, Quality Is in Every Step

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 10, 2026

A woven label is woven, not printed — miss that and you will never fully understand woven label pricing or quality. From yarn to finished label, production passes through warping, drafting, weaving, cutting, folding and inspection, and every step shapes both cost and quality. Here is the full tour.

Following the process also sharpens inspection: when you know where broken threads, mispicks and frayed edges occur, you check with purpose — and judge quotes more fairly.

1. Before Weaving: Warping & Drafting Set Colour and Precision

A woven label pattern is formed by interlacing warp and weft yarns. Before production, yarns are wound by colour onto a beam (warping), then each warp thread is drawn through heddles and reed (drafting). More colours and wider labels mean longer preparation — one reason small runs cost more per piece.

  • Uneven warping tension later shows up as wrinkling, skewed wefts or shade bars;
  • drafting and the jacquard file must be checked against the artwork point by point — one wrong thread shifts the whole design.

    • 2. Weaving: The Jacquard Loom Builds the Design Thread by Thread

    At weaving, the jacquard lifts warp threads needle by needle from the electronic pattern, wefts are inserted in design order, and the motif literally grows thread by thread. Among common weaves, satin has long floats — the best sheen and hand-feel; plain is the tightest and most durable, ideal for fine text; jacquard renders rich multi-colour artwork. The denser and more complex the design, the slower the weaving and the higher the loom-time cost.

    3. Finishing: Cutting, Folding & Stiffening Define the Final Detail

    The loom delivers a full web of labels that still needs edge cutting (flat, hot or ultrasonic), slitting, folding and stiffening to become neat individual labels. A misplaced fold or a wrong cutting temperature shows up as frayed edges, creases or off-size pieces.

  • Folded labels (fold-over or double-fold) add a step — measure the fold position and finished length piece by piece;
  • stiffening makes labels crisp and easy to sew, but over-stiff labels can feel scratchy next to the skin — match material and finishing to the garment use.

    • 4. Common Defects & Inspection Points

  • Broken threads / mispicks: yarn breaks or lift errors, common in dense or tiny-letter areas — check lettering under a loupe for completeness;
  • Shade variation / barrés: yarn-lot differences or tension drift — compare the batch with the approved sample under standard light;
  • Frayed or fuzzy edges: cutting method or worn blades — check edges for neatness and loose filaments;
  • Size deviation: slitting or folding errors — measure every folded label and spot-check the width of flat-cut ones.

    • 5. Put Your Process Requirements in the RFQ to Cut Sampling Rounds

    Specify all of the following and sampling usually passes in one round: material and weave, finished size and fold, Pantone references or physical swatches, text and fonts, wash-fastness or special finishing needs, and position (neck, side or waist).
