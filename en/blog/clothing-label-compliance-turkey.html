Turkey Clothing Label Requirements: Turkish Text, Fibre Content and TAREKS

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 21, 2026

Turkey's rule for garment labels comes down to one sentence: consumer-facing label information must be in Turkish, and the rules are almost identical to the EU's. Turkey's regulation on labelling textile and leather products mirrors EU 1007/2011 — fibre content, care instructions, origin and the responsible party are all mandatory — and imports pass through the TAREKS risk-inspection system. For a Chinese trims supplier, Turkey means EU logic written in Turkish.

This guide runs through the legal framework, a Turkish terminology table, fibre content and tolerances, care symbols, import inspection and the responsible party, and a pre-sampling checklist you can use as a self-audit.

1. Turkish Labelling Rules Share DNA with EU 1007/2011

Turkey is not an EU member, but it shares a customs union with the EU and has long kept textile labelling aligned with EU rules, issued and enforced by the Ministry of Trade. Four points cover the essentials.

  • Consumer information in Turkish: composition, care, size and the responsible party must be given in Turkish; other languages may be added but never substituted
  • Permanent labelling: composition and care must be attached to the goods — a sewn-in care label — not merely printed on a tag or bag
  • Textiles and leather: the regulation covers both, so leather patches and leather hang tags are in scope too
  • Standards body: the Turkish Standards Institution (TSE) runs the standards; a TSE mark on apparel is generally voluntary unless a buyer or public tender demands it

Once terminology is agreed with the buyer, freeze it in the spec sheet rather than letting each sampling round improvise. Turkish buyers are sensitive to wording, especially bakım talimatları (care instructions) and menşe (origin).

3. Fibre Content: Percentages, Tolerances and Grouping

Fibre content is the first thing Turkish inspectors check. Following the EU approach, list fibres by weight percentage from highest to lowest, using the standard Turkish fibre names — never a vague mixed fibres or other.

  • Tolerances: the manufacturing tolerance between declared and actual composition is normally ±3 % (wider allowances exist for specific cases such as raised or blended fabrics); beyond that the composition is treated as wrong
  • Grouping: fibres present in very small amounts may, where the rules allow, be grouped as diğer lifler (other fibres); the threshold and combined share follow the regulation, so do not simplify on your own
  • Trims and interlinings: whether decorative fibres, interlinings or piping may be excluded depends on whether they are functional parts; when in doubt, declare in full as agreed with the buyer
The classic failure is three versions of the composition: what the fabric mill states, what the hang tag prints and what the care label is sewn with. Inspectors treat the care label as the reference, and the other two as conflicting evidence. One cross-check before shipment is cheaper than explaining later.

4. Care Instructions: Symbols Plus Turkish Text

Turkey follows the EU care-symbol system (the TS EN ISO 3758 line), with a fixed order: washing, bleaching, drying, ironing, professional care. Unlike purely symbol-based markets, Turkish retail habit expects Turkish wording alongside the symbols, especially for mass-market brands.

  • Temperature and wash method must match what the fabric really tolerates: declare 30 °C and the fabric had better not shrink
  • Symbols such as do not wash or dry clean only must be backed by a wash test or the mill's guidance, not added for a premium look
  • Symbol sets should be complete: on mixed-material garments, such as cotton with leather trims, state care per material so one set does not mislead

5. Import Inspection and the Responsible Party

Textile imports into Turkey are assessed through the Ministry of Trade's risk-management system (TAREKS), and some categories need extra surveillance or conformity documents. Labels are the most visible thing checked on site — and the most frequent finding.

  • A Turkish entity as responsible party: the importer name and address on the label should be a Turkish-registered company; never fill it in yourself — get it confirmed in writing
  • Consequences: non-compliant labels can bring administrative fines, mandatory relabelling or a sales ban; surveillance is run by the Ministry of Trade and its provincial offices
  • Label at origin: sewing labels at the factory beats rework after arrival; local relabelling labour and storage usually cost more than the labels themselves

6. Six-Point Checklist Before Sampling

  • Has the Turkish been proofed by a native speaker, with standard Turkish fibre names
  • Do the percentages total 100 %, and is the tolerance wording confirmed in writing
  • Do symbols and Turkish text agree, and do temperature and wash method match the fabric
  • Does the menşe wording match the customs documents
  • Is the importer a Turkish entity, with name and address confirmed in writing by the buyer
  • Can the care-label width and type size hold all the Turkish — it is long and the usual victim of overflow
This article reflects export practice; Turkish labelling rules and import surveillance are revised from time to time. Before production, confirm against the current regulation and the buyer's import requirements.
