Apparel Gift Box Packaging: Rigid, Magnetic and Drawer Boxes Explained

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 20, 2026

A garment gift box — a rigid box built from 1.5–3 mm greyboard wrapped in printed paper — is what apparel brands use for gifting, VIP orders, collaborations, boxed sets (shirt plus tie, coat plus scarf) and seasonal editions. It does not replace poly bags or paper bags: bags handle transit and display, a rigid box handles the moment the customer opens it.

Rigid boxes are also the category where structure drives cost most visibly: at the same footprint a two-piece lid-and-base box and a magnetic flip box can differ by more than half, and upgrading the insert from folded card to EVA adds another step. This guide covers when a rigid box is worth it, the three structures, inserts, wrapping and finishing, sizing maths, cost and shipping — with a checklist to paste into your enquiry email.

1. First Decide: Does This Garment Need a Rigid Box?

Three tests: retail price (usually only worthwhile above the equivalent of a few hundred RMB), channel (gifting, premium e-commerce, corporate orders, concept stores) and whether the unboxing itself carries the message — a rigid box with a proper insert makes an ordinary shirt read like a gift set. For pure transit protection, full-container export or promo lines, a poly bag or paper bag costs far less and nobody notices the box.

3. Inserts: Hold It, Shape It, Hide the Board

An insert does three jobs: it holds the garment still, gives the box volume, and hides the raw greyboard. Four common routes: folded card trays are the cheapest and suit folded shirts and tees; EVA or foam lined with fabric feels soft and substantial, good for accessories and structured pieces; flocked vacuum-formed trays hold irregular items such as buckles and brooches most precisely; ribbon or paper straps across the garment cost least but protect least and can leave creases after transit.

Always leave 3–5 mm of fitting clearance in a garment insert, or the piece will not go in — or will not come out. Sample with the actual garment inside, never with an empty box.

4. Wrapping: What Decides How Expensive It Looks

Roughly eighty percent of a rigid box's perceived quality comes from the wrapping paper, not the printing. Coated paper with a matt lamination is the most economical route; soft-touch film — matt, silky, fingerprint-resistant — is what premium apparel brands specify today; speciality stocks such as cotton, earth or stardust papers bring texture without printing and suit tonal, understated looks; fabric wraps (linen, velvet, faux leather) cost the most and stand out the most.

On print, hot foil, embossing, spot UV and screen printing can be combined, but each pass adds a machine set-up and spoilage. Keep it to two effects per panel and spend the budget on wrapping and inserts instead: those two are felt the moment a customer picks the box up, while one extra foil block is barely registered.

5. Sizing: Work Back from the Garment

Never order from the outer dimension first. The right sequence: measure the folded garment at its longest, widest and thickest points, add the insert thickness, add 3–5 mm of clearance — that is your internal dimension. Add two board thicknesses plus the wrap, and you have the external size and the carton plan. The classic mistake is setting the outer size from the garment: the piece arrives squeezed and deformed, or loose enough to shift in transit.

6. Cost, MOQ and Shipping

A rigid box is priced as board plus wrap, plus insert, plus finishing, plus hand assembly. Magnetic and drawer boxes are largely assembled by hand, so labour makes up a much larger share than on hang tags or paper bags. Minimum order quantity follows the smallest economical cut for the box shape and insert — commonly 500–1,000 pieces per design — and a custom formed insert means a one-off tooling cost.

Rigid boxes are bulky and crush-sensitive, so exports usually follow one of two models: assembled and cased at the factory, ready to use on arrival but costing more freight and container space; or shipped flat with the inserts packed separately and assembled locally, saving roughly thirty to fifty percent of volume at the cost of local labour and storage. Work out the per-unit volume and container count before negotiating the unit price.

💡 Tip: always sample a rigid box in the flesh and run a fit test with the real garment inside — insert it, take it out, close the lid, shake it, then drop-test the export carton and reopen. Tolerance problems never show up on screen or on a flat proof.
