QR, NFC or RFID: Choosing the Data Carrier for an Apparel Tag

By TAIGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 11, 2026

QR, NFC and RFID are usually presented as steps on one upgrade path. They are three different tools. A QR code is printed, costs almost nothing extra, and needs someone to deliberately scan it. NFC responds only within about four centimetres. RFID reads a whole carton from one to eight metres. Choosing wrong is expensive: printing QR codes and expecting warehouse counting, or buying RFID inlays and expecting customers to scan them with a phone, are both mistakes discovered after mass production — when the labels are already sewn into the garments.

Start with the reader, not the technology

Carrier Read distance Who can read Typical use
QR code 10–50 cm, needs framing Any smartphone, no app Product page, care info, passport link
NFC 0–4 cm, tap to read Phones with NFC Authentication, membership, one-tap reorder
UHF RFID 1–8 m, whole carton Handheld readers, store gates Receiving, stock counts, anti-theft

What each carrier adds to the unit cost

  • QR code: ink and space only. Adding one 15 × 15 mm code to a tag you already print barely moves the unit price; the real cost sits in the landing page and its upkeep.
  • NFC: an inlay plus a laminating or sewing step. At 3,000–5,000 pieces it typically runs 10–25× a printed QR of the same area; below that the multiple grows fast.
  • UHF RFID: an inlay with an antenna, roughly 5–15× a printed QR at the same volume, plus encoding and per-piece read verification labour.
  • Do not forget the hidden cost: a bad encoding batch or a read rate below target means rework on the whole lot, and reconciling hang tag quantities against care label quantities costs labour. These usually exceed the carrier price itself.

What each one actually enables

  • QR: one code, many destinations — product page, fibre content, a care video, a feedback form, passport data. The weakness is that it needs a deliberate scan; in practice scan rates land in the single digits to around twenty percent.
  • NFC: tap and open, no framing, no app; tamper evidence and one-time write are possible. Good for premium and limited lines, membership and reorder. Limits: four centimetres and device coverage.
  • RFID: a whole carton in one pass, with stock accuracy of 95–99 %. Fits multi-store, omnichannel operations that count often. It adds little to the individual customer's experience.

The Digital Product Passport pushes QR toward the default

The EU Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR, EU 2024/1781) sets up the Digital Product Passport framework: every product carries a unique identifier reachable through a data carrier. The textiles delegated act and its application dates are still moving, so the question is not whether to prepare but which carrier to reserve first. For cross-border apparel, QR is the only option that needs no dedicated reader and works for customs and customers alike. NFC gives a better consumer moment but requires contact. RFID's cost and reader investment make it an operations tool. In practice, the calm answer is to put both on one tag: QR outward, RFID or NFC inward.

What each market actually requires of the carrier

In every market the legal marking has to stay physical, readable and durable; a digital carrier is an addition, not a substitute. The EU asks for manufacturer and EU responsible-person traceability under GPSR (EU 2023/988), fibre composition under (EU) No 1007/2011 and care symbols per EN ISO 3758, with several member states requiring the local language. Japan requires composition, care and country of origin in Japanese under the Household Goods Quality Labelling Act. Korea requires care marking in Korean under its quality and safety management act. Brazil's CDC (Lei nº 8.078/1990) requires Portuguese wording and an identified responsible party. Plan the carrier around the printed block, not instead of it.

Washing, metal and dye: what survives

  • An RFID inlay sewn into a care label must be wash-tested: run the conditions behind the EN ISO 3758 care symbols and measure read rate after 25 and 50 cycles, not just whether it looks intact.
  • Metal buttons, zips and sequins detune the antenna: keep at least 5 mm between antenna and metal, or read range can halve.
  • Dark dye lots and softener residue affect bond strength; an NFC inlay on heavy dark fabric tends to lift after washing, so wash a prototype before approving.
  • QR asks least: enough contrast, modules no smaller than 0.4 mm, a 3 mm quiet zone, and a matte or soft-touch surface scans better than gloss.

A decision table

If you need Choose Why
Compliance info, passport link, content marketing QR No real added cost, readable by any phone
Premium feel, authentication, membership NFC, ideally with QR Opens on contact and can be tamper-evident
Multi-store counts, omnichannel, receiving UHF RFID Carton-level reads at 95–99 % accuracy
Tight budget, customer lookup only QR, skip the chip The risk of reprinting thousands outweighs the chip premium
Practical advice: decide the data destination before the carrier. Plenty of projects buy NFC or RFID and then stall on where the tap should land and who maintains that page. A scan that opens a homepage is worse than no code at all.
