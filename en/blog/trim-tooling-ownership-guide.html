Trim Tooling Ownership: Dies, Plates, Fees and Transfers

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 26, 2026

The most overlooked line in a trims order is tooling: die-cutting dies, foil stamping dies, embossing dies and printing plates for hang tags, jacquard files for woven labels, and print cylinders for poly bags. They are not the product, yet they decide whether you can change supplier, what switching costs, and whether a reorder two years later needs new tooling. This guide covers the types of tooling, the three common ways plate fees are charged, who actually owns them, how a factory stores them, what can and cannot travel to a new supplier, and the eight things to confirm before you order.

1. Which Tooling Exists in a Trims Order

The same two words — plate fee — can describe completely different things: a one-off consumable, or an asset reused across orders for years. Align on the category before comparing quotes, otherwise you compare A's tooling cost with B's and the conclusion is meaningless.

Category Typical tooling Reusable across orders
Hang tags Cutting die, foil die, embossing die, screen Yes, for years if well stored
Woven labels Jacquard patterning file, cutting die Reusable on the same machine, often not across mills
Poly bags Print cylinder, sealing blade Tied to web width and machine; usually not transferable
Paper bags and boxes Cutting die, printing plate, creasing matrix Yes; cylinders depend on web width

2. The Three Ways Tooling Fees Are Charged

The tooling fee itself is rarely the problem; the rules around it are. The same quote can mean a fee that is never refunded or one credited back in full at 50,000 pieces. What matters in negotiation is not the amount but the refund condition and the ownership wording.

Model How it works Best for and watch-outs
Listed separately, non-refundable Charged once on the first order, not again on reorders Unusual shapes, high-value items; confirm reorders are truly free
Refund at volume Paid upfront and credited once cumulative volume is reached, often 30,000 to 100,000 pieces Brands with steady reorders; put volume and validity period in writing
Amortised into unit price No separate fee; absorbed into the first-order unit price Small runs, simpler paperwork; harder to benchmark when re-tooling elsewhere

3. Ownership: Who Pays, Who Owns

  • If the customer paid for the tooling, the physical tooling normally belongs to the customer and the factory merely stores it. State that in the quotation email rather than relying on an oral understanding: two years later it is the sentence that decides whether you pay again
  • Common factory tooling is neither charged nor transferred: standard radius dies, regular size dies and generic sealing blades are the factory's own assets. If such a fee is quoted separately, ask why
  • The designer's source file is not the tooling: an AI or PDF file is artwork, while the die line, bleed and foil artwork are separate files worth requesting at sampling stage

4. Storage: Register, Shelf Life and Wear

A careful factory numbers customer tooling, logs it, stores it separately and prints the tooling number on orders and packing lists so a reorder can pull the same die. A factory that stacks tooling unlabelled will 'lose' it, and the customer ends up paying again.

  • Retention: customer-specific tooling is usually kept two years, some factories three; beyond that, or when the client confirms no further orders, it may be scrapped. The proper practice is written notice 15 to 30 days before disposal
  • Normal wear is the factory's cost: a dulled die, a worn foil die or an aged cylinder coating. Redoing tooling because the artwork or dimensions changed is normally the client's cost
  • Storage conditions matter: dies should stand upright away from damp and rust, foil dies must not be knocked out of shape, and cylinders need wrapping. Two years in a humid warehouse and a die can come out rusty and unusable

5. Moving Suppliers: What Travels, What Does Not

The most valuable asset when switching supplier is usually not the physical tooling but the electronic files: die lines, artwork with bleed, foil artwork, the jacquard file for woven labels, the print layout for bags. Those let a new supplier start without a development fee. Whether the physical tooling still works depends on the machinery.

  • Usually transferable: hang tag dies, foil dies, embossing dies and paper bag dies, provided the new factory's machine and format match
  • Often not transferable: jacquard tooling for woven labels (cylinders are brand and width specific) and poly bag print cylinders (tied to web width and sealing line). These are usually scrapped; transfer the electronic file instead
  • The release process: settle outstanding invoices, confirm release in writing, check the tooling out of the store, pack in a crate or on a pallet, then ship or collect. Freight and damage liability should be agreed upfront: a crushed die or a dented foil die is scrap
  • Re-confirm after the move: reusing the tooling does not mean reusing the quality. Paper, ink, pressure and equipment differ, so sample the first batch before bulk

6. Pre-Order Checklist

  • List tooling separately in the quote and name every item included (die, foil, emboss, jacquard) to avoid later add-ons
  • Write the refund condition: which volume triggers it, how long it stays valid, and whether it is credited or refunded
  • State ownership: where the client pays, the tooling belongs to the client, is stored by the factory, and note the retention period
  • Ask for the tooling number on every later order and packing list so a reorder uses the same tooling and avoids drift
  • Request file backups in parallel: die line, artwork with bleed, foil artwork, jacquard file — keep them in your own trim archive
  • Agree who pays for re-tooling: design changes are the client's cost, normal wear is the factory's
  • If a future switch is possible, ask early which physical tooling is transferable and roughly what return freight costs, rather than discovering it cannot move
Ask about rules, not just the price: how much only covers this order; what is included, the refund volume, the ownership wording, whether it survives two years and whether it can move decide your cost for the next three years. Save those questions as a mail template and send them with every enquiry.
