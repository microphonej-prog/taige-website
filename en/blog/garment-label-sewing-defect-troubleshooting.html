Label Sewing Defects: Causes and Fixes for Wrinkling, Skew and Curling

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 25, 2026

Wrinkled labels, skewed rows, corners lifting off, labels arching after the first wash — nine times out of ten these are not the label supplier's fault but a mismatch between label specification and the sewing process. The same woven label lies flat on fine jersey and lifts off a heavy hoodie; the same care label behaves under a lockstitch and rolls into a tube under an overlock. This guide takes each defect in turn, gives the cause and the fix, and closes with acceptance criteria you can paste into a tech pack.

The conclusion first: fix the sewing method and fold before you fix label size and substrate. Do it the other way round and you will rework at sample stage.

2. Shrinkage: The Most Overlooked Variable

Arching is fundamentally a shrinkage mismatch. A cotton woven label shrinks 2 to 4 percent in the wash, a polyester satin hardly at all, while a 100 percent cotton jersey can shrink more than 5 percent; sewn together, the label arches by the third wash. The answer is not a more expensive substrate but asking the label plant for the shrinkage of that specific lot, then choosing the substrate against the fabric: stretchy-shrinking fabric gets polyester or blended labels, only stable fabric justifies all-cotton.

  • Run a co-wash at sampling: fabric and label washed together five times, with the water temperature, drying and machine cycle the end customer actually uses.
  • Measure label length after washing; above 1.5 percent, change substrate or sewing method. Ironing will not fix it.
  • Stretch fabric needs slack: do not pull the label seam tight; anchor both ends and leave the middle slightly loose.

3. Fold and Seam Allowance Belong in the Tech Pack

Two words, main label, mean four different things once you choose half fold, centre fold, three fold or a double-folded selvedge: seam allowance, topstitch position and flat dimensions all change. Factories work from habit, which is fine until the style changes and the error hides where nobody looks — fold the wrong way and the copy faces inward. Put four things in the tech pack: fold type, visible finished size, seam allowance, and topstitch spec — face or blind, stitch length, thread colour.

  • Half fold, folded at the centre: 10 mm seam allowance, topstitched both sides. The most common and the easiest to skew, so it needs location marks.
  • Centre fold, folding top to bottom: watch the fold direction against the face of the copy — folding the wrong way hides the text.
  • Three fold: right for care labels with a lot of content, 12 mm seam allowance, and watch how the three panels divide and how thick the folded stack gets.
  • Double-folded selvedge: sewn without folding, the neatest edge, and the highest cost and minimum, so it suits premium and skin-contact styles.

4. Four Lines That Prevent the Problem

Being specific beats changing suppliers. Put these four items in the enquiry or order and most defects disappear before production.

  • Fabric composition and weight, and whether it stretches — this decides substrate softness and shrink matching.
  • The customer's wash method and pass criteria — water temperature, tumble drying, and after how many washes the label is judged.
  • Sewing method, fold, seam allowance and topstitch spec — never say the same as last time; that is where change orders go wrong.
  • Acceptance criteria — what defect rate is allowed, which AQL to sample at, and which defects are an automatic reject.

5. How to Write the Acceptance Criteria

Grade label defects by impact. Anything that affects wearing is a major defect — irritation, loose stitching, arching after wash — at AQL 1.0 to 1.5. Appearance-only issues such as slight skew or an uneven edge are minor at AQL 2.5 to 4.0. Take 20 pieces per lot for a co-wash test; arching after wash rejects the whole lot.

Also run a 50 to 100 piece trial sewing before bulk: fixing a label after a trial is far cheaper than fixing garments. Wash the trial pieces the way the customer will before judging them — many defects only appear after the first wash.
