Packaging Cost per Order: Six Lines and One Number You Can Defend

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 19, 2026

Brands usually know what a single paper bag costs, yet cannot answer what one shipped order costs to pack. Six cost lines sit in between: the primary pack, the void fill, the closure, the labels and stickers, the labour minutes, and the damage and repack rate. Miss the last three and packaging is understated by at least 30%, which means the pricing model treats it as free.

1. Unit Price Is Not Cost

A unit price answers how much one piece costs; it does not answer how many pieces go into one order, who packs it, or what happens when it splits. At the same bag price, brand A uses one per order while brand B double-bags multi-item orders and adds a thank-you card, so the real cost per order can differ by a factor of two. The unit to model is the order, not the piece.

2. The Six Cost Lines and How Each Is Measured

Cost line How to measure Typical share
Primary pack (bag, box) Unit price × pieces per order 40–60%
Void fill and inserts Unit price × quantity 10–20%
Closure and tape Unit price × quantity 5–10%
Labels and stickers Unit price × quantity 5–10%
Labour minutes Seconds per order ÷ 3600 × hourly rate 15–25%
Damage and repack Last month's repack rate × cost per order 2–8%

Add the six lines and you have the cost per order. Divide that by average order value to get the packaging ratio; most apparel e-commerce sits between 2% and 5%, and above 6% it is worth a full specification review rather than more price comparison on the bag itself.

3. Labour Minutes: the Line Nobody Quotes

Time it with a stopwatch, not with impressions. Take 20 real orders and clock each one from pick complete to box sealed and label applied, then use the median rather than the mean, because the mean gets dragged up by whoever is new. Single-item orders commonly land between 60 and 120 seconds, folding, bagging, labelling and weighing included.

Then convert to money: seconds ÷ 3600 × hourly labour cost. This step is usually skipped because it is not a line on the purchase order, yet it is often a fifth of the cost per order, which makes it the easiest item to improve and the easiest to overlook.

4. Dimensions Decide Freight, and Freight Often Exceeds the Pack

Couriers bill the greater of actual weight and dimensional weight. Dimensional weight is length × width × height in cm divided by a divisor: 6000 is common in domestic express and 5000 in international air. A mailer measuring 35 × 25 × 5 cm works out at roughly 0.73 kg, so an order weighing 0.5 kg is billed at 0.73 kg.

So a cheaper bag is not automatically a saving. Shrinking a dimension by 2 cm can drop dimensional weight into the next bracket, and the freight saved dwarfs the bag difference. The reverse is also true: compressing a garment to save a few cents on a bag creates creases, and one return wipes out the saving from dozens of orders.

5. Three Ways to Cut Cost Without Cutting Protection

  • Standardise specifications: collapse a dozen bag formats into five, which raises batch size and in-stock rate and stops the packing line memorising ten folding routines.
  • Share artwork and tooling: let the tag, the sticker and the insert card share one set of dimensions, and changeover downtime drops by half.
  • Re-lay the packing station: tape, stickers and void fill within arm's reach on one side saves 5–10 seconds per order, which moves the number more directly than switching suppliers.

6. Where Not to Save

Under-protection costs returns rather than packaging: damage, moisture and crush marks produce two-way freight, repacking, and a shop rating that slides. In Europe, add packaging law to the model — Regulation (EU) 2025/40, the PPWR, and national producer responsibility registration fees turn into a fixed cost per order, so recycled content ratios and registration fees belong in the same table.

Nor should unsubstantiated green claims go on the pack. Recyclable and biodegradable both need material evidence and certificates behind them; France applies the Code de la consommation to environmental claims, Spain applies unfair competition law, Japan's Act against Unjustifiable Premiums and Misleading Representations covers eco wording on packaging, and Korea applies its Act on Fair Labelling and Advertising. Getting it wrong costs far more than not saying it.

A table you can defend looks like this: six cost lines, one line for the total per order, one line for that total as a share of order value. Build it once and packaging stops being an estimate and becomes a number design, logistics and finance can argue about together.
