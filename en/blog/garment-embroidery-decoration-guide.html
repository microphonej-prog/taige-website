Garment Embroidery Guide: Flat, Chenille, 3D Puff and Appliqué

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 13, 2026

Garment embroidery stitches the brand mark and the decoration straight onto the fabric: the chest logo on a polo, the big graphic on a sweatshirt front, the small mark on a jeans back pocket, the sleeve badge on a down jacket. It reads more three-dimensionally than a woven label and survives washing better than print — but stitch count drives both cost and lead time, and the demands on fabric, backing and care conditions are stricter: the same artwork can behave completely differently on another fabric or with another backing.

This guide has five parts: it draws the boundary between embroidery, woven labels and print, compares five mainstream embroidery techniques, covers the three invisible trims behind any embroidery (thread, backing and bobbin), explains how embroidery works with care labels, hang tags and packing, and closes with six checks for sampling and acceptance.

1. Embroidery, Woven Labels and Print: Drawing the Boundaries

The three are constantly lumped together, yet their pricing logic, visual character and durability are three completely different routes.

  • Woven labels: woven first, then cut — graphics and lettering form in the loom in one pass. Washable, colour-controllable and the easiest to cost and to plan for MOQ; the limits are the number of colours, coarser detail and a flatter look.
  • Print: screen or digital, ideal for large colour blocks and gradients, with almost unlimited colours and a unit cost that follows the printed area; the weak points are cracking and dulling after washing, and printing a light colour on dark fabric needs a base layer.
  • Embroidery: thread builds real relief on the fabric, giving the best colour fastness and durability and the strongest perceived quality; the trade-offs are that stitch count sets the price, tiny lettering and continuous gradients are hard to render, and the reverse side needs thread ends and hard edges dealt with.
💡 Tip: the same brand mark is quoted on completely different bases — woven by the thousand pieces plus width, print by area plus number of colours, embroidery by thousand stitches plus colour count. Leave the basis unstated and you will argue about unit price later.

2. Choosing Among Five Mainstream Embroidery Techniques

TechniqueCharacteristicsTypical Use
Flat embroideryEven stitching, colours added by simply changing thread, capable of small lettering and fine lines; cost follows stitch countPolo chest logos, shirt cuff marks, jeans back pockets
Chenille / loop embroideryA thick thread forms loops on the surface, giving the best relief and hand feel; thicker build, unsuited to fine letteringLarge sweatshirt front graphics, kidswear motifs
3D puff embroideryA foam layer under the stitching expands into a 3D effect with strong impact; demanding on backing and pressingCap fronts, sportswear
Appliqué embroideryA pre-cut patch is fixed first and stitched around its edge, trading colour blocks for efficiency and enabling large multi-colour motifsSchool uniforms, workwear, home textiles
Sequin and cord embroiderySequins or cord are fed into the embroidery for strong decoration; how well they hold depends on the fixing method, so a wash test is compulsoryEveningwear, dancewear, fashion

3. Three Invisible Trims: Thread, Backing and Bobbin

  • Embroidery thread: rayon has the best lustre but low wet strength, polyester is the most stable in strength and colour fastness, and cotton suits vintage or natural looks. For export orders and dark motifs, prefer polyester or a polyester-wrapped core.
  • Backing: thin fabric must be backed or dense needle holes will perforate it; water-soluble film suits tulle and the openwork look on towelling, tear-away paper suits standard wovens, and non-woven backing suits knits and stretch fabric.
  • Bobbin thread: its colour should sit close to the face colour, otherwise a different shade shows when the embroidery edge lifts; elastic fabric needs stretch bobbin thread, since a standard bobbin thread tends to snap after washing.
  • Reverse finishing: any area touching skin needs a soft cover backing over the thread ends, otherwise it itches while worn; on childrenswear the reverse must carry no hard edge and no loose threads — these two are the usual audit targets.

4. How Embroidery Works with Care Labels, Hang Tags and Packing

  • Keep care advice honest: pressing temperature drops once a garment is embroidered, so the iron symbol and temperature limit on the care label must match what the embroidery thread and backing can actually take.
  • Match shrinkage: cotton and viscose shift after embroidery and washing, so confirm finished size and embroidery placement in the pre-shrunk state, otherwise labels and motifs drift after the first wash.
  • No clashing positions: chest, sleeve and back-neck marks must not overlap the same stitching; allow 5–8 mm of visual breathing space between the embroidered area and the size label or neck label, especially behind strong light-dark contrasts.
  • Packing against crush and snag: embroidery both crushes and snags — separate pieces with a card or dust bag, keep the tag string off the embroidery, and add a sheet of tissue inside gift boxes for cushioning.
💡 Tip: most complaints of embroidery puckering or stiffening after washing do not come from the machine but from water-soluble film or tear-away paper left behind, or a backing that shrinks differently from the shell. One full wash test at the garment's care level before bulk is the cheapest insurance you can buy.

5. Sampling and Acceptance: Six Checks on Embroidered Work

  • Stitch and colour count: must match the approved sample; if stitch count exceeds the agreed figure, confirm price and lead time up front rather than discovering the change in bulk.
  • Placement and size: mark the motif centre on a drawing or photo; placement tolerance usually sits within ±3 mm, tightened to ±1.5 mm on small motifs.
  • Thread colour: compare against the approved swatch under a colour-matching light box, confirm no shade drift between two runs of the same motif, and keep one thread batch per order where possible.
  • Wash and rub: run wet and dry rubbing plus a wash cycle at the garment's care level, checking for fuzzing, thread breakage, colour loss and backing residue.
  • Reverse and hand feel: no hard edges or loose threads where the piece touches skin, and no scratching when rubbed with the back of the hand; grade softness acceptance by product category.
  • Small-part safety: sequins, cord and appliqué decoration need tension testing to confirm they stay on after washing; detachable small parts on childrenswear are the highest-risk item.
