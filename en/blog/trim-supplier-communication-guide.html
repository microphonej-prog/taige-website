Trims Communication Guide: Ask Once, Chase Delivery, Report Quality

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 29, 2026

Trims are cheap and small, so most confirmations happen in a chat message or a phone call: make it like last time, the colour is close enough, as early as possible. In each side's head those words mean two different things — and the mismatch only shows up when the bulk arrives with the wrong shade, an off-centre hole or a late date. This article covers four things: which confirmations must be written, how to ask everything once, how to write a chase-up and a quality complaint that actually works, and how to cut ambiguity across languages and time zones.

1. Three Confirmations That Must Be in Writing

  • Colour: shade reference, tolerance and viewing light must be written down. Close to navy is not a standard; a Pantone reference plus a ΔE tolerance is.
  • Size and placement: millimetres, angles, hole positions and stitch lines all need numbers, ideally on an annotated drawing — never leave it to a default assumption.
  • Lead time and batches: quantity, tolerance, latest arrival date and shipping mode belong in an email — a verbal next week is not a delivery date.

2. Ask Everything Once: Turn the Enquiry into a Checklist

One complete enquiry email usually saves three rounds of back and forth. These six items are the ones most often missing — and the ones that cause rework:

ItemWhat goes wrongHow to write it
Use and styleThe supplier quotes a generic item and you rework after approvalBrand, style number and garment category (outerwear, kidswear, swimwear)
Size and toleranceOnly length and width are given, so hole and fold positions follow habitMillimetres, tolerance (for example ±0.5mm) and a drawing reference
Material and processKraft paper alone can mean a quote that differs by doublePaper type, grammage and finishing (for example 300g coated one side, matt film, spot UV)
Quantity and wasteA handful short stops the line, and emergency top-ups are costly and slowNet requirement, waste percentage and delivery quantity tolerance
Date and destinationAs soon as possible ends up last in the production queueLatest arrival date, delivery address and shipping mode (courier, air, sea)
Compliance and documentsLanguage or claims only checked at destination, so the batch is reworkedDestination country, label languages, certification claims and whether test reports are needed

3. Annotations and Versions: Make Revisions Executable

  • One annotation, one point: box the area in red and state what it must become — not it feels cold or too busy, which nobody can produce.
  • Version and date in the filename: for example HT-9054-BLK-v03_20260929.pdf, and keep order and style numbers in the subject line so history is searchable.
  • More than three changes means a redraw: stacking annotations across three rounds invites the which-version question; a clean redraw is faster to approve.
  • Send a written recap after every call: following our call, please confirm these three points — a quotable sentence beats a shared memory.

4. Chasing Delivery: Three Lines Beat Daily Nagging

An effective chase-up email has three moves: facts, impact, request. Strip out the emotion and keep what can be acted on.

  • Facts: order number, original date, current status and today's date — only what can be verified.
  • Impact: our line date, the extra cost of switching to air freight, the store delivery window. Quantify the consequence so the priority is obvious.
  • Request: ask for a concrete answer — a new date and a deliverable quantity — confirmed in writing, and agree progress checkpoints at T-7 and T-3 so chasing becomes reporting.

5. Quality Complaints: Evidence, Scope, Request

  • Evidence: overview photo, close-up and a short video, with batch or carton number visible along with inspected and defective counts.
  • Scope: say whether it was a full or sample inspection, which batches and how many pieces — so the supplier does not assume the whole order is scrap.
  • Request: sorting, rework, replacement, credit or return — pick one or two workable options and state the reply and completion deadlines.
  • Keep the record: file the email and photos — a chat message disappears, but this record becomes the basis for any claim, deduction or negotiation.
💡 One rule of thumb: anywhere a default assumption could creep in, write the confirmation as an explicit sentence. Nine out of ten trim disputes are not caused by bad faith — they are caused by two sides defaulting to different things.

6. Across Languages and Time Zones: Minimise Ambiguity

  • Fix units and terms up front: millimetres against inches, the Chinese wire gauge at 0.01mm against microns, yards against metres, grammage — agree the convention in the first email and share a glossary.
  • Short numbered lines: write points as 1, 2, 3, one idea each, with a picture attached — long paragraphs lose the most in translation.
  • Agree response times: acknowledgement within 24 hours, an answer within 48 for example. Across time zones, send questions a day early so the other side gets a full working day.
  • Plan around long holidays: lock lead times and approval points three to four weeks before Chinese New Year; emails sent during the break only add anxiety.
