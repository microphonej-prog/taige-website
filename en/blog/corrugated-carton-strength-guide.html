Corrugated Carton Selection Guide: Choosing Wall Grade and Strength

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 10, 2026

The shipping carton is the cheapest part of trims packaging and the easiest to get wrong: choose too light a board and thirty-odd days at sea with humidity and stacking will collapse the bottom cartons, so bent hang tags, curled woven labels and dented paper bags all show up the moment the box is opened. Choose too heavy a board and every carton costs a few cents more while the container gains hundreds of kilos of chargeable volume. Selecting corrugated cartons is not about how many layers — it means calculating carton weight, stacking height and transport environment together.

1. Triple Wall, Double Wall or Single Wall: Start with Carton Weight and Stacking Height

StructureCommon flutesTypical thicknessTypical use
Single wallB, C or E flute2.5–4.5 mmHang tags, care labels and other small items, 5–15 kg per carton
Double wallAB, BC or BE flute6–8 mmPaper bags, poly bags and kit trims; preferred from 10–20 kg per carton or when a printed face is needed
Triple wallAAA or ABA12 mm and upHeavy cargo, long storage or repeated handling

E flute is thin and flat with the best print definition, a good fit for cartons carrying hang tags where the front panel matters; B flute balances compression strength better and is the most common choice for trims cartons.

2. Do Not Just Say Extra Strong Five Ply: Align on Strength Numbers

Notations such as K=K, K=A and A=B describe the liner and medium combination, but real strength varies widely between mills — the same notation can differ by more than 20 percent. A more reliable approach is to specify burst strength and edge crush strength directly, and ask the supplier for a board test report.

  • Liner grade and grammage set burst strength: heavier liner resists puncture better and costs more
  • The medium sets edge crush strength, which governs compression and stacking — the most overlooked parameter
  • Large print coverage, hand holes, die cuts and slots cut strength by 10–20 percent, so size the board on the reduced figure

3. Stacking Strength Is Not Carton Strength

  • Humidity is the biggest killer: at roughly 90 percent relative humidity a carton may keep only half its compression strength — exactly the environment inside a sea container
  • Time decay: compression keeps falling under sustained load, so a safety factor of 0.5–0.6 is prudent — never extrapolate 40 days at sea from dry lab data
  • Stacking pattern: interlocked stacking holds better than column stacking, and pallet combinations above and below count too
  • Handling count: transhipment, container consolidation and repeated loading all accumulate damage, so inland destinations need extra margin

4. Two Hidden Losses Created by the Sea Freight Environment

Condensation: the day and night temperature swing inside a container forms dew on the carton surface, and a wet carton softens fast — which is why a batch that passes tests in the warehouse can arrive collapsed. Add container desiccant bars and an inner liner or moisture barrier bag inside the carton.

Rubbing and movement: in a part-full container cartons rub and shift against each other, and corners suffer first. Fill gaps with air bags or bracing panels, and use paper corner protectors for crush-sensitive items such as paper bags and brochures.

5. Closing the Carton: Staples, Hot Melt or Strapping

MethodStrengthWatch out for
StaplingHigh strength, low cost, stable speedStaple heads can scratch contents, opening is awkward, and some markets raise recycling questions
Hot melt gluingClean appearance, automatable, no metal residueSensitive to humidity and temperature; tack suffers in the cold
PP or PET strappingStops burst cartons and spillage, essential for heavy loadsNeeds corner protectors or the strap crushes the carton edges
Paper tape sealingMono-material and easy to recycle, matching European and US plastic-reduction preferencesStrength depends on tape quality and application; heavy cartons need a thicker tape

6. Six Parameters to Send with Any Carton Enquiry

  • Internal dimensions (length × width × height), not outside dimensions — internal size decides whether the goods fit
  • Packing quantity and gross weight per carton, including inserts and void fill
  • Stacking layers and storage duration, to work out the safety factor
  • Flute type and wall count, or the burst and edge crush values directly
  • Closing method: staples, hot melt, strapping or paper tape
  • Shipping mark content and print position, plus whether waterproofing or moisture protection is needed
💡 Tip: the most common rework on trims cartons is not weak board but an internal size off by a few millimetres — the kit will not fit or rattles badly and the whole batch has to be repacked. Pack real goods into the sample, measure the internal size, then confirm the drawing: far cheaper than reordering boxes.
