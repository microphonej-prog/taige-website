School Uniform and Kindergarten Labels: Name Tags, Crest Woven Labels and Group Sorting

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 2, 2026

Trims for school uniforms, kindergarten uniforms and teamwear are not used the way trims on ordinary garments are: one garment is washed dozens of times a year, and it has to be identifiable among dozens of identical pieces. This guide covers how to build name labels and crest woven labels, how to code sizes and class groups, how far to specify wash and colour fastness, how to sort and deliver group orders, and the six items to include in an enquiry.

1. Three Ways School Uniform Trims Differ From Ordinary Garments

  • Washing is frequent: uniforms are washed every week and replaced each season, so household wash cycles far outnumber those of fashion items. Wash grade, print durability and edge fraying must be designed for long service, not for the state of the garment on the day it ships.
  • Group identification: with dozens of identical pieces in the same colour, you need name labels, class marks or individual codes. Relying on the eye is where mistakes and complaints start.
  • Safety and compliance: small parts, drawcord length and migratable elements must be checked against the rules of the target market (CPSIA, EN 14682 and others), and name labels and crests fall inside that scope as well.

2. Name Labels: Choosing Between Three Common Solutions

A name label has three jobs: the marking must survive washing, must not scratch the skin, and must be read at a glance by a parent or a school. Choosing by those three tests saves more trouble than choosing by price.

3. Crest Woven Labels: Colour Count, Detail and Abrasion

  • Colour count and detail drive cost. A school crest usually runs four to six colours; satin jacquard can render fine lines and gradients, but every extra colour and every step up in density adds cost, so fix the budget before redrawing the artwork.
  • Thicken thin lines and keep minimum spacing. Once the crest is scaled down to finished size, hairlines in the original artwork merge together, so prove a reduced proof at sampling before bulk.
  • The chest is where abrasion peaks. Choose a high-density label with hot-cut edges to limit fraying, and sew with matched thread so no white specks or snags appear on a dark crest.
  • A crest usually belongs to a school or a third party: produce it only against written authorisation from the customer, never by copying from a photograph. That is the precondition for a long relationship.

4. Size Labels and Class Marks: Coding That Does Not Fail

  • Mark sizes in the children's height system — 110, 120, 130, or 4Y, 6Y, 8Y — and print a height-to-chest chart on the hang tag or packaging so parents pick right the first time and returns stay low.
  • For class or group marks, use a tag colour or an individual code — year, class, serial — which is far more reliable and wash-proof than handwriting or stickers.
  • Keep the code aligned with the sorting rule: one class or one size band per carton, with the class, sizes and quantity split printed on the carton mark, so the receiver does not have to open and count.

5. Wash Durability and Colour Fastness: Write It Into the Spec

  • Run the wash test to the ISO 6330 domestic procedure and state both the number of cycles and the pass criteria — for example legible after 60 washes, no obvious fading, no delamination. Wash-proof on its own means nothing.
  • Dark uniform colours — navy, burgundy, forest green — bleed easily. Use high-fastness ink or matched yarn and confirm the white lining does not take up colour; this is the single most common complaint on schoolwear.
  • Colour fastness on a woven label depends on yarn and dyeing, so approve a wash-tested sample before freezing the artwork. Changing a yarn once costs far less than reworking a bulk run.

6. Delivering and Sorting a Group Order

  • Work back from the start of term and ship in batches — lower years first, or the largest size band first — so the school is not buried under one delivery.
  • Bag by class or size, label the carton with a list of class, sizes and quantities, and drop a check sheet inside so a teacher or parent representative can verify on the spot.
  • Hold back three to five per cent for late joiners, losses and spoilage, and make sure any top-up uses the same material batch — otherwise the colour difference is visible to the naked eye.
💡 Tip: the biggest risk on schoolwear is not the unit price — it is shade variation between batches and a name label that runs out of room. Prove three things at sampling: the maximum name length the label can take, how the crest actually reads on a multi-colour ground, and the real condition after 60 washes. Clear those three and the next two years run quietly.
