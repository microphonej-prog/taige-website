Sustainable Sticker Labels: Linerless Rolls and What Happens to the Liner

By TAGE Packaging · Updated November 24, 2026

A sticker stuck on a hang tag or a poly bag is bought together with its liner. Glassine liner usually runs 60 to 90 g/m²; PET liner around 23 µm. Both cover the same area as the sticker itself, which means that after the store applies the label, roughly half of the material you paid for goes straight into general waste. Nobody sees that half, but you bought it and it lands on your environmental account.

This piece skips the general plastic-reduction slogans and works through three sums: how much material the liner actually costs, whether linerless can run on your line, and what evidence the word recyclable requires in the EU and in local markets.

1. The Material Bill of Three Liner Routes

Glassine is cheap, flat, and runs on almost every sheet-fed and roll machine, but the silicone coating keeps it out of the waste-paper stream; paper mills will not take silicone-coated liner. PET liner can enter plastic recycling, provided the store separates it and a recycler actually takes it, otherwise it is incinerated. Linerless removes the layer entirely and cuts material use by roughly 40–60% by area, at the cost of new equipment and a different print method.

Route Material and end of life What it takes to adopt
Glassine liner 60–90 g/m², silicone blocks paper recycling, mostly incinerated Runs on existing equipment, no line change
Recycled PET liner About 23 µm, recyclable as plastic if the store separates it Needs a recycler and store training, or it is decoration
Linerless 40–60% less material by area, zero liner waste Needs a thermal printer with a cutter; label size is constrained

2. Three Conditions for Linerless to Work

  • Print method: linerless usually relies on direct thermal printing with the silicone release coat on the back. That limits you to single-colour variable data such as price, size and barcode; four-colour branding still needs conventional lined printing.
  • Equipment and cutter: linerless is cut on the roll and peeled by hand, so cutter life and the gap setting drive downtime. Label length is typically limited to 20–100 mm with roughly ±0.3 mm tolerance.
  • Store cooperation: staff have to accept peeling one label at a time and give up the tagging gun. Centralised tagging in a distribution centre converts easily; scattered tagging across department-store counters is the hardest case.

3. What the Word Recyclable Actually Needs

Regulation (EU) 2025/40 on packaging and packaging waste requires all packaging to be designed for recycling by 2030, with recycled content phasing in on a timetable. Recyclable therefore stops being an adjective and becomes a design assessment plus a real collection route. The practical test is the RecyClass Design for Recycling guidelines and score, alongside ISO 14021, which requires self-declared environmental claims to be specific and verifiable rather than generically green.

  • Say this: liner is PET, it recycles with plastics, and the store has to separate it. Material, route and condition in one line.
  • Do not write 100% recyclable or eco material. A 100% claim without a collection route is not substantiated, and EU green claims rules plus national consumer law both target it.
  • Attach evidence: a RecyClass assessment, the liner supplier's material declaration, and a recycler's acceptance note. Without those three, the claim is just copy.

4. Specification and Acceptance

Three numbers carry the acceptance: peel adhesion (FINAT FTM 1, 180° peel), loop tack (FINAT FTM 2), and silicone coat weight (0.5–1.5 g/m²; too thin will not release, too thick migrates into the adhesive). Store at 20–25 °C and 50% relative humidity and rolls keep for 12 months. A summer sea container reaches 60 °C, the adhesive migrates, and that is the most common complaint, not bad material.

5. Local Rules and Units

  • EU and France: Regulation (EU) 2025/40 covers packaging, while France's AGEC law adds sorting information and the Triman mark. Whether you may call a liner recyclable depends on whether a stream actually exists locally.
  • Spain: Ley 7/2022 and its royal decrees require separate collection and producer responsibility; packaging weight is declared in grams and dimensions in mm and cm, and claims must match the producer responsibility registration.
  • Japan: the Containers and Packaging Recycling Act governs packaging, and green claims fall under the Act against Unjustifiable Premiums and Misleading Representations, which requires a reasonable basis; sizes are given in mm and sticker weight in g.
  • Korea: the Act on the Promotion of Saving and Recycling of Resources requires packaging material marking and grading, and green claims fall under the Act on Fair Labelling and Advertising; sizes appear as Korean numbers or cm, sticker specs in mm.
  • China: the GB/T 16716 series covers packaging and packaging waste, and under dual-carbon policy brands now ask suppliers for packaging reduction statements; specifications are stated in mm and g/m², not the shorthand gsm.

The practical sequence: run linerless variable-data labels in one distribution centre for a quarter, move glassine to a higher recycled-content grade in parallel, and only then discuss a full switch once the store collection route is proven. MOQ is typically 3,000–5,000 pieces per style; changing size or adhesive means a new sample round. The sample fee is credited against bulk, the lost calendar time is not.

The liner is the most ignored cost in the sticker chain: it carries no branding, never shows on the shelf and generates no complaints, yet it takes half the material by area. Measure it first, then replace it.
