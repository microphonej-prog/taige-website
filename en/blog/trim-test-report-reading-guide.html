How to Read an Apparel Trim Test Report

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 18, 2026

Most people open a trim test report and read the conclusion on the last page, which is the wrong end. The method code and test conditions on the first page decide whether the report can be used at all: a grade 4 for colourfastness to washing means two different things when one lab ran a mild 40 degrees programme and the other ran a 60 degrees cycle. This guide follows the order in which a report should be read: method and conditions, how grades work, typical thresholds, the four fields people skip, and the three things a report cannot replace.

1. Method Code and Conditions Before the Conclusion

Wash fastness exists in several parallel methods. ISO 105-C06 splits into programmes such as A1S, A2S, B1S and C1S depending on machine or hand conditions, with ISO 6330 governing pre-treatment and drying; the United States uses AATCC 61, Japan JIS L 0844, Korea the KS K 0430 series. Different methods and conditions cannot be compared, and a grade 4 from a mild 40 degrees programme cannot be presented as machine washable at 60. Before reading the result, confirm which condition the method code carries, how many cycles were run, and whether drying was line or tumble.

2. Reading the Grey Scale from 1 to 5

Change in colour and staining are graded separately against two grey scales, 1 the worst and 5 the best. In practice most results sit at 3, 3-4 and 4, where a half grade means between two boxes. Every report carries two sets of numbers: change in colour describes the sample itself, staining describes how much each strip of the adjacent multifibre fabric took up. Reading only the change column is the commonest misreading. A dark elastic band can score 4 for change while staining the white cotton strip at 2-3, and that band will fail at the customer.

3. Common Tests and the Thresholds Buyers Actually Use

Test Method (EU/US/JP/KR) Typical buyer threshold
Colourfastness to washing ISO 105-C06 / AATCC 61 / JIS L 0844 / KS K 0430 4 for change, 3-4 for staining
Colourfastness to rubbing ISO 105-X12 / AATCC 8 / JIS L 0849 / KS K 0650 4 dry, 3 wet; dark denim is negotiated separately
Perspiration and water ISO 105-E04 / AATCC 15 / JIS L 0848 / KS K 0715 4 on both change and staining
Dimensional change after washing ISO 6330 + ISO 5077 / AATCC 135 / JIS L 1096 / KS K 0870 within ±3 % warp and weft; knits are often relaxed to ±5 %

These numbers are the values buyers write into quality agreements; they are not law. The law governs labelling and information: fibre composition and the EU responsible person under (EU) 1007/2011, the mix ratio and care symbols under Japan's Household Goods Quality Labeling Act, quality display and origin in Korea. A test report proves that what you say is true; labelling law says what you must say. Neither replaces the other. A care label promising a 40 degrees machine wash against a report that only covered a mild 30 degrees programme is the gap market surveillance finds first.

4. The Four Fields Everyone Skips

  • Sample description: do the weight in g/m², composition and colour on the report match this lot? A new fabric or dyehouse voids the old report.
  • Lot number and submission date: a single-lot report does not cover later batches, and buyers usually require seasonal or per-batch retesting.
  • Condition details: water temperature, liquor ratio, detergent and drying. Change any one and the grades stop being comparable.
  • Validity and lab accreditation: most buyers accept reports under a year old and require ISO/IEC 17025 accreditation.

5. Three Things a Report Cannot Replace

First, it cannot replace bulk consistency. A pass covers the sample submitted, not the next batch from a different knitting mill or dye lot. Second, it cannot replace labelling law: the EU responsible person and GPSR information, the Japanese business operator name and care symbols, the Korean-language origin and importer are labelling obligations, not tests. Third, it cannot replace the quality agreement itself: the buyer's thresholds live in that document and the report only proves them, so if the method is not named there, two parties will argue with two different reports.

Three things you can do on the receiving day. Copy the method code and lot number from the report into the goods-in record. Cut a 10 cm × 4 cm swatch and check its weight against the g/m² on the report. Put the next re-test date in the purchasing calendar. Each takes five minutes and each finds the problem before the debit note does.
