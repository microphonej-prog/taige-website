Ribbon Trims Guide: Material, Width, Printing and Fixing

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 15, 2026

Ribbon and tape usually sit on the last line of a garment trims list, yet they land in the most visible places: a neckline bow, a waist tie, a hang tag cord, a lace-up detail, a gift box wrap or a paper bag handle. Ribbon is both decoration and function, and when it is chosen on looks alone the problem usually surfaces after the first wash — or after three weeks on a shelf.

This guide works through seven parts — end use, material and weave, width and thickness, colouring and printing, fixing and edge finish, wash testing, and a request-for-quotation checklist you can copy straight into an email.

1. Separate the Four Uses First: the Priorities Differ

In a catalogue they are all called ribbon, but what gets checked at inspection differs a great deal. Decoration is judged on sheen and drape, ties on tensile strength and how they untie, tag cords on edge stiffness and cut ends, packing ties on stiffness and how the knot holds. The table sets the four side by side.

Decoration and bowsSheen, drape, stable width6–25 mm satin or organzaWidth drift, fraying edges

2. Five Common Ribbons: Material and Weave

  • Satin (satin weave): glossy on one face, matte on the other, with good drape and rich colour — ideal for bows and gift boxes; the drawback is a face that scratches and fuzzes where it rubs.
  • Grosgrain: dense crosswise ribs with a stiff edge that keeps its shape, which makes it the usual choice for tag cords and ties; mostly supplied plain, and if printing is needed, specify an ink-receptive base weave.
  • Organza and chiffon: translucent and light, common on bridal work and gift decoration; the edge must be hot-cut or bound, otherwise it frays almost immediately.
  • Velvet ribbon: a raised pile face with strong presence, used on high-end gift boxes and autumn/winter ranges; pile has a direction, so state with-pile or against-pile in the spec or one batch can still show light and dark panels.
  • Cotton and herringbone tape: matte, soft and skin-friendly, suited to aprons, childrenswear and natural-looking packaging; shrinkage is relatively high, so pre-shrink or allow extra length.

3. Fixing Width, Thickness and Length

  • Width is quoted in millimetres, commonly 3, 6, 10, 15, 20, 25 and 38 mm; for decoration, settle the artwork proportion first and then the width, so a bow does not cover the brand name.
  • State the width tolerance in the spec (commonly ±0.5 mm, or agreed as a percentage), otherwise both sides will argue at inspection.
  • Gauge thickness by weight per metre or by yarn count rather than by width alone; the same width in different thickness feels completely different, especially for satin and grosgrain.
  • Work the tie length backwards from the allowance needed after knotting, and add wash shrinkage (commonly 2–6%, depending on material).
  • Where the ribbon must pass through an eyelet or tag hole, confirm the thickness clears the hole; allow at least 1 mm of hole diameter over the tape width.

4. Colouring and Printing: Plain, Dyed or Printed

  • Match plain ribbon to a reference (Pantone or the brand's own swatch) and write the acceptable colour tolerance into the spec; where one style uses ribbon in several places, order it in a single batch.
  • Lettering is usually screen printed or heat transferred: grosgrain has crosswise ribs that let screen ink bleed and blur the strokes, so below 3 mm cap height switch to heat transfer or use a woven label instead.
  • Double-face contrast, glitter and metallic ribbons shift with the viewing angle, so approve them on a physical sample — files and photos both flatter.
  • Where the ribbon will be washed, choose wash-resistant ink and run wash and rub fastness first; for gift boxes and packaging the requirement can be relaxed.
  • On projects with sustainability requirements, confirm fibre content, dye compliance and recycled content early, and allow lead time for the certificates themselves.

5. Fixing and Edge Finish: the Detail Behind Returns

  • How it is cut decides the end: hot cutting seals polyester satin and organza cleanly, while cotton and natural-fibre tape is cold cut and then bound or folded so it cannot fray.
  • A bow must not rely on glue alone: secure it after knotting with a heat-seal tack or a stitch, or it works loose in transit and in the fitting room and staff end up redoing it.
  • Keep stitching away from stress points — the stretched neckline, the zip channel, buttonholes, the underarm — and agree stitch density and thread so the seam does not pinch a crease into the ribbon.
  • Assess rubbing wherever the ribbon meets metal eyelets, tag holes or hard decoration, and add a radius, a pad or a softer tape where needed.
  • Keep reflective, glow and metallised ribbons away from high-temperature pressing lines; the coating loses performance or yellows with heat.

6. Washing, Testing and Inspection

  • For washable ribbon, test colour fastness to washing, rubbing and perspiration to the target market's conditions, and write those conditions into the spec sheet.
  • Confirm shrinkage and distortion against the real care routine — temperature, drying method, number of cycles — and for dry-clean-only items check solvent resistance.
  • For childrenswear, watch cord and small-part safety — cord length, free ends, detachable decoration — and keep traceable test reports on file.
  • For ribbon with metallic yarn or metal fittings, check nickel release and heavy-metal restrictions for the target market, and record material and plating details.
  • Keep a retained sample per batch: record colour reference, width, thickness, weave and supplier lot number, so any colour or hand difference can be traced back.
💡 Practical note: when one garment uses more than one ribbon (a satin neckline bow, a grosgrain tag cord and a packing tie, say), approve all three colours on one swatch at the same time. The usual failure is not one bad ribbon but three ribbons that do not look like one collection.

7. Ask It All at Once (Copy and Send)

  • Use and position: neckline bow, waist tie, tag cord, paper bag handle or gift box wrap.
  • Material and weave, width and thickness, plus the width tolerance you expect.
  • Colour: a reference number or physical swatch, and whether one batch is required or the reverse face differs.
  • Whether printing is needed: content, cap height, faces, and wash-resistance requirement.
  • Cutting and fixing: hot or cold cut, edge binding, and whether sewing or heat-seal tacking is included.
  • Service conditions: washing or not, water temperature and cycles, dry cleaning, and skin contact on children.
  • Quantity in metres, yards, rolls or pieces, with target market, required compliance documents, expected lead time and whether a sample is needed.
