Fiber Content Tolerance on Care Labels

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 18, 2026

Two numbers decide whether a care label survives a compliance check: the composition printed on it, and the composition a laboratory measures. Everything between them is tolerance, and tolerance is the part most brands write from memory. This guide sets out the three thresholds that actually get applied: the 3% allowance for unavoidable manufacturing variance, the 5% threshold below which a fibre may be folded into other fibre, and the rule of whole-percent rounding. It then shows a blend written to pass both a chemical analysis and a market surveillance check.

1. Tolerance Covers the Unavoidable, Not the Careless

Tolerance exists for processes nobody can control exactly: fibre mixing at the spinning mill, yarn loss at weaving, weight change in finishing. It does not cover a change you made yourself. If a brand moves from 60/40 to 65/35 without reprinting, the label is wrong however small the gap looks. The care label is the permanent carrier sewn into the garment, so it is the first thing a market surveillance officer reads, long after the hang tag has gone in the bin.

2. The Three Numbers: 3%, 5%, Whole Percent

First, Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011 requires the fibre composition as a percentage by weight, and enforcement practice accepts roughly 3% of variance for fibres that production cannot exclude. That allowance exists so a well-run mill is not punished for physics; it is not a licence to declare a different recipe. Second, 16 CFR Part 303 in the United States lets a fibre below 5% be disclosed as other fiber, which is intended for trace contamination and decorative yarns, not for the fibre that gives the garment its function. Third, every market wants whole percentages, and they must add up to 100.

3. A Worked Example: 62/33/5

A jersey measures 62% cotton, 33% polyester and 5% elastane. The correct label lists all three: 62% cotton, 33% polyester, 5% elastane. Even where the rule would allow it, do not fold the elastane into other fibre. Stretch is the reason the customer buys the garment; hiding it behind a general term removes functional information from the buyer, and omissions of this kind tend to come back as misleading-labelling complaints.

  • Any fibre at or above 5% must be listed on its own and cannot be merged.
  • The percentages must total 100; lining, filling and trims are listed separately or excluded with a note.
  • If the bulk re-test comes back at 58/36/6, that is no longer variance but a different product: reprint.

4. Market by Market: Legal Basis, Language, Tolerance

Market Legal basis and language How tolerance is handled
European Union (EU) 1007/2011; member-state language; GPSR responsible person also required Around 3% manufacturing variance accepted; undeclared fibre must not amount to another recipe
United States 16 CFR Part 303; English; 16 CFR Part 423 care instructions also required Under 5% may be other fiber, but functional fibres cannot be hidden
Japan 家庭用品品質表示法 (Household Goods Quality Labeling Act); Japanese; mix ratio and care symbols to JIS L 0001 Judged against the measured value; a gap beyond measurement scatter is a labelling failure
Korea 품질경영 및 공산품안전관리법; Korean; mix ratio, care symbols, origin The importer must hold test reports and the label must match them

One garment sold into four markets usually needs care labels in two languages plus a folded label: one face for the EU wording with the GPSR responsible person, one face for Japanese and Korean. Label width then becomes a hard constraint. On the common 2 cm and 3 cm tapes, 2 cm holds about three lines plus one row of care symbols; four fibres in two languages generally needs 3 cm or a folded label. Do not squeeze it: below roughly 1.5 mm cap height neither a store assistant nor a customer can read it.

5. How the Lab Tests, and Why Results Drift One or Two Points

Quantitative fibre analysis is mostly chemical dissolution: the ISO 1833 series dissolves one fibre at a time and weighs what is left. The United States works to AATCC 20A, Japan to JIS L 1030, Korea to KS K 0210. A specimen is typically a 10 cm × 4 cm piece, so a 180 g/m² jersey and a 320 g/m² sweatshirt yield very different amounts of fibre — one reason a development sample and a bulk lot disagree. The practical sequence is to fix the claim on the development sample, re-test the bulk lot, and only print when both agree.

Three habits that save a reprint. Re-test whenever the yarn supplier, dyehouse or knitting mill changes, even if the recipe did not. Keep the test report, the label artwork and the bulk lot number in one folder so a surveillance request takes five minutes. And settle in writing who pays for a reprint if the bulk re-test disagrees — that clause is cheaper to agree at quotation stage than after a debit note arrives.

6. Five Checks Before You Print

  • Does the stated value match the latest bulk report rather than the development sample?
  • Do the percentages total 100, and are lining and filling declared separately?
  • Are language, units and mandatory items complete for each target market, including the GPSR responsible person and origin?
