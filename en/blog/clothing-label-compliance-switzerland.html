Switzerland Clothing Label Requirements: Fibre Content, Language and Responsible Party

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 24, 2026

Switzerland is not an EU member state, yet it is often treated as a market where an EU label simply works. In practice Switzerland has its own consumer-goods legislation: textile fibre labelling is closely aligned with the EU rules, the language requirement follows the Swiss official languages, and an importer sits in the responsible-party role for the Swiss market. This guide covers what a Swiss clothing label must state, which language to use, how to handle the responsible party and barcodes, and the restricted-substance and childrenswear points that are most often missed.

1. Sorting Out Which Rules Apply: Switzerland Is Neither EU Nor EEA

Switzerland is an EFTA member with bilateral agreements with the EU, but it is not in the EU customs union and not in the EEA. That means EU regulations such as REACH, GPSR and PPWR do not apply directly in Switzerland: Swiss national law mirrors comparable requirements, and the enforcing authorities are different. For textiles the three main strands are fibre composition and consumer information, restricted substances in articles intended for human contact, and product safety with market surveillance. In practice one label can serve both the EU and Switzerland, provided you re-check language, responsible-party details and the restricted-substance list against the Swiss position.

2. Fibre Content and Language: German, French and Italian

Swiss fibre labelling follows the same logic as the EU: fibre names with percentages, blends in descending order, a tolerance of about three percentage points and the usual grouping rule for other fibres. Where projects go wrong is language: Switzerland has three official languages — German, French and Italian. For nationwide retail the safest layout is German and French side by side; a regional launch can follow local language; leaving English as the only language generally does not satisfy consumer information duties.

  • Swiss German has no ß: write ss instead of ß in German copy so the label avoids font and typesetting problems
  • Composition must match the goods: care label, hang tag and the online listing must state the same composition; inconsistencies fall back on the exporter and the Swiss importer
  • Elastane and microfibre wording: give the generic fibre name first and the brand second; a brand name alone can be treated as insufficient information in an inspection

3. Care Instructions and Wash Symbols: Not Mandatory, but Retail Expects Them

As in the EU, Switzerland does not impose a general duty to sew a care label into every garment, but in practice goods without care information struggle to get onto Swiss retail shelves and fall short of basic consumer expectations. Use the ISO 3758 symbol system plus one line of text in the local language; German-speaking and French-speaking regions can share one label, as the symbols are universal. Where a style needs special treatment — dry clean only, do not iron — state it as a clear conclusion rather than a vague hint.

💡 Tip: Switzerland is a small market with three languages, and the most common label mistake is English only. The most cost-effective layout is German and French on one label — together they cover the great majority of retail — with English added only where e-commerce or tourism channels need it.

4. Responsible Party and Importer: Who Stands Behind the Product in Switzerland

Whoever places a product on the Swiss market carries product safety duties, and an importer is generally treated on the same footing as a manufacturer for those duties. Swiss importers therefore usually want their name and a Swiss address on the label: it gives regulators and consumers a contact and creates a clear liability path. The usual exporter approach is to keep the brand on the neck label and reserve a fixed block on the care label for the importer's name and Swiss address; where the customer is a multi-channel Swiss brand, the brand's Swiss entity or distributor can fill that role. Keep test reports, composition records and restricted-substance reports for at least five years.

5. Barcodes, Origin and Customs: GS1 Prefixes and Made in

  • Barcode prefix and registration: Swiss retail reads the GTIN, and Swiss GS1 numbers start with prefix 760. The prefix only shows which member organisation issued the number, not where the goods were made, so imported goods often carry a number registered by the exporter
  • Origin marking: Made in China, or its equivalent in the target language, must match the origin declared at customs; a mismatch triggers a labelling correction
  • Switzerland is outside the EU customs union: a shipment going to both the EU and Switzerland is cleared separately, so prepare label and packing documents per destination rather than one set that satisfies neither
  • Import taxes and duties: Swiss import VAT and duties sit with the importer, so state whether the price is EXW, FOB or DDP to avoid a dispute when the goods land

6. Restricted Substances and Childrenswear: The Two Areas Inspections Check Most

Switzerland folds textiles, leather and other articles intended for human contact into dedicated consumer-goods legislation, setting limits on azo dyes and aromatic amines, formaldehyde, heavy metals, alkylphenol ethoxylates and phthalates, and treating nickel release from metal parts as a contact-item issue — broadly aligned with EU REACH. For childrenswear, two more areas matter: cords and drawstrings (the industry widely follows the EN 14682 assessment approach) and the security of small parts with sharp points and edges. Switzerland is also home to the OEKO-TEX association, and Swiss customers often simply ask for an OEKO-TEX STANDARD 100 report as proof on restricted substances — for a Swiss order that report usually saves more back-and-forth than a self-declared checklist.
