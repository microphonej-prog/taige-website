Care Label Fold Types: Bi-Fold, Tri-Fold and Fold Margins

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 26, 2026

How a care label is folded decides how much information the same piece of label can carry, where it can be sewn, and whether a shopper can actually read it. Plenty of reorders go wrong not because the printing was wrong but because the fold direction, the readable face or the seam edge was chosen badly: the regulatory text ends up folded inside and the finished label will not open.

This guide runs in six parts: how the four common fold structures compare, how folding changes the usable panel area, the order in which content should be laid out, the relationship between material and crease, how to fix the seam position and fold direction — closing with a sampling checklist you can inspect against directly.

1. The Four Common Fold Structures and Their Limits

The fold structure follows from two things: how much information the label must carry, and where it will be sewn. Once the structure is wrong, no amount of type-size tuning will rescue it.

StructureState when foldedTypical use
Flat, no foldOne strip, two faces, the fewest steps but also the least areaSimple knits, short side-seam labels
Bi-foldTwo faces, four panels, the usual apparel choice, read in one flipStandard styles with composition plus care
Tri-fold (accordion)Three faces, six panels, the standard answer for multilingual marketsEU multi-country, complex fibre blends
Four folds or moreFour faces and up, the most information, but fold margins eat a lot of usable widthOne style sold in several countries, size and origin both required

2. How Folding Changes the Usable Area

The same unfolded size can yield twice the usable panel area depending on the fold. The calculation order must start from the finished folded size and work back to the unfolded size by fold count — not the other way round.

  • Fix the folded width first: 20–40mm is the safe band — wider presses on the neck at the centre back, narrower will not hold two lines
  • Fold margins cost width: each extra fold takes roughly 2–3mm per side, so a four-fold label loses 8mm or more of usable width against its unfolded width
  • Keep text 2–3mm off the crease: otherwise the fold presses into the type after washing, the edge whitens first and it reads as fading
  • When it will not fill, add length not width: widening causes more itching and curling than lengthening, and a longer label also flips more easily
Work out the folded size first, then the unfolded size. Labels calculated the other way round almost always end up either impossible to fold or with the text folded inside.

3. The Order in Which Content Goes On

Care instructions, fibre content and origin are regulatory and must sit on a face readable without unfolding, at eye level. Brand and size are operational and can go on an inner panel. Once the order is set, every style in the range should follow it.

  • Face one: care symbols plus fibre composition, with a character height no smaller than 1.2mm — this is the only part that must be guaranteed
  • Face two: care guidance and multilingual text; EU markets often need several languages side by side
  • Inner panels: size, style number, origin, batch or a QR code — warehouse and traceability data a shopper does not need at first glance
  • Symbols before words: ISO 3758 symbols take little space and cross languages; text should supplement, not explain every symbol
  • Treat languages as fixed blocks: adding a market changes the text, not the layout, so reorders do not need a fresh layout each time

4. Material and the Crease

The crease is where a folded label fails first: coatings crack, woven edges fray, printed film lifts. The material has to match the fold — not the habit material first and the structure afterwards.

  • Coated nylon or polyester: crisp printing at controllable cost, but the coating cracks at a fold — better for flat and bi-fold
  • Woven tape (twill, taffeta): wash and fold resistant, with a crease that stays soft after repeated laundering — the right base for three folds and beyond
  • Printed satin ribbon: soft against skin, but the edge must be hot-cut or ultrasonically sealed or it frays within two washes
  • Four folds and beyond: prefer woven tape, or route the crease through a blank band between text blocks so the fold lands where nothing is printed

5. Seam Position and Fold Direction

The fold direction decides which way a customer opens the label: folding up or folding down determines whether the care information comes to hand. The seam type then decides whether the label itches.

  • Centre back neck: single-edge seam, folded upward so the symbols show on opening — the most common arrangement
  • Side seam: double-edge seam with both ends closed; face one must be outward or the customer sees a blank back
  • Hems and inside waistbands: fold downward so the label cannot flip and expose its back where it will be seen
  • Next-to-skin and childrenswear: round or bind the folded end and keep the seam allowance under 5mm to cut friction and itching — the single most common and cheapest return cause to fix

6. Sampling Checklist

  1. Folded size matches the drawing: the corners square up and it returns to shape after repeated opening
  2. Key information readable without unfolding: hold it at eye level and check whether symbols and composition show at first glance
  3. Crease clear of type: no cracking, no exposed core, no ink loss; on laminated labels check whether the film lifts at the fold
  4. Wash 3–5 cycles by the real process: no fraying at the crease, no coating loss, and the symbols must stay legible
