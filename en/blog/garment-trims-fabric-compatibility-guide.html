Fabric and Trim Compatibility: Shrinkage, Colour Fastness and Wash Testing

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 25, 2026

A trim can be beautifully made and still fail the moment it goes through the wash with the garment. If woven label shrinkage does not match the fabric, the label puckers and lifts; pair a dark fabric with a white care label and the label picks up dye within a few washes; send a polyester label and a cotton fabric through the same cycle and one barely moves while the other shrinks 3%, and the problem lands on the seam. This guide sets out how to match trims to fabric — shrinkage, colour fastness and care method — and the verification work to finish before you place the order.

1. Three Ways Trims and Fabric Fight Each Other

  • Puckering after washing: trims and fabric shrink at different rates and the seam pulls the label out of shape — most common when cotton, linen or viscose meets a polyester label
  • Staining and cross-staining: a dark fabric bleeds onto a pale label, or a dark label bleeds onto pale fabric; white and pale close-to-skin styles show it after a single wash
  • Curling, stiffening, cracking: coated care labels and heat transfers curl after hot ironing or dry cleaning, stiffen over repeated washes and can crack at the fold

2. Shrinkage Matching: Test the Fabric First, Then Choose the Trim

The first step is a real washed-shrinkage figure for the fabric. Run a domestic wash programme to ISO 6330, measure dimensional change to ISO 3759, test the same fabric lot at least three times and average the results — and record the drying method (tumble or line dry), because the same fabric can differ by one or two percentage points depending on how it is dried.

  • Match the fibre family first: cotton fabric goes best with cotton tape or cotton printed labels, synthetics with polyester or nylon trims — same-family materials diverge least
  • Know the orders of magnitude: polyester woven labels usually stay under 1 % shrinkage, cotton tape commonly runs 3–5 %, blends land in between — but use your own test figures, not figures copied from someone else
  • When you cannot match, change the process: fold under and stitch, anchor only at two ends, leave 1–2 mm slack, or use a smaller label so it has room to move — more reliable than forcing a shrinkage figure
  • Tags stay out of the wash: hang tags are normally removed before washing, but confirm crease and steam resistance — the steam and stacking of garment pressing will curl and soften paper tags

3. Colour Fastness and Staining: Pale and Close-to-Skin Styles Fail First

Colour fastness is not about how the trim looks; it is about whether it drops a grade under washing, perspiration, ironing or bleaching, and whether it hands its colour to the neighbouring fabric. Choose test items against the customer standard and record the conditions — temperature, time, detergent, drying — on the approval sample. Common limits are a change of shade at grade 4 or better and staining at grade 3–4 or better, but the brand standard governs.

  • Colour fastness to washing (ISO 105-C06): wash with a multifibre adjacent strip and read both the trim's own shade change and its staining of six fibre types — the baseline test
  • Perspiration (ISO 105-E04): acidic and alkaline solution tested separately; essential for close-to-skin, knit and activewear styles
  • Ironing (ISO 105-X11): foiled, coated and heat-transfer trims change most at high temperature, so test up to the real pressing temperature of the garment
  • Chlorine and industrial washing: swimwear follows colour fastness to chlorinated water (ISO 105-E03), while workwear and rental uniforms need durability after industrial laundering (ISO 15797)
  • Dark fabric, pale label: favour a label in the same family or slightly deeper, or place it where fabrics do not rub together in the drum, to cut the visual risk of cross-staining

4. The Care Method Sets the Test Matrix

Care method Reference standard What it demands of the trim
Domestic washing ISO 6330 / ISO 3759 No curling or fading, and shrinkage in step with the fabric
Dry cleaning ISO 3175 Coatings and glued marks swell and lift, so suiting is better served by woven labels
Ironing and pressing ISO 105-X11 Foil must not migrate, heat transfers must not re-tack, paper tags must not curl
Pool and seawater ISO 105-E03 Elastic labels powder and embrittle without chlorine resistance, so swim styles need chlorine-resistant material
Industrial laundering ISO 15797 Under high heat and alkalinity, tape, thread and print each need separate validation

5. How to Run the Compatibility Check Before Ordering

  • Use bulk-lot fabric: figures taken from development or a previous lot drift; start the check with the fabric that will actually be cut
  • Sew the candidate trims where they belong: attach them in the real position and with the real seam, then wash the assembly — do not wash a loose label on its own
  • Photograph before and after: same light, same background, and record shrinkage, colour shift and hand feel — numbers beat adjectives
  • Get the approval sample signed: state material, specification, test conditions and results on it; once approved it becomes the acceptance benchmark
  • Re-test whenever the bulk material changes: a new yarn, coating or paper mill means re-running the key tests; if the acceptance standard does not follow the material, the risk lands on the garment factory

6. Compatibility Checklist

  • Are fabric content, construction and finishing confirmed
  • Do the shrinkage figures come from the bulk lot, and is the drying method recorded
  • Is the trim from the same fibre family as the fabric, with an acceptable shrinkage gap
  • Have the washing, perspiration and ironing tests run to the customer standard, at the required grades
  • Is there a plan for cross-staining between dark fabric and pale trims
  • Are before/after samples kept on file and the customer's approval in writing
