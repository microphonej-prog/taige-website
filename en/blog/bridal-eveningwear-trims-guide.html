Bridal and Eveningwear Trims Guide: Satin Labels, Foil Tags and Gift Boxes

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 24, 2026

Bridal and eveningwear are the most particular trims category: the highest unit value, the smallest runs, and the least tolerance for the wrong label on a delicate fabric. Silk, satin, organza and lace snag and hold needle marks, beaded and rhinestone styles will not survive a careless press, and an order is often only 50 to a few hundred pieces. This guide covers how to choose a satin neck label, how to word care labels for dry-clean-only and beaded styles, how to pair foil-stamped tags with ribbon and metal fasteners, how to keep gift boxes and dust bags from yellowing, and gives a specification table you can paste straight into an enquiry email.

1. Why Bridal Trims Cannot Copy a Standard Garment Spec

  • Small runs, high unit value: 50 to 300 pieces per style is normal, so settle MOQ and sampling fees up front — pricing built for bulk orders scares projects off at the sample stage
  • Delicate fabric: silk and satin snag and hold needle marks, so label edges must be soft and the sewing needle fine with a tight stitch. Keep the neck label away from exposed seams
  • Channel decides the care wording: bespoke studios, rental houses and online couture have very different care needs. Rental stock must survive repeated dry cleaning, so label and care card wording should be more conservative
  • Presentation first: the neck label and hang tag show up in wear shots, livestreams and unboxing videos, so visual consistency matters more than information density — do fewer techniques, but do them well

2. Neck Labels: Satin, Double-Face Weave and Tagless Options

On bridal, the neck label has to deliver branding and comfort at once, and the strategy is soft, thin and tonal. For silk and light satin, specify a thin satin woven label with ultrasonically cut edges. On a satin dress, a tone-on-tone jacquard reads more refined than a high-contrast colour. For lace, tulle and sheer styles, use a tagless heat-transfer label or move the label to the side-seam lining so it does not show through. Always test heat-transfer labels for dry-clean durability and the effect of fabric softener before bulk sewing — rework on bridal is extremely expensive.

3. Care Labels and Size Labels: Dry-Clean Symbols and Beaded Styles

Almost all bridal and eveningwear is dry-clean only, so the care label should state a clear conclusion rather than a vague hint. Follow the ISO 3758 symbol system: professional dry cleaning (P in a circle), do not wash, do not bleach, iron or do not iron, dry flat — plus one line of text to reduce disputes caused by customers washing a gown. The symbols travel worldwide; the wording follows the selling market.

  • Silk and acetate: say dry clean only, and give the actual maximum iron temperature instead of a generic low heat, otherwise the customer has nothing to judge by
  • Beads, rhinestones and sequins: mark do not iron, or iron on the reverse under a cloth at low heat — one pass on the face can lift stones and damage sequins
  • Lace and organza: specify dry flat, do not hang — these fabrics stretch and distort under their own weight
  • Rental and multi-market: symbols are universal, text follows the market. For rental stock, add professional dry clean after each use to stop renters improvising
  • Size labels: bridal sizing is not standardised, so put the garment size plus key measurements (bust, waist, back length) on the label or inside tag — it cuts disputes on high-return made-to-measure and online orders

4. Hang Tags and Hardware: Foil, Emboss and Ribbon

Bridal hang tags are usually larger and heavier than standard garment tags, commonly 350 to 450 gsm specialty or cotton paper, finished with foil stamping, embossing and spot UV. Foil shows best on a dark matte stock; embossing gives the logo a tactile memory; on a tight budget, one foil plus one emboss usually reads more premium than a fully decorated tag. Choose the cord and fastener to the tag's proportions — ribbon suits bridal better than a plastic fastener.

  • Metal fasteners and charms: specify low-nickel-release or coated metal (EN 1811 is the usual test reference), especially where metal touches skin or sits near the neck
  • Eyelets and punching: eyelets last longer on bridal tags, with hole diameter matched to ribbon width; keep the hole at least 12 mm from the paper edge to stop tearing
  • Matching insert cards: make the care card, fabric content card and atelier card the same size and finish as the tag so they read as one set
  • Switching price systems: use a tear-off price ticket so retail and rental pricing can change without reprinting the whole tag run

5. Gift Boxes and Dust Bags: Yellowing, Moisture and Restricted Substances

Gowns are often stored for years, so packaging is not only about looks. White and ivory satin yellows in contact with acidic paper, sulphur-cured rubber or PVC, so line boxes with acid-free tissue and acid-free board, and choose PP nonwoven or washable cotton dust bags rather than long-term PVC. Use silica gel for mould prevention, never a dimethyl fumarate (DMF) anti-mould sachet — DMF is banned in consumer products and desiccants in the EU and other markets, and a non-compliant batch can be rejected in full. For sea freight, add a moisture barrier bag and a humidity indicator card, then re-check one carton on arrival.

💡 Tip: make the couture box reusable as a storage box — clients keep it to store the gown, which earns far more exposure than a single-use gift box. When moving to acid-free lining, also confirm the lining adhesive does not ghost under humidity, otherwise yellowing starts inside the box.
