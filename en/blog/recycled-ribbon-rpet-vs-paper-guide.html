Recycled Ribbon: What rPET Changes, and Where Paper Wins

By TAGE Packaging · Updated November 26, 2026

Ribbon is the trim most easily overlooked: it is filed under packaging rather than trims, weighs a fraction of a gram on the BOM, and no buyer schedules a meeting for it. The volume tells a different story. A 15 mm ribbon at 180 g/m² runs about 2.7 g per metre, so a standard bow using three metres consumes 8 g; on a 5,000-piece order that is 40 kg of material in ribbon alone. When a brand starts asking about recycled content, ribbon is usually the last item raised and the hardest to answer. This guide covers what rPET actually changes, where paper and cotton make sense, how dyeing and fastness testing decide the real environmental score, and which documents a buyer should request.

1. Why Ribbon Is Always the Last Question

Three structural reasons. First, ribbon usually sits in the packaging group on a purchase order alongside paper bags and stickers, and packaging requirements tend to stop at recyclability, never reaching recycled content. Second, it is light enough to disappear in a carbon calculation, so nobody prepares data for it. Third, the ribbon supply chain is separate from woven labels and care labels: weaving, dyeing and slitting are three different plants, and the information trail breaks at the slitting step. The only way to close the gap is to pull from both ends, specification and documentation.

2. rPET Ribbon: Three Real Differences

rPET ribbon is not the same product with a different feedstock. Recycled bottle flake is washed, pelletised and spun, and its molecular weight distribution differs from virgin resin. Three things show up in the finished ribbon, and each of them is visible at inspection.

  • Slightly lower breaking strength: shorter chains in recycled feedstock usually pull the same construction below virgin polyester. How much depends on the source and the spinning process, so measure it: ISO 13934-1 strip method or GB/T 3923.1, five specimens, average value, not a verbal assurance.
  • A narrower heat window: the gloss face is set by heat, and rPET shows ironing marks or a mirror sheen more readily. Set the ironing temperature from a test (it commonly falls between 110 and 150 °C) and always press through a cloth, never directly on the gloss face.
  • Larger batch-to-batch shade variation: recycled feedstock carries a grey or yellow cast, so the same colour code shifts between lots. Do not approve from a single swatch; judge by Delta E against a retained standard and ask the dyehouse for a shade report per lot.

3. Paper and Cotton Ribbon: Trading Strength for Recyclability

When the goal shifts from containing recycled material to letting the whole tag go into paper recycling, rPET stops being the answer: it is plastic, and it drags paper packaging into mixed material. Paper ribbon (twisted kraft cord, flat paper tape) and cotton ribbon become the solution. The price is written into the specification: paper tape has low tensile strength and softens with humidity, cotton stretches when damp and is harder to dye to a high fastness.

Virgin polyester satin about 2.4–3.0 g Low: plastic, contaminates paper if not separated Good, survives 60 °C wash Premium tags, gift box ties
rPET satin ribbon about 2.4–3.0 g Low, but recycled content may be claimed with a GRS TC Good, but a narrow ironing window Brands needing recycled content
Twisted kraft cord about 1.2–2.0 g High, same stream as the paper tag Not for washing, retail display only Recyclable tags, plastic-free programmes
Cotton ribbon about 3.5–4.5 g High, pure cotton follows the textile stream Medium, stretches when wet Natural styling, organic cotton lines

4. Dyeing and Finishing: Where the Real Score Is Decided

Fibre is only part of a ribbon's environmental load; dyeing and finishing usually dominate water, energy and chemistry. When a buyer asks whether a ribbon is green, the sharper questions are about the dyehouse's effluent treatment, azo and heavy-metal restrictions on the dyes, and whether any water repellency is PFAS-free. None of that is detectable by touch; only documents show it.

  • Fastness needs a number, not an adjective: washing per ISO 105-C06, rubbing per ISO 105-X12, grade 4 or better is the usual bar. 'Does not fade' is not a specification.
  • Dye compliance: the EU controls azo dyes and certain amines under REACH (EC) 1907/2006 Annex XVII, and brands usually add the ZDHC MRSL as a chemical list. Submit both together.
  • Water repellency: if the ribbon is treated, declare it PFAS-free, since the EU and several US states restrict them. Otherwise an environmental selling point turns into a return reason.

5. The Evidence Chain: Four Documents per Ribbon

Recycled content is a documentation problem, not a materials problem. When a buyer asks for proof, only a complete set answers it: a GRS or RCS scope certificate proving the plant may produce recycled goods, a transaction certificate proving this specific lot carries the recycled content, test reports for physical performance, and a chemical list for dyeing and finishing compliance. Miss one and the claim drops back to the verbal level.

6. Six Lines to Put in the Tech Pack

Most ribbon disputes begin with a thin specification. Six lines in the tech pack give quotation and inspection a common basis: width in mm, thickness in mm, grammage in g/m², breaking strength in N with the test method named, fastness grade with the test standard named, and material plus certification requirements. Add one more sentence, that shade variation within a lot is judged at Delta E of 1.5 or below, and most rework disappears.

If the goal is a tag that goes into paper recycling, choose paper cord, not rPET. If the goal is a recyclable-content statement, choose rPET and have the TC ready. If both goals apply, build two tag versions; that is cheaper than compromising on one.
