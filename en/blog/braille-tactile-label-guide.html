Braille and Tactile Labels on Garment Tags: An Accessibility Guide

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 5, 2026

Consumers with a vision impairment still need the care instructions, the size and the fibre content — making key information something you can feel is a design move more apparel brands are adding. Braille labels and tactile marking on garments usually take one of a few forms: raised dots woven into a woven label or care label, embossing on a paper hang tag, thick-film UV print for a raised varnish, and alongside those, high-contrast large type and voice-linked QR codes. Which one fits depends on whether the information must actually be read, whether the substrate survives repeated washing, and how much the brand is willing to invest.

This guide has seven parts: why apparel brands are adding tactile marking, the three levels of accessible information, four tactile processes compared, tactile marks on a care label, tactile design on hang tags and packaging, an accessibility check at the design stage, and sampling and sourcing criteria.

1. Why Apparel Brands Are Adding Tactile Marking

Vision impairment affects well over a billion people worldwide, tens of millions of them blind (WHO figures), and size, care and fibre information is just as necessary for them. Two legal points need separating: the European Accessibility Act (Directive (EU) 2019/882), applicable from 28 June 2025, covers the products and services listed in its annex — including e-commerce services — which means online product information must work with assistive technology; it does not force braille onto clothing labels. The requirement for braille is on medicinal product packaging. So braille on a garment tag today is mainly voluntary inclusive design: reaching customers with a vision impairment, backing a brand value, or answering a retail customer's request — not a mandatory compliance item.

💡 Tip: treat braille as a product-experience question, not a compliance box. Brands that do tactile marking properly usually fix online information and in-store assistance first and then look at labels — in the reverse order, the effort is large and the return small.

2. Three Levels: Braille, Tactile Symbols and Accessible Content

  • Standard braille: genuinely readable by a braille user, but it must respect dot diameter, dot spacing and line spacing (Marburg Medium is a common international reference), and the text has to go through braille translation rules for contractions, numbers and symbols. You cannot simply arrange letters or characters as raised dots by shape.
  • Tactile symbols: clusters of raised dots, raised lines or shaped marks that carry limited key information such as size or wash temperature without claiming to be braille. The process threshold and cost are far lower, which suits small, quickly recognised signals.
  • Accessible content: high-contrast large type, matte surfaces to avoid glare, voice-linked QR codes or NFC guidance that works with screen readers. This layer carries the most information and is the easiest to reuse online.

4. Tactile Marks on a Care Label

A care label is the one label that gets washed and rubbed repeatedly, so three things must hold. Dot durability: woven dots beat printed dots — coating and screen-printed relief flattens after repeated home laundering. No clash with care symbols: the wash symbols are a printed graphic set; a tactile version is better reduced to simplified marks for the key items rather than forcing the whole set. Still identifiable after washing: run 5-cycle and 10-cycle checks to an ISO 6330 home-laundry programme and write the result into the acceptance criteria. Dot height, spacing and feel must be confirmed by hand at the sampling stage — never from the artwork alone.

5. Tactile Design on Hang Tags and Packaging

A hang tag is a carrier the brand controls: emboss the brand lettering or monogram, keep hole and eyelet positions away from tactile clusters, shape the tear notch so it can be found by touch; paper bags and boxes can carry a tactile opening cue (dots marking the opening direction); gift boxes can add a textured or embossed layer so the message registers before the box is opened. The approach luxury and pharmacy brands favour is minimal visuals plus one embossed gesture — understated yet tangible, and the easiest thing to remember at the point of sale.

6. An Accessibility Check at the Design Stage

  • Keep text contrast at WCAG AA (4.5:1 or better for body text) and avoid fine reversed type and pale grey small print
  • Colour must never be the only carrier of information: size, composition and care level all need text or symbol redundancy
  • Type size: meet the minimum legible type height for labels; a tactile mark never replaces readable text
  • QR codes and NFC must lead to a page that can be read aloud or by a screen reader, not just an image
  • After sampling, have a user with a vision impairment test it once — more informative than any specification sheet

7. Sampling and Sourcing Criteria

For tactile marking, send three things together: the information to convey and its language, the editable copy needed for braille transcription (checked by us or a braille conversion tool so contractions are right), and the durability acceptance conditions (laundry programme and cycle count). Sampling takes 3–7 days and bulk 10–25 days, with flexible minimums: 2,000–3,000 hang tags for a trial run, woven labels from 1,000 pieces, care labels from 300–500. If the process cannot hold true dot diameters, call it tactile marking rather than braille — a brand should never be accused of an accessibility claim it cannot support.
