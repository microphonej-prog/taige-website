Woven Label Density and Clarity: Weft Density, Minimum Text Height and Fine Detail

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 30, 2026

Small text blotting together on a woven label, or the same artwork crisp from one mill and soft from another: the first suspicion is usually the equipment, but in most cases density and artwork simply do not match. Density decides how fine a line and how small a letter the weave can hold, and it decides hand feel and cost at the same time.

This guide separates warp from weft density, gives the ranges commonly used for neck labels, sleeve labels, next-to-skin positions and babywear, sets out the numbers that let you fix expectations before the contract — minimum readable text height, finest line width, negative space — then shows how density lifts clarity and cost together, and closes with four hard artwork rules and six specification lines for your enquiry.

1. Warp Density and Weft Density Are Not the Same Thing

A woven label is two sets of yarn crossing: the warp runs lengthwise and decides how much detail can be resolved across the width, while the weft is inserted across and decides clarity along the length. In the trade, density usually means weft density — how many weft yarns are woven into one centimetre.

  • When warp density is too low, the vertical strokes and small counters of letters stick together, and horizontal lines blur first.
  • When weft density is too low, letter edges look fuzzy and colour boundaries jagged — the most common reason the same artwork comes back different from two suppliers.
  • The two have to match: raising weft density alone, with warp unchanged, improves little and only makes the label stiffer.
  • Yarn count matters as much: fine yarn with high density is the crispest and the dearest, coarse yarn with low density the softest and cheapest — but only for larger motifs.

2. Common Density Ranges by Application

The ranges below reflect common practice; actual figures depend on yarn count, motif detail and the loom. Explaining the application and the smallest text is far more useful in an enquiry than quoting a density figure alone.

ApplicationTypical weft densityClarity and handNotes
Neck labelHigher, around 100–120 picks/cmSmall text legible, label firmChoose this when fine text and thin lines are needed
Sleeve or side labelMedium-high, around 90–110 picks/cmBalances clarity and softnessCommon for everyday styles and visible positions
Large motif or sloganLow to medium, around 80–100 picks/cmSoftness firstBetter value when the motif carries no small text
Babywear, underwear, next-to-skin knitwearLower density with fine yarnSoftest; touch comes firstReplace small text with colour blocks or simplified copy

3. Minimum Readable Sizes: Fix Expectations Before the Contract

  • Minimum text height usually falls between 1.5 and 2 mm; below that, strokes crowd each other and become harder to read after washing.
  • The finest line is roughly 0.15–0.2 mm; anything thinner gets swallowed by the neighbouring yarns.
  • Negative space — the gap between letters and between lines — must be at least as wide as the line itself; cramped layouts are the most common cause of a failed woven label.
  • Gradients, drop shadows and hairline serifs do not weave well; replacing them with solid blocks, white space and heavier strokes gives a far more reliable result.
  • Sign off on the washed state: labels shrink and fuzz in the wash, and clear before washing does not mean clear after.

4. How Density Raises Clarity and Cost Together

The higher the density, the more weft yarn is consumed and the longer the loom runs, so unit cost rises; the label also becomes thicker and firmer, which can be scratchy next to the skin. Clarity and cost tend to rise together, so improving clarity should always be weighed against a second route.

  • Density up: clarity up, label firmer, cost up.
  • Density down: softer and cheaper, but small text and thin lines become a risk.
  • Often the better value: fewer colours, heavier strokes, larger type, or detail replaced by solid blocks — usually a bigger visible gain than more density, at a lower cost.

5. Four Hard Rules for the Artwork File

  1. Send vector files (AI, EPS or PDF); never pass an enlarged bitmap off as vector.
  2. Convert type to outlines and check spelling and letter spacing character by character — when small text fails, spacing is usually the culprit, not the size.
  3. Separate colours into layers with clear boundaries, and leave roughly 0.1–0.2 mm between neighbouring colours to prevent bleed during weaving.
  4. Dimension the artwork at 1:1, and state the width, the fold and the edge finish (hot cut, ultrasonic cut or woven selvage).

6. Six Specification Lines for Your Enquiry

  • Size and fold: flat, end-fold, centre-fold or two side folds, together with the finished width.
  • Clarity requirement: state the minimum text height and finest line width — far easier to execute than asking for high density.
  • Yarn material: polyester, cotton or recycled yarn; if recycled content is required, state the certification basis (for example GRS).
  • Colour count and references: give Pantone numbers or a physical swatch, never a verbal description of colour.
  • Edge and back requirements: backing needed or not, sealed edges or not, and whether content is woven on the reverse.
  • Application and certification: next-to-skin, babywear or export market, and whether certificates such as OEKO-TEX are required.
💡 Tip: when density is in doubt, the cheapest test is not zooming in on screen but asking the supplier for a sample at finished size with your thinnest line and smallest motif included — one look at the physical label settles more than an hour of debate.
