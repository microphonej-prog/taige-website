Heat Transfer Labels Guide: Processes, Materials and Applications

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 15, 2026

Heat transfer labels are produced on a carrier film first, then bonded to the garment with a heat press under controlled temperature, pressure and dwell time. Without the stitches of a woven label or the raised edges of a printed label, they feel soft against the skin and can reproduce full-colour gradients — which is why activewear, kidswear, underwear and technical fabrics increasingly rely on them.

This guide covers four decision layers — process type, fabric compatibility, wash standards and cost structure — so you can judge whether heat transfer labels suit your product, and which type to choose.

1. How Heat Transfers Differ From Woven and Printed Labels

A woven label is built from interlaced yarns and has a raised edge that must be sewn on. A printed label is printed onto tape or satin and still needs stitching. A heat transfer is bonded directly into the fabric and adds virtually no thickness. They are not substitutes but a division of labour: use woven where you want dimensional texture and a durable outer brand mark, and heat transfer where you want softness and complex graphics.

3. Fabric Compatibility: Start With Fibre Content

Fabric choice decides roughly 80% of the outcome. Not all polyester behaves the same: pair it with the wrong product and you either scorch the elastane or watch the transfer lift after two washes. Below are practical starting points by fibre.

  • 100% cotton — the most forgiving substrate; nearly every transfer type works. It heats slowly, so allow a slightly longer dwell time.
  • Polyester and blends — use low-temperature adhesive and always run a dye-migration test, since disperse dyes can sublime under heat.
  • Elastane and stretch knits — choose high-stretch rubber or elastic silicone; a rigid transfer will crack when the fabric recovers.
  • Nylon and coated waterproof shells — the coating blocks adhesion; test first and consider silicone heat pressing or high-frequency welding instead.

4. Setting the Right Wash Durability Standard

Wash requirements belong in the brief, not in a complaint file later. Brands normally work to three levels: home machine wash (care symbol permits it, transfer must stay bonded for a defined number of cycles), industrial laundering (common on denim and workwear, stricter) and dry clean only (suits, coats). If the care label says machine washable, the transfer must pass the matching cycle count; if it says hand wash or dry clean, the bar is lower.

💡 Tip: Ask your supplier to press samples on your actual fabric, not a standard test cloth. Fibre content, GSM and finishes (softener, water repellent) all change adhesion, so the same artwork can perform one full grade differently on two fabrics.

5. Sampling, MOQ and What Drives the Cost

Cost breaks into four blocks: tooling or plate charges (one-off per artwork, not repeated on reorders), material (film and adhesive grade), production (colour count, overprints, dimensional effects) and labour and packing. Because the tooling is reusable, reorder unit prices are usually well below the first order.

On quantity, transfers are far more flexible than woven labels: digital film can run from a few hundred pieces down to single-piece customisation, while silicone or laser transfers involve tooling and usually only make sense from the thousand-piece range. If one graphic is used across several colourways and sizes, you tool once for the graphic — not once per size.

6. Artwork and Production Notes

  • Keep hairlines above 0.3 mm and type above 6 pt; anything finer tends to break up after pressing.
  • Leave 0.2–0.3 mm trapping between adjacent colours to avoid register gaps showing through.
  • Vector artwork (AI/PDF) is safest; for raster files supply 300 dpi or more at 1:1 size.
  • State the placement and tolerance (for example 2 cm below the neckline, centred, ±2 mm).
  • For kidswear and next-to-skin items, confirm the materials meet CPSIA/REACH chemical safety requirements.
