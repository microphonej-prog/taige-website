Trims Trade Show Sourcing: How to Walk Intertextile, Canton Fair and Texworld

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 17, 2026

The real risk in trims sourcing is not a high price — it is picking the wrong supplier and losing a season to rework. A trims trade show is still the fastest filter: dozens of tag, label and packaging factories in one day, paper you can touch, weave density you can feel, processes you can discuss face to face. Yet most visitors leave with a bag of business cards and a stack of quotes, and three months later cannot tell which sample came from whom. The problem is not the show, it is the way we walk it.

Six parts: what each of the three show types is actually for, a pre-show preparation list, eight questions to ask at the booth, how to take samples and record them properly, a 30-day follow-up rhythm after the show, and the alternatives — online sourcing and a factory visit.

1. Three Show Types, Three Jobs

Decide before you go what this trip must solve: new categories, pressure on current prices, or a read on where the industry is heading. The three show types answer different questions, and mixing them usually means doing neither well.

2. Before You Enter: Seven Things to Bring

  • Your current trims: one hang tag, one woven label, one care label in use. Ten times more useful than photos — a factory learns your level and budget by touch.
  • Fabric swatches and colour references: trims must match colour and weight. Bring A4 swatches of your main fabrics plus Pantone references and you can judge colour direction on the spot.
  • Spec sheet and volumes: annual volume per SKU, number of colours, number of sizes and expected lead time. Without volumes, a factory can only quote at retail-level pricing.
  • A target price band: on paper, in your pocket. Saying it is too expensive conveys nothing; a band lets you trade off processes.
  • Cards with a QR code: put your category and target markets on the back, with a WeChat or WhatsApp code in plain sight — nobody wants to type a number on a show floor.
  • Comfortable shoes and a power bank: sounds trivial, but ten to twenty thousand steps a day plus constant photos and video means day two collapses on battery.
  • A blank record sheet: print the fields you need as a table before you go. Far faster than scrolling chat history, and easy to enter into a system afterwards.

3. Eight Questions to Ask at the Booth

  1. Is this process in-house or outsourced? If printing, foiling, die-cutting and weaving sit in three different shops, both lead time and accountability get diluted.
  2. What is the MOQ, and can a trial order be smaller? Ask whether it is per design or per colour — that distinction is often a factor of ten.
  3. How many days to sample, what does it cost, is it credited against bulk? A refundable sample fee is a common signal of good faith and the fastest single filter there is.
  4. Bulk lead time, and how far does it stretch in peak season? The normal figure is not enough — ask what actually happens around Chinese New Year and in the September peak.
  5. What standard governs quality? Colour difference tolerance, dimensional tolerance, acceptable appearance defects. Few factories can hand you a written standard on the spot; the ones that can are usually the safer bet.
  6. Have you supplied my category? Do not ask whether they work for big brands — ask about the specific category and process: childrenswear tagless labels, chlorine-resistant care labels for swimwear.
  7. Which compliance documents can you supply? OEKO-TEX reports, FSC chain, recycled content figures, test reports. A supplier who can list them on the spot usually has tighter internal processes too.
  8. Can we visit the factory? The most important question and the most often skipped. A supplier happy to open the floor any time and one who is only warm on the stand are two different animals.

4. Samples and Quotes: Structure the Information or Lose It

  • Number every sample before it goes in the bag: booth number, date and a running number on the back of the sample or the bag. Do not rely on business cards — after three days and dozens of bags, the only thing that still matches is your own handwriting.
  • Six fields on every quote: which processes the unit price includes, whether tooling and die costs are extra, tax status, currency and exchange basis, payment terms, and validity. Miss one and you renegotiate later.
  • Shoot three kinds of photo: booth overview, macro detail of the product, and the pages of the process sample book. In the post-show review, one macro shot saves a round of back-and-forth.

5. The 30 Days After: Rhythm Decides the Deal

Show-floor enthusiasm evaporates within two weeks. What separates buyers is the three weeks after: days 1–3 to enter and grade the records (A can sample now, B needs data, C is backup only), days 4–7 to ship physical samples with a clear spec, week two to confirm the difficult process points, weeks three and four to compare quotes and sample results side by side. Without grading, thirty suppliers become thirty unanswered emails.

6. Beyond the Show: Online Sourcing and the Factory Visit

Shows happen a few times a year, so sourcing cannot depend on them alone. Once you find suppliers online, send the same spec sheet to three at a time — only then does price comparison mean anything. And once there is intent, one factory visit beats ten emails: see the equipment, the housekeeping, and whether someone else's goods are waiting to ship in the warehouse. We are in Humen, Dongguan, one to two hours by road from the Guangzhou and Shenzhen venues; many clients come straight from the show and settle samples, pricing and the production line in one trip.

💡 Practical tip: treat a show as round one of supplier screening, not as a place to collect quotes, and your whole approach changes. Round one does three things only: confirm the process is in-house, confirm MOQ and sampling terms, confirm they will let you into the factory. Fewer than three in ten pass all three — and only those are worth the time spent comparing price.
