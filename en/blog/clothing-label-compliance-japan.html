Japan Clothing Label Compliance: JIS Care Symbols & Composition

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 14, 2026

Japan is a high-margin market for apparel exports, but its labelling rules form a system of their own: care symbols follow JIS L 0001, composition and labeller details fall under the Household Goods Quality Labelling Act, and imported goods must also carry a country of origin. Many Chinese suppliers simply reuse a GB or ISO file for a Japanese order and get rejected at customs, inspection or on the marketplace.

This guide sets out the legal basis, the mandatory items, the differences between JIS care symbols and ISO/GB, how blend ratios should be written, and how hang tags, main labels and care labels share the compliance load — plus a checklist of the mistakes Chinese factories make most often.

1. Which Rules Govern Clothing Labels in Japan?

Japan's textile labelling requirements come from three layers rather than one law: (1) the Household Goods Quality Labelling Act and the fibre product labelling standards issued by the Consumer Affairs Agency, which fix the mandatory items; (2) JIS L 0001, Care labelling symbols for textiles, which defines the washing, bleaching, tumble drying, ironing and dry-cleaning symbols; and (3) country-of-origin rules for imported goods. Getting only one of the three right can still be ruled non-compliant.

3. How JIS Care Symbols Differ From ISO and GB

JIS L 0001 is based on ISO 3758, and most everyday symbols match China's GB/T 8685 — but the details are enough for a Japanese buyer to reject a sample:

  • Japan keeps symbol groups for wringing and for the drying method (line dry in shade, dry flat), which have no exact equivalent in ISO 3758;
  • Some newer ISO symbols are not adopted in Japan, so copying a European symbol chart produces marks the Japanese market does not recognise;
  • Japanese practice is to cover all five groups — washing, bleaching, tumble drying, ironing and dry cleaning — while GB allows selective use; omitting one group can trigger a complaint.
💡 Tip: for Japanese orders, ask the buyer for their current symbol list or a reference label, then we lay out the care label to JIS L 0001 — that avoids version clashes.

4. Writing Blend Ratios That Clear Inspection

The rule is simple: list fibres from highest to lowest content, with ratios adding up to 100%. Three practical notes:

  • Write out common blends such as 65/35 polyester-cotton in full — never just polyester-cotton;
  • Very small fibre shares may be grouped under a general category (such as other fibres), but the threshold follows the latest official notice — do not merge on gut feeling;
  • Ribbing, lining and decorative threads each need their own calculation — missed items here cause a good share of rejections.

5. Splitting the Job Between Durable Labels and Hang Tags

Japan requires care instructions to be durable — sewn in or firmly attached, and still legible after normal washing. Composition and labeller details may move to a hang tag or the packaging only when the product itself cannot carry them.

  • Care label: carries composition plus care symbols, usually sewn into the side seam or the back neck;
  • Main label (woven): brand mark and size; whether it also carries composition is the brand's call;
  • Hang tag: labeller details, country of origin, price and marketing — but it cannot replace the care symbols on the sewn label;
  • The combo: confirm main label, care label and hang tag as one set so compliance information is allocated in a single pass.

6. Six Mistakes Chinese Suppliers Make Most Often

  • Using a GB or ISO file straight away for a Japanese order, so symbols are missing or version-mismatched;
  • Origin written differently on the tag and the care label — 中国製 on one, Made in China on the other;
  • Listing the Chinese factory as labeller when the responsible party should be the Japanese importer or seller — never confirmed in advance;
  • Missing ribbing, lining or decorative thread in the blend ratio;
  • Using film-type care labels that fade or peel after a few washes, failing the durability requirement;
  • Machine-translating the Japanese copy, leaving unnatural wording that hurts the brand;

7. Build Compliance Into the Trim Package Once

For Japanese orders, confirm the trims as one package: main label, care label, hang tag and packaging sampled in the same round and driven by a single composition-and-symbol sheet — so nothing conflicts on arrival.
