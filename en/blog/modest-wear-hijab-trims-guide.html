Modest Wear Trims Guide for Hijab and Abaya

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 16, 2026

Hijabs, abayas, and open-front robes and kaftans sit in a category the trade calls modest wear. Its trim challenges are unusually concentrated: fabrics are typically chiffon, crepe chiffon, silk-like polyester or light cotton-linen, which drape, show through and snag easily; dressing movements are wide, so label position and edge finishing are felt immediately; and for Middle East and Southeast Asian markets, care labels and hang tags have to handle Arabic typesetting and local labelling rules. This guide separates the four pieces that matter — neck labels, care labels, hang tags and packaging.

1. Three Requirements Specific to This Category

  • Snagging and pull on light fabric: chiffon-type fabric cannot tolerate hard edges or coarse thread. Label edges must be cleanly cut or ultrasonically sealed, thread should be fine, and the stitch dense without being tight — so nothing puckers or waves after washing and no thread is pulled out of a hijab edge.
  • Show-through on semi-sheer fabric: the label body, thread colour and the reverse stitching all read through the outer layer. Match colours to the fabric, never put a dark label behind a pale abaya shell, and where necessary move the label to the lining layer or switch to printed marking.
  • Feel and propriety: hijabs and abayas are worn against skin and hair, so poor edge finishing rubs, irritates the scalp and makes fabric cling to the legs. The comfort bar is as high as for knit innerwear — do not choose trims on the basis of “it is hidden anyway”.

2. Neck and Care Labels: Stitching onto Flowing Fabric

Two labels that are trivial to sew into a heavy coat become a complaint source on a chiffon abaya. Position, material and stitching method have to be decided together; otherwise the sample looks fine and waves or show-through appear after two washes.

  • Position: centre back neck and side seam are the usual choices, but on semi-sheer fabric prefer the inside of a side seam or the inner facing of the front placket. For headscarves, avoid the centre back seam — that is exactly where the styling takes tension.
  • Material: use a thin soft woven label or a heat-transfer print; satin-woven labels reflect and show through on light fabric. For care labels, prefer soft coated fabric or thin tape over a stiff paper ticket.
  • Stitching: fold in all four edges or sew only the two sides and leave the ends free to reduce bulk; use fine thread matched to the fabric and a smaller needle. Sewing the care label and the neck label in two different places hides them better than stacking both in one spot.

3. Label Content: Bilingual Arabic and Information Order

  • Agree the language set first: for Middle East and Southeast Asian markets the usual pairings are Arabic with English, or Arabic with the local language. Follow the client's and the target market's current rules rather than reusing another market's layout.
  • Typesetting details: Arabic runs right to left, so in a bilingual layout the two text blocks need their own leading, size and alignment — Arabic cannot simply be dropped into the English text box; also check that numerals and composition percentages are not mirrored or broken across lines.
  • Order of information: keep composition, care instructions, size, origin and manufacturer as separate blocks, put symbols beside the wording, and lead with what the user most needs to read — size and care. Once translated, back-translate once to catch garbled word order or inconsistent terminology.

4. Details Specific to Hijabs and Undercaps

  • Size and identification: hijabs are usually sized by length and width, so mark inside the fold rather than outside; for orders in several shades and sizes, add an internal item number to the label so warehousing and returns can be checked.
  • Undercaps and elastic: for undercaps the elastic, anti-slip strip and binding decide comfort. Use narrow elastic with low recovery force and a thin, flat binding so no mark is left on the forehead.
  • Supporting trims on robes: place concealed snaps and hooks at the front placket away from where hands travel during prayer; and assess cuff and hem binding, vent reinforcement and tassel fixing against frequent dressing and frequent ironing.

5. Market Compliance and Seasonal Planning

  • Confirm labelling rules per destination: Gulf states generally have explicit requirements on label language, composition and manufacturer details and may involve pre-shipment conformity assessment. Check the current rules with the client before ordering; our Saudi Arabia and UAE compliance articles are a starting point.
  • Seasonal rhythm: Ramadan, Eid al-Fitr, Eid al-Adha and the Hajj period are the peaks for this category, with shipments concentrating one to two months ahead. Trims must be confirmed and stocked before the peak season queues up, or they get stuck in printing and weaving schedules.
  • Sizing and returns: size expression differs by market, and robes are often sold by length in centimetres rather than letters. Agree one expression between label, hang tag and e-commerce listing to cut returns caused by “not as described”.

6. Packaging and Gifting

  • One piece, one bag: light fabric marks and snags easily, so bag each piece with tissue inside and add a tear notch. Avoid staples or rigid clips, which leave permanent marks on the fabric.
  • Gifting and festive packaging: gifting rises sharply around Eid and other festivals, so bags, gift boxes and ribbon combinations should be sampled early. Adding a sustainability note or a multilingual greeting card inside the box costs little and is strongly remembered.
  • Avoid show-through and snagging: before packing, confirm the hang tag will not emboss a crease and the cord will not wrap around the fabric — fix the tag outside the bag or use a clear pocket where needed. Sequinned or embroidered styles need a separate protective sheet.
💡 Practical tip: returns and complaints in this category are rarely about workmanship — they are about show-through and puckered stitching. During sampling, hold the finished piece over a pale underlayer against strong light, then wash it twice and look again; that catches most of it.
