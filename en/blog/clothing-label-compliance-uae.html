UAE and Gulf Clothing Label Compliance: Arabic Labels and GSO Rules

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 24, 2026

The UAE is the main gateway for apparel imports and re-exports in the Middle East, and Dubai's re-export network reaches the Gulf, Africa and South Asia. Labelling here does not follow the European or American model: Arabic is not a nice extra but a hard requirement at customs and in market surveillance, and textiles must also satisfy the Gulf Standardization Organization (GSO) labelling technical regulations. This guide covers the mandatory information items, the practical limits of Arabic on a woven label, the care-symbol system, how the UAE differs from its neighbours, the six most common clearance and retail-check problems, and a sample approval checklist.

Order of the guide: the labelling logic of the Gulf market, mandatory information items, Arabic layout and weaving constraints, symbols plus wording, differences between the UAE and its neighbours, common clearance problems, and a sample checklist.

1. The Gulf Labelling Logic: Arabic Is a Hard Requirement

UAE consumer protection law requires goods and the information relating to them to be provided in Arabic — other languages may appear alongside, but a foreign language alone is not enough. Textile labels are also governed by Gulf Standardization Organization (GSO) technical regulations, and import, storage and retail are checked by the Ministry of Industry and Advanced Technology (MoIAT), municipal authorities and customs. The practical consequence is blunt: garments carrying English only can be pulled for correction, over-labelled, or returned, while goods entering free zones or moving on for re-export usually have to satisfy the destination country as well.

💡 Tip: these technical regulations get updated and enforcement varies between emirates and sales channels. Before bulk production, ask your UAE importer to confirm the current requirements from the authority's latest notice — our job is then to place that confirmed wording precisely on the label.

2. The Minimum Information on a Garment

Item What is required Where it usually sits
Fibre content Listed in descending order, in Arabic alongside English; spot checks run quantitative verification Neck or side label
Washing and care Care symbols plus Arabic wording; the symbol system generally follows ISO 3758 Care label
Country of origin Must be explicit — a city name alone is not enough — and usually appears in Arabic too Neck, side or care label
Importer or agent details Name and address, as the party responsible for clearance and recalls Care label or tag back
Size Consistent with local retail practice — centimetres or letter sizes, never two systems mixed Size label
Barcode Retail channels require it to scan and to fit their inventory system; prefix and registration follow their rules Hang tag

3. The Practical Difficulty of Arabic on Labels

  • Opposite direction: Arabic runs right to left, so mixing it with English on one line easily scrambles the order or pushes punctuation to the wrong end. Give Arabic its own block.
  • Size and joined letters: at small sizes the strokes merge, especially on a woven label. Keep the letter height at 1.5 mm or more, and switch to a printed care label or satin weave if needed.
  • English abbreviations do not carry over: MACH WASH or DO NOT BLEACH mean little to an Arabic reader — use full wording or symbols instead.
  • Numerals: Arabic-language material mixes Western and Eastern Arabic numerals, and retail channels usually prefer Western. Confirm before artwork is signed off.
  • Never sign off on machine translation: a mistranslated garment term creates a compliance argument. Have a native speaker proof it and approve the artwork before weaving starts.

4. Care Symbols and Wording: You Need Both Tracks

Care symbols handle cross-language recognition; Arabic wording satisfies legal readability. Neither replaces the other. For multi-market exports, a common care-label build is symbols plus two lines of text — Arabic and English: symbols follow the international system (generally ISO 3758), and the wording varies by market. When label space runs out, keep fibre content, care instructions and country of origin complete and move the rest to the back of the hang tag.

5. How the UAE Differs From Its Neighbours

Market Key points of enforcement
United Arab Emirates MoIAT conformity schemes plus joint checks by municipalities and customs; label checks before shelf placement are strict and correction or over-labelling is common
Saudi Arabia Regulated by SASO; the importer obtains a product certificate of conformity through the SABER platform; Arabic labelling is required with explicit importer details
Other GCC members Broadly follow GSO technical regulations; the details depend on the local importer and customs — confirm once and reuse across markets where possible
Re-export and free zones Dubai re-exports often have to carry the destination country's markings — a two-stage approach with neutral packing plus destination labels works well

6. The Six Most Common Clearance and Retail Check Problems

  • English-only labelling with no Arabic
  • Fibre content that disagrees with the test result, or is not in descending order
  • Missing country of origin, or a city name only, or placed on the inner bag rather than the product
  • No importer or agent name and address
  • Vague care wording — hand wash only, or symbols with no words at all
  • A barcode that will not scan or does not match the retailer's item file

7. Sample Approval Checklist

  • Where the Arabic text comes from — importer or our typesetting — and who signs it off
  • Character and line limits of the label: what moves to the tag back if it overflows
  • Letter height and technique: 1.5 mm or more on woven labels, printed care labels if needed
  • Composition matching the test report, with the order of percentages correct
  • Importer details: left blank, over-labelled on arrival or printed from the start
  • Shipping documents: do the certificate of conformity, invoice and origin details match the label line by line
💡 Tip: rework on an Arabic label costs far more than on any other language — one wrong word and the whole woven batch is scrap. At the sample stage, confirm twice: photograph the physical label and have a native speaker read it back. Then archive the final artwork so repeat orders reuse it instead of re-typesetting.
