How Many Hang Tags to Order: Per-Garment Count, Spares and Reprint Cost

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 20, 2026

Getting the hang tag quantity wrong is expensive in both directions. Too few and the last production days have nothing to attach: the shipment either waits or leaves unlabelled, and an unlabelled shipment is a compliance problem rather than a cost problem. Too many and a stack of tags printed with last season's collection name goes to scrap. This breaks the number into four additions and one multiplier, and works one example end to end: a 10,000-piece order that ends up at 12,000 tags.

1. Start From Garment Pieces, Not From the Tag Price

The usual mistake is to look at the tag price first and buy up to the minimum order quantity. A hang tag is a component of a garment: its ceiling is set by how many garments you are making, not by the printer's price ladder. The right order is to multiply garment pieces by tags per garment, add spares and fixed uses to get total demand, and only then compare that total against the supplier's price breaks to decide whether to step up one level. Reverse the order and you buy 20,000 tags for 8,000 garments.

2. Tags per Garment: Single, Sets and Language Versions

Arrangement Tags per piece Notes
Single main tag 1 Most common; basics and quick reorders
Tag + content card + price ticket 2–3 For layered branding or department stores; strung together in one pass
One tag per language market Ordered separately per market One multi-language tag works in the EU; Japan and Korea expect single-language tags

The size ratio does not change the total count, it changes how you split cartons and sizes. For 2,000 pieces split S to XL at 2:4:3:1, the tags themselves are usually size-agnostic unless the size is printed on them, but every size carton still needs its own count. In practice, pack tags at total pieces times 1.02 into the master carton and split them by size ratio into the inner bags: simpler than counting per carton, and no size ends up short.

3. Spare Rate: What 3, 5 and 8 Percent Actually Cover

  • 3 percent: production loss only. Tags torn while stringing, split at the hole, creased in packing or re-attached at the factory. Fine for low-cost basics on stable styles where a small reprint is acceptable.
  • 5 percent: adds re-labelling and rework. Showroom samples, buyer changes that need re-tagging, rejects picked out at QC and re-labelled. This is the usual value for export orders and the default we recommend.
  • 8 to 10 percent: multi-warehouse, e-commerce and live-selling returns. Every return means re-tagging, and transfers between stores and warehouses consume tags too. Above a 20 percent return rate, take the full 10 percent.
  • Plus a fixed 100 to 500 tags that have nothing to do with piece count: sampling, photo shoots, live-stream sets, press mailings, store display and retained reference samples. Estimate these by activity, not by order size.

4. The Cost of Running Short: The Tag Carries Mandatory Information

The spare rate is not a comfort margin, it is a compliance buffer. A hang tag usually carries the brand, the collection, the fibre composition, the country of origin and a barcode, and much of that is mandatory information. In the EU the fibre composition is declared under Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011, and the General Product Safety Regulation (EU) 2023/988 requires traceability and an economic operator established in the Union. In the United States, FTC rules at 16 CFR Part 303 require fibre content and country of origin. Japan requires composition and handling instructions under the Household Goods Quality Labelling Act, and Korea requires composition and origin on an attached label under its safety control act for household textiles. A garment without its tag cannot ship compliantly; it waits. One week of storage and penalty costs far more than 5 percent of the tags.

Weight belongs in the freight quote. A 55 × 90 mm tag at 350 g/m² weighs about 1.7 g, so 10,000 tags are roughly 17 kg, or 21 kg with strings and inner bags, usually two cartons. On air freight charged by volumetric weight, a difference of that size can eat half the discount from stepping up a price break.

5. Over-Order or Reprint: Three Numbers Settle It

The hidden cost of a reprint is usually underestimated. It has four parts: plates and the die, where a surviving die means only the printing plates are charged; the minimum run, which at most suppliers is 3,000 to 5,000 pieces per design, so topping up 300 pieces can cost up to three times the unit price of 3,000; lead time, seven to twelve working days plus freight; and colour drift, because a new paper and ink lot commonly lands at a Delta E of 1.5 to 2.5, which a premium buyer will see. The decision rule is therefore simple: if the probability of needing a reprint is above about 30 percent, step up one price break, but keep the extra quantity under 15 percent. Beyond that it is inventory, not insurance.

Situation Recommendation
Basics, artwork frozen, supplier keeps the die Take 3 percent; do not chase a price break
Export orders with QC and re-tagging Take 5 percent and step to the nearest break, capped at plus 15 percent
Multi-warehouse e-commerce, live selling, high returns Take 10 percent and do not step up; the artwork may change first

6. Scheduling and Storage: New Year, Sea Freight and Humidity

A hang tag looks like a small item, but it is the last operation before a garment can ship, and without it nothing leaves. Three dates matter. Chinese New Year shuts factories for two to three weeks and the four weeks before it are the busiest window for printing and logistics, so tags should be in hand 45 to 60 days before the garment delivery date. Sea freight to Europe or the Americas adds another 30 to 35 days, and if tags and garments are made in different places, allow three to five days of domestic transfer. In storage, tags fear moisture rather than cold: keep them at 20 ± 5 °C and 50 ± 10 percent relative humidity, on pallets 100 mm off the floor, reseal opened cartons with desiccant and work first in, first out. Damp tags curl and soften at the string hole, and together with grain direction that decides how flat they finally sit.

7. One Worked Example You Can Copy

Item Quantity How
Garment pieces 10,000 Order quantity
Tags per piece 1 Single main tag
Spares at 5 percent 500 10,000 × 5 percent
Fixed uses 100 60 samples plus 40 for shoot and display
Total demand 10,600 Plus 1 percent in-plant loss, 10,700
Actually order 12,000 Nearest break is 12,000, an 11 percent overshoot inside the 15 percent cap

One last check that gets skipped: whether the mandatory information on the tag still matches the finished garment. A changed fibre content, a new fabric mill or a new country of origin means new artwork, which is why the spare rate should not be pushed to 20 percent by reflex. Brands that revise styles often are better off keeping spares lean, treating reprints as routine, keeping the die on file and recording paper and ink lots in the specification so the colour drift risk on a reprint comes down.
