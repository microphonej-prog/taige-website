Laser Engraved Hang Tags: What the Beam Can and Cannot Do

By TAGE Packaging · Updated November 17, 2026

A laser-engraved hang tag is cut and marked by a focused beam instead of ink and a steel die, so there is no printing plate, no colour approval and no die cost. For a small brand or a short run it looks like the easy choice. It is also easy to get wrong: board scorches, leather shrinks, and the edge of a 2 mm plywood tag looks like a burn mark if nobody set the power. This guide covers what each material tolerates, the tolerances to write into the purchase order, and the quantity above which laser becomes more expensive than die cutting.

1. Why Laser: No Ink, No Die, No Colour Approval

A laser does exactly two things: vaporises material (engraving) or separates it (cutting). There is no ink film on the surface, so abrasion and crocking tests are not the issue, and there is no Pantone approval, because the colour is simply the colour of the burnt or vaporised substrate. That removes the two slowest loops in hang tag production, colour drawdown and die fabrication, which is why a laser tag ships in five to seven days while a foil-stamped tag usually needs two to three weeks. The trade-off is one tone per material, so no four-colour brand mark.

2. Material Limits and Usable Thickness

Substrate Usable thickness What the beam gives you Watch out for
Plywood / MDF 2–4 mm dark brown engraved face, clean cut edge MDF resin gives an uneven tone; use plywood for consistency
Solid wood veneer 1.5–3 mm warm tone, natural variation grain shifts darkness across the batch; knots burn deeper
Vegetable-tanned leather 1.2–2.0 mm caramel to dark brown chrome-tanned leather smells acrid; surface finishes burn white
Acrylic (PMMA) 2–3 mm frosted white mark, glossy edge the cut edge flame-polishes; cast and extruded sheet behave differently
Coated paperboard 350–450 g/m² 0.4–0.6 mm cuts well, engraves badly the coating chars and flakes; switch to uncoated board if you need engraving
Kraft board 0.5–0.8 mm brown burn edge the smoke smell lingers for days; air it properly

3. Kerf, Bridges and Tolerances

The beam does not cut a hairline. A 60–80 W CO₂ laser removes about 0.1–0.2 mm of material on each side of the path, so a nominal 3.0 mm hole cuts at roughly 3.2 mm. The drawing should state substrate, thickness, a ±0.3 mm tolerance on the outside profile, and a minimum 1.5 mm bridge between the engraved area and the cut edge; anything thinner will snap on a 2 mm wooden tag. String holes should be at least 3.5 mm so two strands of 2 mm waxed cord pass through, and 4 mm or more if you plan to use a plastic fastener.

  • Engraving depth: 0.15–0.4 mm already reads solid on wood; deeper just widens the burnt edge
  • Kerf: 0.1–0.2 mm per side at 60–80 W, narrower with a smaller nozzle
  • Position tolerance: ±0.3 mm within a sheet; ±0.5 mm on 600 mm sheets run without a jig
  • Minimum feature: 0.8 mm stroke for small type, no font below 6 pt, or the letters fill in

4. Charring, Smell and What Buyers Actually Complain About

Two complaints come back from the retail floor: the tag smells of smoke, and the cut edge marks the garment. Both are process problems, not material problems. Charring is reduced by air assist and a second low-power pass; the smoke smell is reduced by extraction during cutting plus 24 hours of airing before packing. The fastest in-house test is to seal ten tags in a poly bag for 24 hours and open it: if the smell is still obvious, the batch needs longer airing. Edge transfer is easier still: press the cut edge on white cotton for ten seconds and look for a mark.

5. The Compliance Points That Actually Get Checked

Laser marking adds no ink, so heavy metals and phthalates from ink films drop out of the conversation; what remains is the substrate. Leather and wood on a product sold in the EU must still meet the restricted substances list of REACH (EC) No 1907/2006, and the tag has to identify the responsible economic operator under the General Product Safety Regulation (EU) 2023/988. France adds a readability requirement: composition and care information must stay legible under the Code de la consommation, so a laser mark is approved on a physical sample, not on screen. Japan works the other way round: the Household Goods Quality Labelling Act requires fibre composition, handling instructions and country of origin to appear together, so brands repeat that information in Japanese on the hang tag, and laser-engraved Japanese needs a 0.8 mm minimum stroke or the kanji fill in. In Korea, an eco-process claim carries a burden of proof under the Act on Fair Labelling and Advertising, so no ink does not by itself make a tag environmentally friendly.

6. Cost and the Crossover Point

Laser has no tooling cost but it has machine time, and machine time scales with engraved area and the number of passes. A 60 × 30 mm plywood tag engraved 0.3 mm deep runs at roughly 1.5–3 seconds per pass; at 20 pieces per sheet and two passes, a 3,000-piece order is several hours of machine time, which is why laser is usually quoted per piece rather than per order. Dies are the opposite: a few hundred dollars to make, but the per-piece price collapses above 5,000 pieces. In practice the crossover for a simple two-colour tag sits between 3,000 and 6,000 pieces depending on size; below it, laser, above it, die cutting plus offset printing.

Nine lines for the purchase order: substrate and thickness, engraved side, engraving depth, cut profile with the ±0.3 mm tolerance, kerf, hole diameter, minimum bridge, edge finish (sanded or raw burn), and the airing period before packing.

7. Acceptance: Ten Tags From Each Position

  1. Dimensions: ten tags each from the start, middle and end of the run, measured with a caliper; log every reading outside 0.3 mm
  2. Colour transfer: press the cut edge on white cotton for ten seconds; no visible transfer allowed
  3. Smell: after 24 hours of airing, seal in a bag for another 24 hours; no obvious smoke smell on opening
  4. Hole strength: thread a 2 mm waxed cord and pull at 5 kg for one minute without tearing
  5. Artwork: the 0.8 mm minimum stroke still opens up at 100% view, and Japanese or Chinese strokes are not filled in
