Static Control in Apparel Packaging: Dust on Dark Garments and Clinging Bags

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 26, 2026

A dark garment wiped once on the inspection table leaves a white smear, an OPP bag will not open, small stickers cling to the guide rail instead of dropping — on the floor these are rarely quality defects. They are static. The higher the synthetic content, the drier the air and the faster the packing line, the more visible it becomes. Static changes no physical property of the garment, yet it turns a conforming order into foreign-matter complaints, rework for lint picking and a stopped packing line.

What follows covers four things: where the charge comes from, how it actually shows up on trims and packaging, what to do on the material side and on the shop floor, and how to write the requirement into a specification and verify it.

1. Where the Charge Comes From: Three Conditions at Once

Static needs friction or separation to generate charge, a material that will not readily dissipate it, and air dry enough to stop natural leakage. Remove any one condition and the problem disappears — which explains why the same packaging behaves fine in a humid southern summer and starts failing in a northern winter or an air-conditioned plant.

  • Friction and separation: tearing a bag open, pulling a garment out, dragging fabric across a table, peeling a label off its liner — all instant separations where charge has no time to leak away
  • Material: polyester, nylon, PP and PE have high surface resistance and store charge; cotton, viscose, paper and nonwoven dissipate more readily — one reason a paper bag is calmer around dark garments
  • Environment: below roughly 40% relative humidity the air stops helping charge leak away; northern winters and heavily air-conditioned plants commonly sit at 25 to 35%
  • Speed: automatic bag opening, vacuum pick-and-place and high-speed labelling separate materials in milliseconds and build charge faster than hands do

2. Four Ways It Shows Up, and Why It Pays to Fix

The awkward part is that static never appears in an inspection report: the fabric passes, colour fastness passes, measurements pass, and the buyer still opens the carton to a bloom of white fibre. The complaint targets the garment; the source may be the bag.

SymptomWhat it looks likeConsequence
Dust pickup on dark garmentsA white smear under the lamp, short fibres lining the inside of the bagForeign-matter complaints, lot-wide lint picking
Bag mouth clingingThe opener cannot grip, or pulls two bags at once; operators spend time rubbing them apartSlower line, stoppages and extra labour
Small labels scatteringStickers cling to the rail, fingers and bench instead of landing in placeMisplaced or missing labels, rework
Foil and laminated surfaces picking up chipsStacked tags stick together and scratch when separatedSurface scratches and manual separation

3. Material Side: Four Levers, Cheap to Thorough

Anti-static is not just a sprayed additive. Ranked by durability and cost there are four routes, and real projects usually combine them: masterbatch film for bags, humidity on the floor, and paper or nonwoven for dark, high-sensitivity categories.

  • Masterbatch in the film: anti-static masterbatch compounded into PE or PP at extrusion; it migrates to the surface and forms a leakage path — the longest-lived option, reaching a surface resistance in the 10⁹ to 10¹¹ ohm range
  • Surface coating: cheapest and fastest to show an effect, but rubbed and wiped away — fine for single-use inner bags, wrong for finished bags stored for months
  • Substitute the material: paper bags, nonwoven and tissue dissipate quickly. For dark, sensitive categories such as suiting and coats, paper packaging is often simpler than engineering an anti-static film
  • Humidity is the cheapest anti-static: hold 45 to 55% RH and most floor problems vanish; a hygrometer costs very little compared with changing material

4. Shop Floor: Four Actions

Getting the material right is not enough; the floor has to cooperate. The four actions below run from cheapest to most expensive, and most plants solve around 80% of complaints with the first two. Ionising equipment is costly, so put it at the most sensitive stations — labelling and separation — rather than across the whole plant.

  • Control and log humidity: target 45 to 55%, with a meter and a record. Judging humidity by feel is the same as not managing it
  • Tack roller plus angled light: roll each dark garment before packing and inspect under raking light — the cheapest action with the most direct effect on foreign-matter complaints
  • Ionising fans and static bars: mount 20 to 40 cm in front of the bag-opening, labelling and separation stations, covering the actual work zone rather than the aisle
  • Grounding and workwear: ground the bench, avoid synthetic gloves and uniforms, and stop rubbing foam or bubble wrap at the station

5. Writing It Into the Spec: Three Verifiable Tests

Anti-static is measurable; most orders simply never specify it. The result: when trouble hits, the supplier says the bag has always been like this and the factory says the floor has always been like this, and nobody owns it. Run one comparison test at sampling and fix the conclusion in writing.

MethodWhat it measuresWhere it fits
Surface resistanceHow readily film and bag surfaces leak chargeTo ASTM D257 or IEC 62631; 10⁹ to 10¹¹ ohm is the anti-static band
Charge decayHow fast charge dissipates, as a half-lifeFabric, nonwoven and bags; closer to real use than surface resistance
Bench comparisonBag-opening feel, chip attraction and double-pick rateA/B comparison at sampling; intuitive but not quantifiable, so use it as support only

6. Three Traps

  • Anti-static is not ESD shielding: shielding bags are for electronics, need 10⁴ to 10⁶ ohm and a metallised layer, and cost far more; specifying them for garments spends money without solving anything
  • Additives migrate: a coated anti-static can react with inks and laminating adhesive, mottling the print and weakening bond strength. Test compatibility before the run, not after printing
  • Over-humidifying breeds mould: push past 60% and static improves while mould pressure jumps. On sea-freight orders keep the ceiling near 55% and budget it together with moisture control
The thing about static is that it never shows up in an inspection report — only in the buyer's unboxing video. Money spent on a hygrometer and a tack roller is usually an order of magnitude less than replacing a batch of packaging.
