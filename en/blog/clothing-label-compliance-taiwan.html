Taiwan Apparel Labeling: Chinese Wording, Care Symbols and Factory Checks

By TAIGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 9, 2026

Taiwan is a small market that imports a steady volume of apparel, and its retail culture reads labels closely: high brand density, fine-grained channels, and shoppers who compare care instructions the way European buyers compare fibre content. A garment label there is not decoration. Under the Commodity Labelling Act, apparel must follow the Ministry of Economic Affairs' apparel labelling standard and state the product name, fibre composition, size, care method, country of origin and supplier details, written legibly in Traditional Chinese. The rejections we see are rarely about fabric: a missing item, a wrong blend ratio, or care symbols that promise more than the fabric can take.

This guide covers five things: exactly which items belong on the label, how to set Traditional Chinese type, how to choose care symbols, whether to print measurements in cm, and who owns the importer details. It ends with a pre-production checklist you can tick through, and the way to combine labels when one style also ships to Japan, Korea and the EU.

1. The five items every label must carry

Item What is required Most common failure
Product name and fibre content Fibres listed from highest to lowest share, blends as percentages Writing only 'cotton blend' with no ratio
Size A size code plus cm measurements so the shopper can judge fit An 'M' with no body measurements
Care method Symbols plus wording, both matching what the fabric actually survives Stamping 40 °C machine wash on everything
Country of origin Country level, matching the customs paperwork Label says Vietnam, packing list says China
Supplier or importer details Name, phone and address — one missing makes it incomplete Only a brand website, no address or phone

Two layout details are routinely missed. First, Traditional Chinese is the required language: simplified characters get flagged as errors at retail inspection in Taiwan, especially pairs like 纖 and 纤. Second, type height has to match label width: on a 3 cm wide neck label, Chinese below 1.2 mm blurs, and after three washes it is unreadable — we keep Chinese at 1.5 mm minimum and Latin text at 1.2 mm.

2. Care symbols: use the five ISO 3758 families, do not freehand them

Taiwan uses the ISO 3758 care symbol family, in five groups: washing (a tub), bleaching (a triangle), drying (a square with a circle or lines inside), ironing (an iron) and professional dry cleaning (a circle). The numbers and dots are limits, not decoration: a 30 in the tub means 30 °C maximum, two dots on the iron mean 150 °C, and the P or F inside the circle names the solvent class. When a German buyer asks for a Pflegeetikett, we print the same symbols and only swap the wording into German — the pictograms travel.

  • Printing 40 °C when the fabric only survives 30 °C: the garment shrinks, prints crack, and the claim lands on the seller.
  • An empty bleach triangle allows any bleach; on a blend with elastane it should carry a cross.
  • A P inside the dry-clean circle when local cleaners mostly run F solvents — not a compliance defect, a complaint waiting to happen.
  • Symbols under 8 mm or drawn with hairlines blur after washing; spacing between symbols matters too, or inspection misreads them.

3. Sizing: put centimetres next to the code

Shoppers in Taiwan read S/M/L fluently, but an M from one brand can differ by 4 cm in chest from another — a letter alone pushes the judgement onto the customer. Give the code plus centimetres: chest, waist and body length, each labelled as flat measurement or circumference, with the tolerance stated (we normally print ±1 cm). When the same block goes to Japan, the size code shifts to Japanese sizing with cm alongside, and that mapping has to be fixed at sampling — not discovered in the packing room.

4. Importer details: customs does not ask, retail always does

Export clearance looks at the product description, HS code and value; the supplier block on a label is consumer information. The two get conflated. Taiwanese importers are not asked for the label at customs, but the pre-listing audit checks name, phone and address item by item — all three must belong to one responsible entity, and one missing makes the label incomplete. The fix is simple: have the customer confirm the name and address on letterhead during sampling, freeze it in the artwork file, and print every reorder from that same record so the address does not drift when purchasing staff change.

Incomplete or inaccurate labelling can be penalised under the Commodity Labelling Act, with a deadline to correct and repeat penalties if it is missed. The expensive part is elsewhere: a shipment that reaches the retail warehouse with a wrong blend ratio needs relabelling or a return — rework usually costs more than a hundred times the price difference of one label. Our rule is simple: ask one extra question rather than absorb one extra claim.

5. Four things to lock before bulk production

  • A labelling sheet: product name, fibre percentages, size, care method, origin and supplier details listed line by line, signed before platemaking.
  • A signed sample: keep one physical label with date and version number; that is what settles a dispute later, not a chat log.
  • First-article inspection: pull 20 pieces from the first bulk run, check type height, position and stitch strength, and confirm the care symbols match the fabric test report.
  • Post-wash re-check: wash three pieces three times as labelled and see whether the print survives and the edges fray — the step most often skipped, and the one that causes claims.

6. One style for Taiwan, Japan, Korea and the EU: stack the four label sets

Four markets do not need four labels sewn in — that raises sewing cost, scratches necks and pushes the return rate up. The workable answer is a folded label: one woven tape folded into two or three plies, each ply carrying one market's text, sewn into the side seam so the shopper reads whichever ply applies. Traditional Chinese for Taiwan, Japanese for Japan and Korean for Korea each take a ply, while the EU ply carries English plus French. One label then covers four markets at a fraction of the extra cost, with no change to the sewing operation.

  • Japan: the Household Goods Quality Labelling Act requires composition, blend ratio, care symbols and country of origin in Japanese, with Japanese size codes alongside cm.
  • Korea: quality information is required under the Electrical Appliances and Consumer Products Safety Control Act — fibre blend ratio, origin and precautions — with Korean sizing or S/M/L plus cm.
  • European Union: fibre composition follows Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011 in the local language, and the General Product Safety Regulation GPSR (EU) 2023/988 requires a responsible economic operator inside the EU; German-speaking markets add the Textilkennzeichnungsgesetz (TKG) and their Pflegeetikett conventions.

7. Three questions we get every week

First, can an English-only label ship into Taiwan? No: the statutory language is Chinese, English may sit alongside but cannot replace it. Second, can care be given as words without symbols? In practice symbols plus words is the safest combination, because word-only labels get read as unclear at retail inspection. Third, can origin read 'designed in Taiwan, made in Vietnam'? The origin line must name the manufacturing country; the design location can be added separately, but the two must not share one line or both customs and retail will object. These three come up every year — written into the labelling template they cost nothing.
