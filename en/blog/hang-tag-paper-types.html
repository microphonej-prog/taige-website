Hang Tag Paper Types: Art Paper, Kraft, Cotton & PVC — How to Choose

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated August 16, 2026

Seven-tenths of a hangtag's perceived quality comes from its paper. The first thing a shopper touches is the tag; its thickness, stiffness, gloss and texture all speak for your brand. With art paper, kraft, cotton, PVC and more to choose from, how do you pick the right hang tag paper? This guide breaks down each material's properties, weights and best uses.

1. Define Your Brand's Vibe First

Material is an extension of your brand language: minimal luxury suits thick cotton or textured paper, bright fashion suits high-grammage art paper, natural and eco suits kraft, and waterproof practical uses call for PVC. Set the tone first, then choose the paper — this saves endless revisions and re-sampling.

2. Art Paper: The Mainstream Standard

Art paper is smooth, takes ink evenly and reproduces colors vividly — the most common hangtag material. Standard weights run 250–400gsm: 250–300gsm suits high-volume fast fashion, 300–350gsm balances stiffness and value, and 350gsm+ feels like a card, ideal for mid-to-high-end brands. Matte lamination adds tactility; gloss lamination boosts color and durability.

3. Kraft & Recycled Paper: Natural and Eco

Kraft paper keeps the pulp's natural color with a rugged, organic texture — a classic match with cotton or jute string, ideal for organic cotton, outdoor and 'slow fashion' brands. Recycled paper is made from recovered fibers, slightly darker with a natural grain; paired with soy-based inks and no lamination, it tells a clear eco story. Note that kraft absorbs ink strongly, so dark prints can show mottling — plan your design accordingly.

4. Cotton & Textured Paper: The Premium Choice

Cotton paper contains cotton fiber — soft, warm and breathable to the touch. Paired with foil stamping or embossing, it is the classic choice for luxury brands. Textured paper (linen, canvas or leather embossed) gains a dimensional surface and a premium hand feel. Both cost more than art paper, but deliver the clearest brand premium.

5. PVC & Specialty Materials: Waterproof and Tough

PVC tags are waterproof, fold-resistant and hard to tear — suited to swimwear, outdoor gear, tools and kids' products. They are usually die-cut and printed with lamination, available in clear, frosted or holographic finishes. As environmental rules tighten, more brands switch from PVC to degradable PET or rPET.

7. Grammage and Caliper: Reading the Spec Sheet

Grammage (gsm, g/m²) measures weight per square metre, not thickness. At the same 300 gsm, art paper runs about 0.30–0.33 mm, kraft about 0.40–0.45 mm and cotton about 0.45–0.55 mm, because pulp density and calendering differ. When specifying paper, confirm both the grammage and the measured caliper, and ask the supplier for actual readings instead of relying on the gsm figure alone.

Single-ply stock above 400 gsm tends to crack at die-cutting and creasing, so converters usually move to two-ply mounting or greyboard. Mounting adds a process step and cost, but delivers cleaner edges and better rigidity, which is why department-store and travel-retail brands often choose it.

8. Surface Finishes vs Paper: Combinations That Fail

  • Kraft plus gloss lamination: uneven fibre absorption causes bubbles and lifted corners; matte lamination or a water-based varnish is more stable.
  • Cotton or textured paper with large solid dark areas: the open fibre lets ink strike through to the reverse; reduce ink lay or switch to spot colour in smaller areas.
  • Art paper with deep embossing: short fibres crack under heavy pressure; pre-crease first or move to textured stock.
  • PVC or PET with foil stamping: low surface energy means corona treatment and a dedicated foil are required, otherwise the foil rubs off.
  • Recycled paper with four-colour process: the greyish base limits colour fidelity; one or two colours plus a spot colour is far more predictable.

Decide in this order: printing method, then surface finish, then post-press. Reversing it is the usual reason for lost time — choosing foil first and only then discovering the paper cannot take the heat and pressure, which costs a week of re-sampling.

9. Compliance Questions Buyers Ask

Chemicals: EU REACH (EC 1907/2006) caps concentrations of substances of very high concern (SVHC) in articles, and paper, inks and adhesives all fall within scope. For childrenswear tags, add EN 71-3 heavy-metal migration testing; for food or cosmetics gift packaging, a food-contact declaration is required.

Packaging and recycling: EU PPWR (EU 2025/40) requires recyclable packaging and rising recycled content. A paper tag is recyclable as paper, but lamination, foil, stickers and hole position affect how it is actually sorted. If your client files LUCID/VerpackG in Germany, confirm at quotation stage whether tags count as packaging, so buyer and seller report the same way.

Forest certification: if the brand needs an FSC claim (FSC Mix or FSC Recycled), state it on the order, keep the supplier's licence number (starting with FSC-C) on file, and put the FSC claim wording on the invoice at shipment. Without that line, the brand cannot print the FSC label.

On export documents, hang tags usually ship with the garments and are not declared separately. If the client wants tags bagged, invoiced or declared on their own, confirm the HS code, origin and packing-list mapping up front, so clearance is not delayed by missing paperwork on arrival.

10. Paper Selection and Sampling: A Six-Step Checklist

  1. Set the brand tone first, then narrow to two or three candidate papers instead of a dozen.
  2. Check grammage and measured caliper together; a shortfall in either weakens stiffness.
  3. Fix the printing method (offset, digital, screen) and surface finish before discussing post-press.
  4. Sample the same design on two or three papers and compare stiffness, colour and feel by hand.
  5. Run fold and rub tests to mimic transport vibration and repeated handling in store.
  6. Sign off a golden sample before mass production and put paper, grammage, colour reference and finishes in one spec sheet so no factory interprets it differently.

11. Frequently Asked Questions

What grammage should I choose — is 300 gsm enough?

300 gsm is the general-purpose answer: enough stiffness, stable die-cutting and sensible cost for most garment production. For department stores or higher price points, 350–400 gsm feels closer to a card and gives more presence on unboxing; for childrenswear and fast fashion, 250–300 gsm is cheaper and feeds through tagging guns and automatic packing lines more easily.

What do sampling fees and minimum order quantities look like?

Sampling is usually charged by process: digital samples are inexpensive, while foil, embossing and custom die shapes cost extra and are often credited against the bulk order. MOQ depends on process: plain printing usually starts at 1,000–3,000 pieces, while foil, embossing or die-cut shapes start at 3,000–5,000. The smaller the run, the more plate and set-up cost each piece carries — that is the real reason small orders cost more per unit.

What drives the price of a hang tag?

Four variables: paper and grammage, printing method and colour count, finishes and post-press, and quantity. Paper and process set the price band; quantity sets the amortisation. In practice, doubling the run usually drops the unit price one step, while changing paper or adding foil can raise it a whole level. So fix paper and process first, then talk volume — not the other way round.

Which eco options exist for hang tag paper — and are they recyclable?

The usual combination is FSC-certified paper with plant-based inks and a water-based varnish, or recycled paper with no lamination. Recyclability depends on the composite structure, not the paper alone: uncoated paper recycles best, while heavy lamination, full-surface foil and double-sided tape can reclassify a tag as composite material with much lower recovery value. To go green, cut lamination and metallic area first — changing the paper alone is not enough.

12. Paper Choices by Product Category

  • Denim and workwear: 300–350 gsm kraft with cotton string and one-colour print; hard-wearing and true to the rugged look.
  • Childrenswear: 250–300 gsm art paper, rounded die-cut corners and water-based inks to avoid sharp edges and heavy-metal risk.
  • Swimwear and outdoor: PVC or rPET, water- and crease-resistant, with punched holes and plastic barbs that shrug off humidity.
  • Bridal and eveningwear: cotton or textured stock at 300 gsm or more with foil or debossing; the point is touch and how it catches light.
  • E-commerce fast fashion: 250 gsm art paper printed both sides — low cost, stringing can be automated, ideal for big runs and frequent style changes.

13. Sample Approval: How to Tell the Paper Is Right

When the sample arrives, do three things: hold it to the light to check for mottling (uneven coating), fold it 180 degrees by hand to look for white stress marks, and carry it in a pocket or bag for a day to see whether edges fuzz. These three steps filter out most papers unfit for production far better than reading the grammage.

  • Colour: place the sample next to the Pantone chip or the digital proof and check it under both daylight and interior light; it passes only if it holds in both.
  • Stiffness: hold same-size samples vertically and compare the droop angle against the approved sample — a mismatch signals batch-to-batch variation.
  • Edges: no fuzz, cracked colour or paper dust at die-cut and crease lines — dust builds up and jams automatic packing machines.
  • Adhesion: run a tape-pull test; print and lamination must not lift in sheets, only the faintest surface transfer is acceptable.

One more step is often missed: storage and transit. Paper takes on waves when it absorbs moisture, and lamination makes it worse. Sea containers swing widely in temperature and humidity, so ship in moisture-barrier bags with desiccant and avoid stacking tags directly against garments, which leaves pressure marks and oil stains.

A final misconception: thicker is not automatically more premium. Apparent thickness can be created with mounting and embossing, but weight and cost rise with it; conversely, 300 gsm art paper with matte lamination and embossing often feels more premium than 400 gsm with no finish at all. Think about where the customer meets the tag — fitting room, courier parcel or gift box — then choose the caliper.

14. Size, Shape and Stringing: Setting the Spec

There is no single standard tag size, but 40×80 mm is the buyer default: enough room for brand name, barcode, size and price, and compatible with most tagging guns. Childrenswear and accessories often use 30×60 mm, while outerwear and gift lines go to 45×100 mm or 50×90 mm to carry brand story and multilingual copy. Rounded corners (R3–R5) are the safest shape; bell, shield or leaf shapes need a custom die and raise both MOQ and cost.

  • Cotton string: natural look, can be recycled with paper, best for eco and organic lines.
  • Jute string: rugged and stiff, suited to denim, workwear and outdoor; it does fuzz over time.
  • Nylon thread and plastic barbs: lowest cost and fastest to apply, ideal for high-volume e-commerce — but check that plastic parts meet the client's packaging-reduction policy.
  • Safety pins: common on sample garments and photo samples, not recommended for production — they snag fabric and create return risk.

Plan barcodes and 2D codes together. A GS1 linear barcode needs quiet zones on both sides and a minimum module size; a tag that is too small will not scan. A 2D code (GS1 Digital Link) can carry batch, serial and a marketing landing page in one symbol, which suits brands that need traceability and traffic. Leave 3 mm bleed and 3 mm safety margin in the artwork, otherwise die-cut drift clips the copy.

Hole position and diameter also affect cost and handling: standard holes are 3 mm or 5 mm, usually set 5–8 mm from the edge. Too close to the edge and the tag tears; too far in and the hole clips the copy. If you string with a tagging gun, hole size and fastener must match, or application speed drops sharply.

One more overlooked detail: what goes on each side. Keep the logo and key visual on the front; put barcode, composition, origin, care instructions and a service QR code on the back. When the back carries a lot, keep type no smaller than 6 pt — otherwise neither store staff nor shoppers can read it, which adds after-sales questions.

A final practical note on comparing quotes: factories quote differently — some include punching, stringing, folding and individual bagging, others quote bare tags only. Agree on which operations are included before comparing, or the numbers will look far apart when the real gap is scope, not paper cost.

💡 Tip: If you are weighing several papers, ask your supplier for a 'material swatch set' combining different stocks and finishes — it makes in-house comparison much easier.
