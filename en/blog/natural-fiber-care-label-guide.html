Care Labels for Wool, Silk, Cashmere and Linen

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 19, 2026

Care labels for natural fibres are the category most often damaged by a generic template. The same line — machine wash 30 °C gentle, do not bleach, low iron — is correct on a cotton tee and ruinous on a wool sweater, sending a four-figure garment straight into shrinkage and felting. This guide sets the care floor for wool, silk, cashmere and linen, shows how ISO 3758 symbols land on the label, covers blend tolerances and measurable acceptance, and closes with label material and type height, because symbols nobody can read are symbols that do not exist.

1. The Care Floor for Four Fibres

Fibre Wash limit Dry-clean code Main risk
Wool, merino 30 °C wool cycle or hand wash P (perchloroethylene) Felting, pilling, shoulder distortion
Silk, mulberry Hand wash 30 °C, do not wring P or F (petroleum solvent) Water marks, loss of lustre, seam slippage
Cashmere Hand wash 30 °C, dry flat in shade P Fibre migration, stretching, pilling
Linen and linen blends 40 °C normal, or 30 °C if not pre-shrunk F, or washable depending on finish First-wash shrinkage 3–5%, white lines on creases

The line most often broken in that table is wool. Factories write 30 °C gentle because that is the cotton answer, but wool needs low mechanical action more than low temperature: plenty of water, a slow spin, and no rubbing against other garments. Write it as 30 °C wool cycle, spin at 400 rpm or less, dry flat, and returns drop noticeably. If the mill cannot guarantee machine washing, say hand wash — do not trade a claim for a feature.

2. Putting ISO 3758 Symbols on the Label

ISO 3758 is a five-symbol system built around the washtub: washing, bleaching, drying, ironing and professional dry cleaning, always in that order, with the last symbol carrying the strictest limit. China's GB/T 8685 and Japan's JIS L 0001 are broadly compatible, but Japan replaced its four-symbol care labelling with the five-symbol system, so the old pictograms can no longer be printed — shipping to Japan with the old set is one of the most common reasons a care label comes back.

  • Washing: the number in the tub is the maximum temperature (30, 40, 60), one bar underneath means a mild cycle, two bars a very mild cycle, and a hand means hand wash only.
  • Bleaching: an empty triangle allows any bleach, two diagonal lines mean oxygen bleach only, a cross means no bleaching. Wool and silk are always crossed.
  • Drying: a circle inside a square means tumble dry, with dots for heat (one low, two medium); a horizontal line inside a square means line dry, two lines mean dry flat. For cashmere the correct pictogram is dry flat.
  • Ironing: one dot means up to 110 °C, two dots 150 °C, three dots 200 °C. Wool and silk take one dot with a pressing cloth, or steam only.
  • Professional dry cleaning: P in a circle is perchloroethylene, F is petroleum solvent, A is any solvent; a bar underneath means mild treatment. This is the symbol most likely to contradict the wash instruction, so confirm what the fabric was actually tested against.
Do not invent your own symbol order. Suppliers often move the dry-clean symbol to a final line with a box around it for emphasis, and the whole set then reads as dry-clean only. Symbols have no emphasis; order is the grammar.

3. Blends and Fibre Content: The 5% Rule

Most markets allow ±3 percentage points on declared fibre content, but there is an exemption that gets missed: a fibre below 5% may be declared as other fibres without naming it, with a stricter rule for wool and silk blends. The labelling duty itself comes from law — Reglamento (UE) 1007/2011 governs textile names and composition in the EU, Spanish consumer rules require the information in Spanish, and French law, via the Code de la consommation, requires French.

  • List fibres from highest to lowest content using standard names — never invented wording like wool-feel or silk-touch, which reads as misleading.
  • The 5% rule is stricter for wool: below 5%, calling it wool requires evidence that it genuinely affects the product's character, otherwise it cannot be a selling point.
  • A trim fibre such as 2% elastane may fall under 5%, but if it changes how the garment is washed it should still be declared — otherwise the customer washes it wrongly and liability returns to the brand.

4. Dimensional Stability: After Three Washes

A shrinkage promise on the care label should map to a real test, not a supplier's verbal assurance. The standard route for domestic washing is ISO 6330, or AATCC 135 in the US, with fixed measuring points — chest width, body length, sleeve length — re-measured after three cycles. Knitted wool and cashmere usually carry ±3% on length; linen takes ±5% on the first wash. Beyond that, either print a first-wash shrinkage note or change the process with pre-shrinking or resin finishing.

5. Label Material and Legibility: Right Symbols, Readable Print

  • Material: for wool and cashmere use a soft satin or nylon taffeta care label with heat-cut edges so the border does not scratch. On dark garments, white label stock with black print reads better than a matched dark label.
  • Size: keep symbol height at 5 mm minimum and warning text at 2 mm or more. Japanese care and composition marking has to be legible, and in practice 1.5 mm is the floor for character height.
  • Placement: centre back neck, 1–2 cm below the seam, is the convention; long coats can move it to the side seam. If a garment carries several labels, the care symbols appear once only, so they cannot contradict each other.
  • Durability: symbols must still read after three washes. Heat-transfer labels lose print on high-friction wool; prefer woven or printed satin, and wash three sample garments before shipping.

One last practical note: care advice for natural fibres changes with finishing. The same wool can take completely different symbols depending on whether it was pre-shrunk, and when a dyehouse or finisher changes, old labels must be re-confirmed. Recording the fabric test report number in the trim file is worth more than adding another disclaimer line to the label.
