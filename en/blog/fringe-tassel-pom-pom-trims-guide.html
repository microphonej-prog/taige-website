Fringe, Tassel and Pom-Pom Trims: Structure, Attachment and Wash Care

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 15, 2026

Fringe, tassel and pom-pom trims finish the hems of kidswear, the ends of scarves, hat crowns and denim cuffs, and they are the hardest line on a trim purchase order to sign off. They are bought by the metre, judged by hand feel, and all too often come out of the first wash as a matted tangle or a row of loose threads. This guide works through structure, fibre, attachment, wash durability and child safety, so these trims stay attached, stay separate and stay inside the limits.

1. Structure comes first: cut fringe, loop fringe, tassels and pom-poms

The same 5 cm wide fringe tape can differ by forty percent in cost depending on whether it is cut or looped. Cut fringe is woven first, then the weft is slit and the edge sealed with a hot knife or adhesive: soft, good drape, and a small amount of short fibre sheds per metre. Loop fringe keeps the yarn loops uncut: it sheds less, wears longer and costs less, but it reads flatter. A tassel is a bound head plus a skirt; machine-tied heads are perfectly even, hand-tied heads give the staggered lengths buyers actually pay for. A pom-pom is yarn wound on a core and then trimmed round, and the tension of that core decides whether it stays spherical after washing.

Type Width or size Typical fibre How it fails
Cut fringe 3–15 cm wide Polyester, viscose Tangling and shedding after wash
Loop fringe 2–10 cm wide Polyester, cotton Flat look, edge curling
Tassel 4–12 cm skirt Polyester, cotton, metallic Head unravelling, skirt knotting
Pom-pom 2–8 cm diameter Acrylic, wool blend Deforming, core loss after wash

2. Fibre choice: polyester, viscose, cotton and metallics

Polyester is the default: wash-fast, good colour fastness, low unit price, and a 10 cm wide tape at roughly 90 g/m is the easiest spec to repeat. Viscose drapes best but has low wet strength and shrinks 3–5% in length after washing, so anything washed as a whole garment, such as a child's scarf, should be pre-shrunk at 40 °C first. Cotton sells on natural feel and skin comfort and suits baby and organic claims, but cotton pom-poms pick up lint, most visibly in dark shades. Metallics belong to holiday ranges only: they lose lustre with abrasion and cannot be ironed hot, because one press of the soleplate transfers the shine onto whatever is underneath.

3. Attachment: into the seam, topstitched, heat sealed or hardware

Whether a trim stays on is eighty percent attachment and twenty percent material. Four methods cover almost everything, and picking the wrong one means paying for repairs:

  • Into the seam: the trim is sandwiched between two panels and sewn in. Strongest option, right for hems and scarf ends; leave at least 1 cm of seam allowance or the wash will pull it out.
  • Topstitched: pressed onto the face of the fabric. Clean look, shortest labour, but the load sits on the needle holes and fine knits can split along the stitch line.
  • Heat sealed: fixed with hot-melt tape. Fastest on synthetics and metallics, zero needle holes, but the wash ceiling is usually 60 °C and bond strength drops noticeably after three low-temperature washes.
  • Hardware: eyelets, rivets or pins through the trim, common on leather and heavy denim. Nickel release and pull strength must both be tested, and the hole in the trim needs reinforcement.

4. Wash durability and labelling: the law covers fibre and animal origin, not hand feel

Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011 governs fibre composition, and decorative trims largely sit inside its exemption list, but the moment marketing copy says 100% cotton tassels or wool pom-poms, that number has to be true. What actually holds shipments is a different clause: products containing non-textile parts of animal origin must state contains non-textile parts of animal origin, which covers rabbit-fur pom-poms, feather fringes and leather tassels. France's Code de la consommation requires the mandatory information in French, with fibre names not left in English, and Spain's Real Decreto 928/1987 requires composition in Spanish together with the garment size, for instance talla 38.

Under Japan's Household Goods Quality Labelling Act, fibre composition and care instructions must be shown, and in practice a trim sold as part of a garment is labelled with it; Korea's Electrical Appliances and Consumer Products Safety Control Act requires composition, care and country of origin, with Korean sizing such as 55 or 66 on the garment. Both markets check whether the label and the goods agree, not whether the hand feel is nice.

5. Kidswear and costume: small parts and flammability

Pom-poms get caught as small parts: an 8 cm pom-pom is harmless on an adult, and the same part on the hood of a three-year-old's jacket is a recall risk. The EU judges with the EN 71-1 small parts cylinder and the US with 16 CFR 1501 under CPSIA; both hinge on whether the part fits entirely inside the cylinder, with a pull of roughly 70 N applied first. Textile flammability is a separate gate: 16 CFR 1610 sorts apparel fabrics into three classes, and raised-surface, lofty trims tend to land in the middle class, so an actual burn test is needed to classify them. Costume pieces that fall into children's sleepwear territory are judged under the stricter 1615 and 1616.

Check Basis Practical limit
Small parts, under 36 months EN 71-1, 16 CFR 1501 No full entry into the cylinder after 70 N
Flammability, apparel 16 CFR 1610, EN 1103 Raised trims need their own burn class, not the base fabric's
Nickel release, metal parts REACH Annex XVII, entry 27 Migration limit for parts in prolonged skin contact
Appearance after wash In-house acceptance Five washes at 40 °C with no matting and under 5% fibre loss

6. Ordering and inspection: buy by the metre, check by the piece

Trim ships in 25-yard or 50 m rolls, but you should convert from cut-piece consumption plus loss, not from garment count. The working rule is to order 1.08 times the per-garment consumption to cover cutting loss and recuts: a 10 cm cut fringe on a kidswear hem runs about 45 cm per garment, so three thousand pieces needs roughly 1,458 m. What causes disputes at inspection is rarely length; it is pile-length variation between batches and colour deviation. Two batches of the same colour code sewn onto one neckline will show, so large orders should be drawn from one production run with the dye lot printed on every roll.

  • Put pile length tolerance in the spec: 8 cm nominal means 7.5–8.5 cm accepted, anything wider needs sorting.
  • Hold colour deviation to grade 4 or better on the grey scale; darks and metallics are hardest, so keep 10% spares from the same batch.
  • Pack flat or on a hanger, never vacuum compressed: squashed pom-poms do not fully recover and lose one grade of loft.

7. The three mistakes that repeat

First, treating trim as unimportant decoration: only the pre-production sample is judged on appearance, nothing is compared after washing, and the bulk order comes out of the dryer with pom-poms turned into felted strips. Second, ignoring how the trim dictates the garment's care label: a trim that will not take a 40 °C machine wash forces the whole style to hand wash, which rewrites the care label and the product's positioning. Third, substituting the base fabric's flammability and shrinkage data for the trim's, when the two almost never transfer. Write these three into the spec before the order is placed and inspection reduces to reading numbers.
