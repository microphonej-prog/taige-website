Trim MOQ Negotiation: Tiered Prices, Stock Yarn and Combined Runs

By TAIGE Packaging &nbsp;·&nbsp; Updated November 9, 2026

Every trim order starts with a number that is not a price: the minimum order quantity. Buyers treat MOQ as a wall and suppliers often quote it as one. In practice it is a cost recovery figure made of setup time, the smallest material purchase the mill will accept, and the shortest run the machine can produce without losing money. That is why it moves when the structure of the order changes, and why it does not move when you simply repeat the question.

This guide covers four things: where the number comes from, the levers that genuinely lower it, what to prepare before the conversation, and what a shorter run really costs you. All of it applies the same way to woven labels, printed care labels, hang tags and poly bags, because the cost structure behind them is identical.

1. Where the MOQ number actually comes from

A woven label MOQ is rarely set by the loom alone. It is four costs divided by the unit price: the jacquard or plate setup, the minimum purchase of a special yarn or dyed substrate, the waste at the head and tail of each roll, and the machine slot reserved for your run. Hang tags follow the same logic with printing plates, die-cut tooling and paper minimums per grade.

  • Setup: jacquard file, plate, screen or die. Paid once per design, not per piece.
  • Material minimum: a yarn dyed to a Pantone reference, or paper in an unusual g/m² grade, carries a mill minimum below which nobody will run.
  • Waste: the first metres of a roll carry tension and shade variation, so a 500-piece run wastes proportionally far more than a 5,000-piece run.
  • Machine slot: small runs are scheduled between larger jobs, which is exactly where the extra two weeks of lead time comes from.

2. Four levers that genuinely lower MOQ

These are the levers that work in practice, roughly in the order a buyer should use them. None is a discount trick: each one changes what the factory has to buy, or what it has to schedule.

  • Tiered price bands. Ask for three quantity bands, for example 3,000 / 5,000 / 10,000 pieces, priced separately. The bands reveal the real cost curve and let you choose the point where price and consumption meet.
  • Stock yarn or stock substrate. A shade already in stock — black, white, ecru, standard red — cuts the per-colour minimum sharply. Special dye lots are what push MOQ up, so ask for the stock swatch range before designing a new shade.
  • Combined runs. Several colours of one design, or several styles sharing one label blank, printed on one sheet or woven on one warp. The machine cares about total quantity, not quantity per SKU — this is usually the biggest single reduction available.
  • Split delivery against one order. One purchase order, quantity called off in two or three shipments. The factory buys material once and you carry less stock, which matters most when a season is still being confirmed.

Note the unit that matters here: material is bought by the metre or by g/m², so a 400-piece style with a small label at 30 mm width consumes around 12 metres of tape. Converting pieces into metres before the conversation is what turns a vague request into a quote.

3. What to bring to the conversation

A supplier can only lower the MOQ when the request is concrete. Bring a spec sheet with material, size in mm, finish and colour reference; the consumption per garment, so both sides convert pieces into metres; a delivery calendar that matches your production slots; and the destination markets. That last point is not paperwork: labelling obligations decide which label versions have to exist. A garment sold in the EU needs a responsible person under GPSR (EU) 2023/988 and fibre composition under (EU) No 1007/2011, and the German Pflegeetikett must carry the Faserzusammensetzung under the Textilkennzeichnungsgesetz. Ask for all destination labels in one run; that is often where the quantity for a stock yarn comes from.

  • Annual forecast beats first-order quantity. A supplier who sees 30,000 pieces a year will hold material for a 3,000-piece start.
  • Name the acceptable substitutes: a stock yarn shade, a slightly heavier paper, a standard fold. Substitution is the cheapest MOQ reduction on the table.
  • State the destination label set up front. EU plus Japan plus Brazil on one style means three label versions, and printing them in one run is what makes a smaller quantity viable.

4. What a lower MOQ actually costs

Every reduction has a price, and it is better to cost it than to discover it at the invoice. Unit price rises between the smallest and the middle band, usually by 10 to 35 per cent, and the jump is steepest on special colours. Colour continuity suffers when a small special dye lot runs out and the next batch is dyed separately. Lead time grows because a short run waits for a machine slot. And stock risk moves to you: leftover labels from a discontinued style are waste, not inventory.

Approach Effect on unit price What to watch
Price tier bands Lower at the top band Do not order into a band you cannot consume
Stock yarn or substrate Five to fifteen per cent lower Shade limited to the stock palette
Combined runs Ten to twenty per cent lower Every SKU ships on the same date
Split delivery, one order Same unit price Storage and handling sit on your side

5. A short negotiation script that works

Ask in this order: what is the MOQ for this specification, what drives it, which substitutions would change it, and what quantity reaches the next price band. A structural question gets a structural answer, while asking whether 500 pieces is possible gets a no. Then put the answer in the quotation, including the quantity tolerance — plus or minus five per cent is normal on woven labels and print — so the difference is settled before the order instead of after the invoice.

Rule of thumb: negotiate the structure, not the number. A 3,000-piece order that reuses stock yarn and combines three colours usually costs less per piece than a 1,000-piece order with a special dye lot.
