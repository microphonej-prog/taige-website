Scrapping Garment Trims: Destruction Methods, Certificates and Evidence

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 18, 2026

A run of care labels with the wrong size code was sold to a scrap dealer as waste paper. Two months later the same labels appeared on a stall in another wholesale market; the brand traced them to the printer and claimed not for the labels but for channel damage and counterfeit enforcement costs. Scrapping trims looks like the smallest job in the warehouse until it goes wrong.

This guide covers five things: what must be destroyed, how to choose between four destruction methods, the eight fields a destruction certificate needs, how to build the evidence chain, and how to do the maths between destroying at destination and shipping back.

1. What Must Be Destroyed and What Can Be Recycled Normally

There is one criterion: does the item carry identifiable brand information, and could it be used as genuine once it leaves the building. Hang tags, woven labels, care labels, stickers and bags carrying a logo, style number, security code or barcode should by principle be destroyed on rejection; generic material with no brand information can go to normal recycling.

  • Destroy: misprinted hang tags, out-of-season tagged stock, labels on cancelled orders, serialised security tags, samples the customer has asked in writing to destroy
  • Recycle: blank bags, unprinted paper trim and roll ends, unbranded webbing offcuts, cartons and pallets
  • Confirm first: rejected customer-supplied material. Disposal rights sit with the customer, so acting alone is overstepping — get written instruction first

2. Four Destruction Methods and What They Suit

MethodSuitsKey point
Cross-cut shreddingHang tags, stickers, paper bags, cardAim for fragments under 10 mm and mix with other waste so the original cannot be reassembled
Guillotine cuttingWoven labels, care labels, webbing, brand tabsCut through the brand information itself — trimming the edges achieves nothing; cheapest method and good for volume
Incineration with energy recoveryLaminated paper, foil-stamped stock, mixed material, adhesive wasteUse a licensed operator and obtain the disposal certificate plus weight record — a plain receipt is not enough
RepulpingUncoated paper tags, bags and cardsThe only route that recovers material, but confirm the print cannot be recovered after pulping

The hardest cases are laminated and foil-stamped tags: film and foil rule out repulping, incineration is restricted locally, so most end up shredded and sent as general industrial waste. That belongs in the design stage — if the brand requires destruction, an all-paper uncoated construction is far easier to handle than a laminated one.

3. The Eight Fields a Destruction Certificate Needs

A certificate that survives brand review usually carries: item name and specification; quantity in pieces, rolls and kilos; batch number; reason for destruction; method and equipment; date and location; signatures and stamps of operator and witness; before and after photographs with weighbridge ticket numbers; and the destination of the residue. That is nine, and the last one is the one most often left out.

The two gaps cluster around quantity and destination: cartons written without piece counts, or destruction stated with no word on where the shreds went. Brand compliance reads the document to confirm material did not re-enter the market, so if the residue destination is unclear the certificate is worth nothing in review.

4. The Evidence Chain: Weight, Video and a Third-Party Witness

Three levels, chosen to the customer requirement. Self-destruction: done in-house with a certificate and photographs, fine for routine misprints and offcuts. Supervised destruction: customer, representative or third-party inspector present, filmed throughout, with four continuous shots — sealed, opened, fed into the machine, output. Third-party destruction: a licensed operator performs it and issues a disposal certificate with weighbridge ticket, for security tags, high-value brands and legally sensitive lots.

A practical filming trick: open on today's newspaper or the phone clock, then the seal number, then the machine infeed. Three continuous unedited shots beat a page of prose — in a dispute the other side is not reading your wording, they are checking whether this box of material is provably that box.

5. Export Orders: Destroy at Destination or Ship Back

Shipping back means two ocean legs, import clearance and duty — freight often exceeds the goods value. Destroying at destination raises three questions: is the local operator licensed, will the brand accept an overseas certificate, and how is the order written off afterwards. A common compromise destroys the paper items locally (tags, bags) and consolidates branded woven and care labels for return, moving to a full-container return only at volume.

Whichever route, agree the write-off documents with the customer in advance: some want the certificate plus weighbridge tickets, others also the disposal operator's business licence and permits. Asking for those after the destruction is done usually produces nothing.

6. The Three Things Most Often Missed

  • Cylinders, film, dies and plates: destroying product but not tooling leaves the ability to reproduce it in the building. Inventory and destroy them when a contract ends or a style is discontinued
  • Whether the shreds reassemble: strip-cut tags can still be pieced back into a logo, which is why brands ask for cross-cut or a second mixing step before disposal
  • Mixed into ordinary waste paper: selling branded tags to a scrap paper dealer is the most common and most serious leak, and far harder to trace than material carried out by staff
💡 Tip: tie destruction records to the batch number and keep the ledger at least two years. When a brand re-audits, being able to match batch, quantity, date and who witnessed it beats any well-worded certificate.
