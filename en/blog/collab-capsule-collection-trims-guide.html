Collaboration Capsule Trims: Dual-Brand Hang Tags, Licensing and Limited Marks

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 27, 2026

A collaboration looks like two logos printed side by side. On the trims side, three things get harder at once: who authorises what, whose information goes first, and when it has to arrive. When a clothing brand builds a capsule with an IP, an artist or another designer, trims are the only physical carrier of both brands' assets — and the part most often squeezed on lead time. Six questions follow: how to lay out two logos, how many hang tags, which authorisation documents, what goes on the care label and origin line, where limited-edition and anti-counterfeit marks belong, and how to schedule trims on a short cycle.

Trims are cheap, information-dense and impossible to rework. A hang tag may be a few tenths of a percent of garment cost, yet it sets the buyer's first judgement of the capsule; and once a woven label plate, a hang tag die or a spot colour ink is made, a change means starting over. Collaboration budgets go mostly into marketing, trims are a sliver — and that sliver is what holds up a launch.

1. Three Variables a Collaboration Adds

A regular trims brief answers three questions: does it look right, is it compliant, is it cheap. A collaboration adds three more. Authorisation scope — which materials may carry the partner's logo, to what extent, and whether it can appear on packaging and advertising. Hierarchy — who is the lead brand, how the rail and the product page present it, who handles returns. Timing — a collaboration hangs on one drop or one date, and missing the window means no second chance. If these three are not fixed before sampling, every later change is a new plate.

2. Laying Out Two Logos: Rules Before Design

Two-logo layout is not an aesthetic question, it is an executability question. The same lockup has to survive a hang tag, a woven label, a poly bag, a paper bag and a product page — five carriers at very different sizes — and eyeballed spacing will drift. The right move is to write four rules into the trims manual: hierarchy, spacing, minimum size, prohibitions. Once written, five carriers can be produced by different people without drifting.

RuleHow to write itIf you skip it
HierarchyLead brand above or first, partner below or joined by a multiplication signBuyers cannot tell who made it, and returns ownership becomes a dispute
Spacing basisGive a multiple of logo height, for example 0.5 timesOn small carriers the two logos crowd, on large ones they drift apart
Minimum sizeState a millimetre floor; below it, drop to one logo or a text creditSmall type on a woven label turns to mush and thin strokes vanish
ProhibitionsNo stretching, no recolouring, no outline or shadow, no cropping into the clear spaceBrand review rejects the lot, and you rework both plate and material

3. How Many Hang Tags: Three Options

There are three ways to handle hang tags in a collaboration; none is universally better — it depends on price point, channel and what the partner demands. Remember that every extra tag multiplies assembly time, stringing, mismatch risk and stock SKUs: on twenty thousand units, one more tag costs more in labour than in paper.

  • One tag, two logos: cheapest and simplest to assemble, right for fast fashion, online styles and frequent repeats. Both logos must settle the hierarchy on one panel, so design room is tightest
  • Two tags: each brand gets a full panel, good when both brands carry similar weight. The cost is more tags, more stringing labour and more mismatch risk, and both tags must hang from the same point in the same direction
  • Dedicated collaboration tag: a fresh design for this drop, the fullest canvas and the most collectible, right for limited runs and high price points. The plate cost is one-off, but on low quantities it spreads badly per unit
  • Hybrid: keep the standard hang tag and add a partner card or a belly band. No new main plate, yet the partner gets a dedicated surface — the usual compromise on short-cycle projects

4. Authorisation: Three Documents Before Sampling

The most expensive mistake in a collaboration is not a wrong colour, it is cutting the plate before the licence is in place. Three items must arrive before sampling: a trademark licence (materials covered, territories, term, and whether packaging and advertising are included); the original vector file from the partner (AI or EPS with spot colour references, never a screenshot or PNG from their website); and the clauses of their brand guidelines that touch trims (minimum size, clear space, exclusion zones). The term must cover production, selling and end-of-season clearance — a licence shorter than the selling window turns into dead stock.

One practical rule: put 'no licence, no plate' into your internal process. However hard sales pushes, get the licence and the vector file before cutting. Woven label plates, hang tag dies and spot colour inks are sunk costs, and one rework often costs more than the margin on the whole trims order.

5. Care Label and Origin: Legal Information First

The care label carries legal information, not marketing. Brand ownership, manufacturer or importer details, fibre content, origin and care symbols follow the destination's rules; the partner normally appears as licensor or co-brand, never as manufacturer. This is often written wrong — putting the partner in the manufacturer slot asks their legal team to vouch for your quality, and it will come back rejected. Put the limited-edition number on the back of the hang tag or on a separate numbered label rather than inside the care label: that label is already dense, and one more line costs readability and wash durability.

6. Three-Week Production: What Compresses

A collaboration timeline is often half a normal one. Three things can compress: reuse plates and dies (re-colour and re-word last season's tooling instead of cutting new, saving five to seven days); share materials (hang tag board, cords and bag specs from existing stock, avoiding a purchasing gap); ship in stages (tags and woven labels first, bags after — do not wait for a complete set). Two things cannot be compressed: spot colour matching and brand review. A spot colour that misses means redoing the lot, and a review chain that was never opened blocks you at the last gate.

  • Three things to do early: pull last season's equivalent trims as a reference; give the factory one table with both brands' spot colour references; approve hang tags and woven labels in the same sample round, not two
Collaboration trims add three acceptance checks to the usual ones: verify each logo against the licence, match spot colours to the brand's swatch, and spot-check assembly direction and tag count. The first two decide whether you can sell; the third decides the unboxing. Put all three on a signed sheet — whoever signs owns it.
