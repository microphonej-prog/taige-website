Labels and Packaging for Medical and Protective Garments: CE, EN 13795 and Sterilisation

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 31, 2026

Labels on medical and protective garments are held to a stricter standard than ordinary apparel. They do more than state fibre content and size: they carry regulatory identity, performance class and traceability. On an isolation gown, surgical gown or chemical-protective jacket, one wrong class letter or a missing EU authorised representative address can be enough to fail a customs check or a hospital procurement audit.

Order volumes in this category are often small, a few thousand pieces per run, yet the documentation burden is far heavier than for ordinary apparel. Labels, packaging bags and instructions have to be designed together on one logic; buying any one of them separately leaves a gap. The six sections below cover regulatory status, mandatory information, sterilisation compatibility, durability verification, performance claims and instruction language.

1. Establish the regulatory status first: apparel, PPE or medical device

The same white nonwoven gown can fall under three entirely different frameworks, with very different labelling duties. The test is not the product name but the manufacturer's stated intended use: who it is sold to, where it is used, and what protection is claimed.

  • Ordinary apparel: used only for hygiene or dust cover in non-protective settings, with no protection claims. Labels follow ordinary textile rules: fibre content, origin, care symbols, importer details.
  • Personal protective equipment (PPE): claims protection against liquids, particles or chemicals. In the EU it falls under (EU) 2016/425 and needs the CE mark, an EU type-examination certificate (Module B) and the notified body number.
  • Medical device: claims use in a clinical environment, in surgery or for infection isolation. In the EU it falls under MDR (EU) 2017/745 and needs CE, a risk class (sterile class I, Is, IIa and so on), UDI and an EU authorised representative.

2. The five groups of information the label must carry

Once the regulatory status is settled, label content has a basis. The five groups below are what auditors check item by item; any line that does not match the certificate invites a corrective action.

Item Requirement Frequent error
Manufacturer identity Name plus a reachable physical address; in the EU also the importer or authorised representative
CE mark and body number Applied according to risk class, with the four-digit notified body number next to CE
Model and performance class Word-for-word identical to the certificate annex, e.g. Type 5/6 or an EN 13795 level
Batch and dates Sterile items need a batch number, sterilisation date and expiry date
Conditions of use Single use or reusable, maximum wash cycles, contraindications

3. Sterilisation compatibility: labels and packaging must be validated together

The sterilisation method physically changes labels and packaging: ethylene oxide penetrates and leaves residue, irradiation makes some materials brittle and yellow, steam at 134°C makes pressure-sensitive adhesive bleed and softens ink. Material, ink, adhesive and pouch therefore have to be validated as one set for a given method, not chosen separately.

  • Ethylene oxide (EO): watch adsorption and aeration time on porous materials; a common combination is a breathable film with a paper-and-film pouch, and labels should avoid large areas of absorbent paper stock.
  • Irradiation (25 to 50 kGy): inks and adhesive layers must resist yellowing; avoid PVC, which releases hydrogen chloride and discolours.
  • Steam sterilisation: must withstand 134°C and above; some synthetic papers and BOPP shrink or distort, so polyester or a dedicated heat-resistant stock is safer.
  • Validation: include a chemical indicator with each sterilisation batch and leave a designated field on the label for the indicator and record, rather than making the hospital find space.

4. Durability: wash cycles are a product claim and need trim data behind them

For reusable protective garments and surgical gowns, EN 13795 requires performance to hold after a defined number of industrial washes, and the label itself must stay legible over the same count. Seventy-five industrial washes is the common working figure; the exact temperature and programme depend on the standard version and the customer.

  • Visual check after washing: text, symbols and batch number intact, the smallest type still legible, edges not fraying.
  • Care symbols: follow ISO 3758, and make sure the symbols agree with the stated wash limit.
  • Seams and edges: cut, ultrasonically sealed or bound edges decide whether the label curls or delaminates after washing, so the choice belongs in sampling, not in mass production.
Frequent trap: the label claims 75 washes but a standard polyester woven label was used, and after twenty-something cycles the edges fray and the text drops off. The wash count is a product claim printed on the tag; it has to be backed by test data on the trim, not by copywriting.

5. Performance claims: state tested classes, avoid effect words

Protection class is where these products most often go wrong, because it is governed by product regulation and advertising law at once. One principle: every class printed on the label must map to a valid test report, and the model on the report must be the model on the label.

  • Name the level and the lab: EN 13795 has levels 1, 2 and 3; saying it complies with EN 13795 says nothing about barrier level, so state the level and the testing body.
  • Be careful with effect words: antibacterial and antiviral need a matching standard (ISO 18184, for instance) and registration support; on ordinary protective products such wording is high risk.
  • Do not overreach: Type 3, 4, 5 and 6 are whole-garment test classes; a fabric test result cannot be presented as a garment class.

6. Instructions, warnings and language: the most overlooked part

Instructions and warnings are more often overlooked than the label itself. EU rules require instructions to accompany the goods in an official language of the destination country; for single-use items, state why reuse is excluded and how to dispose of the product, rather than printing a prohibition symbol alone.

  • Language: prepare for the official languages of the member states; in practice a multilingual leaflet or a multilingual PDF in the carton, never English alone.
  • Placement: warnings must appear on the smallest sales unit; printing them on the outer carton only is as good as absent.
  • Layout headroom: multilingual copy needs room; French, German and Spanish typically run 1.2 to 1.4 times the English length, so reusing the English layout overflows.

7. Checklist before placing the order

  • Settle the regulatory status and claim scope first, then the label content; the reverse order means redoing it.
  • Obtain the certificate and test reports, then check class, model and validity against the label.
  • Fix the sterilisation method and sample label material together with the pouch, not the label alone.
  • Fix the maximum wash count and validate to that count; do not use lab data to support a higher claim.
  • List the destination countries and required languages, and finalise instructions and label copy together.
  • Confirm the barcode or UDI rule and print position, and leave a clear zone for variable data printing or inkjet.
