Print-on-Demand Apparel Labels: Care Claims for DTG and DTF

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 8, 2026

Print-on-demand and dropshipping make a collection launch very fast: upload the design, produce after the order, hold no stock. The print is the selling point, yet the care label is often where the complaint starts: artwork cracks, fades or stiffens after washing while the label promises machine wash and tumble dry. What a print survives decides what the care label may say; when label and decoration do not match, returns and bad reviews follow.

1. Identify the Decoration Method First

Method How it works and looks Wash limit
DTG direct to garment Water-based pigment ink sprayed onto cotton, with pre-treatment; fine on light garments, soft hand Moderate: 30 °C inside out, no bleach, low tumble
DTF transfer film Artwork is printed on PET film then heat-pressed; saturated colour, strong on dark and synthetic fabrics, slightly thicker hand Good: up to 40 °C inside out, never iron the printed face directly
Sublimation Dye enters the polyester fibre, barely changing the hand; colours look luminous Best: 40 °C machine wash is defensible, but only on light polyester and synthetics
Screen print and plastisol The classic bulk process: thick opaque ink, limited by colour count and minimum quantity Best: 40 °C inside out, though the ink film forms fine cracks as the fabric stretches
Embroidery Not a print but part of on-demand decoration; the most durable, though the back stitches affect next-to-skin comfort Best: lasts as long as the garment, though snagging must be managed

2. The Care Claim Must Follow the Decoration

The same T-shirt from the same brand should not carry the same care label if the decoration method differs. The four points below are where printed apparel most often fails and what to confirm before writing the label.

  • Heat is the first killer of any print: a hot tumble dryer, ironing the printed face or washing too hot cracks, dulls and stiffens the film. If the label allows tumble drying, it must also state the heat setting and whether the garment goes in inside out.
  • A dependable combination: gentle 30 °C or 40 °C machine wash, inside out; no bleach; low tumble or line dry; cool iron avoiding the print; no dry cleaning where the process does not allow it. This set stays safe for DTG, DTF and screen print alike.
  • Draw the symbols to ISO 3758 or ASTM D5489 in the fixed order: wash, bleach, tumble dry, iron, professional care. Do not invent an order or mix the two standards. A wrong symbol is more likely to be flagged by a marketplace or customs than clumsy wording.
  • Next-to-skin and childrenswear carry extra rules: cord lengths, detachable small parts and how labels are fixed fall under EN 14682, CPSIA and similar. If a printed garment gains a decorative button, cord or sequin, that part needs its own pull and small-parts assessment.
💡 Tip: the wash temperature on a care label is not a brand preference, it is the limit the decoration and fabric can survive. Choose the print method first, then the wash programme, and write the label last. Reverse that order and the label becomes a promise you cannot keep.

3. Setting the Wash Test: Cycles, Criteria and Verdict

Saying the print survives ten washes means nothing on its own: with a different temperature, detergent, mechanical action or drying method the same garment gives the opposite result. Spell out the test method and the pass criteria in the order so that both sides share one definition of failure.

  • Common international methods: AATCC 61 accelerated laundering (one 2A cycle approximates several home washes), AATCC 135 for dimensional change after home laundering, and ISO 6330 home wash programmes paired with ISO 105-C06 colour fastness to washing.
  • China and export practice: GB/T 3921 for colour fastness to soaping and GB/T 8629 for home laundering procedures. For domestic e-commerce or when working with Chinese mills, citing these aligns better than quoting AATCC.
  • Put the verdict in the order: after 5 and 10 washes on the agreed programme (20 or 40 cycles if the customer asks), the print shows no cracking or peeling, colour change and staining stay at grade 4 or better on the grey scale, with no obvious stiffening, blistering or lifted edges.
  • Agree shrinkage separately: cotton knit printed garments usually shrink by about 3% to 5%, which belongs in its own line with its own tolerance. It is a different failure from print durability and has a different cause.
💡 Tip: without a lab report, the cheapest safeguard is a same-batch wash trial — three pieces from the same fabric and the same print run, washed according to the habits of the target market, one kept as a control, all photographed and filed. It costs far less than arguing about who was right after delivery.

4. Making Labels and Hang Tags Work in an On-Demand Model

  • Heat transfer labels: fibre content, size and care information on a film label, applied inside the neck or at the side seam, is the most common answer for printed garments. It performs well on dark and stretch fabrics, but pressing temperature, dwell time and pressure must be controlled, and silicone-softened fabrics should be avoided or the edge lifts.
  • Self-adhesive care labels: peel and stick, ideal for small runs, trial orders and fast reorders, and handy for prototypes before the artwork is frozen. Distinguish permanent from removable adhesive and verify adhesion in the same wash test.
  • Blank labels printed in house: buying finished blanks and printing them gives an on-demand or one-off operation the most flexibility, but the ink must match the label coating or it simply washes away. Validate with a 20-piece run before scaling.
  • Hang tags and size labels: on-demand brands often start with 200–300 digitally printed tags before committing to tooling. Composition, origin and responsible-party details on the tag must match the care label, and the size label must agree with it too, or marketplace audits and customer complaints will find the gap.
  • Sewn or bonded: next-to-skin areas, high-wash-frequency positions and childrenswear should be sewn. Bonded labels suit only categories washed at low temperature. When both coexist in one brand, the care label must follow the most conservative of the two.

5. Marketplace and Regulatory Points

  • United States: garments must carry permanent care instructions under the FTC care labelling rule, 16 CFR 423, which also fixes the content and order. Fibre content and origin sit under the Textile Fiber Products Identification Act and the Wool Act. Printed goods must spell out the inside-out and low-temperature conditions; if a complaint lands, the duty remains with the brand.
  • European Union: fibre names and labelling follow the Textile Labelling Regulation (EU 1007/2011) and care symbols generally follow ISO 3758. In addition, the General Product Safety Regulation requires a responsible party with an address in the EU to be identifiable on the product; brands shipping direct from abroad should prepare for this early.
  • Marketplaces: Amazon requires FNSKU labels and poly bag warnings (vent holes or a suffocation warning), while TikTok Shop and similar platforms template their own fibre and care requirements. These are three separate sets of demands — the platform label, the care label and the hang tag — which may corroborate each other but never replace each other.
  • Consistency is what matters: composition, origin and care method must match across the hang tag, the care label, the marketplace listing and the customs paperwork. Four documents telling four different stories is the classic trigger for a customs inspection or a customer complaint.

6. Acceptance Criteria and Quotation Checklist

  • Must-check list: print appearance (cracking, blistering, lifted edges, colour change and staining grades), shrinkage after washing, label adhesion and position, completeness and accuracy of care symbols and wording, consistency between hang tag and care label, and pull strength for any small parts.
  • For a quote: fabric composition and weight, decoration method, artwork file and placement size, the care claim you want to make (tumble dry or not, iron or not), label type and attachment position, hang tag material and quantity, target market and platform, and whether samples or testing are needed.
  • Ordering as a set saves trouble: care label, size label, main label and hang tag together avoid three versions of the fibre content in one season, and adding the packaging bag (with its warning and vent holes) removes the work of comparing suppliers one by one later.
