Hang Tag Printing Defects: The Six You Will Actually See

By TAGE Packaging &nbsp;·&nbsp; Updated November 23, 2026

A hang tag is a small sheet printed at speed, and every defect it can carry ends up in a customer's hand. Most complaints are not about design. They are a hickey in the middle of the logo, ink transferred from one tag to the back of the next, a foil edge that looks bitten, or a fold that cracks through the coating. This guide groups defects by when they happen, gives cause and fix for the six you will actually meet, and closes with an inspection clause you can put in the order, because settling that before the container sails is cheaper than negotiating a discount after it lands.

1. Sort the Defects by When They Happen

The three groups have different owners, and mixing them only produces blame. Prepress: bleed under 3 mm, RGB artwork, a logo scaled up from a bitmap, hairlines below 0.25 pt, no trapping. On press: hickeys, scumming, misregistration, dot gain, ghosting, set-off. Post-press: die nicks, cracked scores, foil pinholes, lamination bubbles, scuffing in packing, edge paint wicking. Decide which group a defect belongs to and you know whether to call the designer, the printer, or the die shop and finisher.

2. The Six You Will Actually Meet

Defect Cause Fix Owner
Hickey (white spot, dirt) Paper dust or dried ink skin on the blanket or plate, far worse below 40% RH Wipe the blanket every 500 sheets, filter the ink, hold the room at 50 to 60% RH Printer
Set-off (ink on the reverse) Ink film too heavy, no spray powder, sheets stacked or laminated while still warm Cut the ink film, switch the spray powder on, cool the stack before it is boxed, and rest 12 hours before laminating Printer
Misregistration Paper movement, humidity swings, uneven plate tension; worse on light stock Condition the paper 24 hours, add 0.1 mm trapping, print register marks outside the trim Printer
Scumming (tinted background) Fountain solution too weak, ink too thin, wrong roller pressure, so the non-image area takes ink Check pH and conductivity, change the ink, reset the roller nip; do not just flood more water Printer
Die nick Chipped die edge, uneven counter, stack too high; most common at rounded corners and the hole Replace the die, re-make the counter, and pull 10 sheets every 500 to check outline and hole position against the light Die shop
Foil pinholes, ragged edge Foil too cold, pressure too low, dust on the plate, packing too soft Proof 20 sheets per run to set heat and pressure, wipe the plate with a lint-free cloth, and keep fine type off hairlines below 0.3 mm Foil department

Two of these are also compliance questions. Inks and coatings on a tag that hangs against a garment fall under the restrictions on certain substances in REACH (EC) 1907/2006, and buyers in Europe and North America increasingly ask the supplier for a declaration. A hickey is cosmetic; an ink film that never dries is a transfer risk onto the garment inside a humid container. Specify low-migration inks and an aqueous coating on the quotation, and the argument never starts.

3. Foil, Emboss and the Edge

Foil quality rests on three variables: heat, pressure and dwell. Let any one of them fall short and the foil bonds at sixty percent — bright to the eye, gone under a fingernail, and almost impossible to catch at inspection because it fails a week into wear. Embossing depends on the board: 300 g/m² takes a 0.15 mm blind relief with a clean edge, while lighter board needs a counter die or the reverse side shows the shape. Edge painting should wick 0.3 to 0.5 mm into the sheet; deeper than that and it eats the paper strength around the hole, so a heavier tag splits from the punch.

4. Consistency Across Runs: Colour and Legibility

A second run that does not match the first is the most common dispute and the hardest to win. Three numbers make it manageable: ΔE of 2 or less for spot colour and 3 or less for process colour, measured under D50 at a 2 degree observer and averaged over three points; the same paper lot; and the same ink batch, with a retained sample. Beyond that, keep a signed golden sample and measure production and reprints against it, because without one the discussion becomes a comparison of two monitors.

The other thing that gets ignored is legibility, which is a compliance requirement rather than a matter of taste. If a tag carries fibre composition, care symbols, country of origin or a retail price, those statements are governed in Europe by regulation 1007/2011, in Japan by the household goods quality labelling law and the premiums and representations law, and in Korea by the fair labelling and advertising act; type that is too small, too low in contrast or filled in by ink is a missing statement. Practical floor: 1.8 mm minimum character height for Chinese and Japanese, 1.5 mm for Latin, one weight heavier when reversed out of a dark background, and a check at 30 cm under 500 lux.

5. The Inspection Clause for Your Order

A clause that works has five sentences. Sampling per ISO 2859-1, general inspection level II, AQL 2.5 for major and 4.0 for minor defects. Defect classes: a wrong barcode, price or variant is critical; ΔE above 3, missing foil or a die cut off by more than 0.5 mm is major; a hickey under 1 mm outside the text is minor. Viewing under D50 at 500 lux from 30 to 40 cm. Five signed pieces retained per 10,000 and kept for twelve months. And reprints measured against the golden sample, with the printer carrying the freight on a remake. Five sentences in the purchase order end the argument early.

A digital proof cannot stand in for a press proof. Neither screen nor inkjet shows foil gloss, emboss depth, lamination bubbles or edge paint wicking. Only a press proof, or a signed first-off, can be approved, and it should be checked once in daylight and once in the light box.
