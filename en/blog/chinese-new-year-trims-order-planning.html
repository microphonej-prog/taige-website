Chinese New Year Trim Lead Times: How to Lock In Capacity Before the Shutdown

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 24, 2026

Trim lead times look short — 10 to 25 days of production — but the real variable is Chinese New Year. Factories fill their schedules two to four weeks before the holiday, need another two to three weeks after it to recover output, and sea and air freight prices climb while space runs out through mid-to-late December. Buyers usually discover the mismatch in December, when it is already too late to protect an on-shelf date. This guide sets out the ordering rhythm around the holiday, a backward-planned timeline, four risks with countermeasures, and a checklist you can copy straight into a quotation request.

The article runs in this order: why orders should be fixed in September to November, the three phases of the holiday shutdown, a backward-planned timeline, four risks and their countermeasures, which trims are worth pre-holiday stockpiling, and a pre-order checklist.

1. Why Chinese New Year Orders Should Be Fixed in September to November

Chinese New Year does not simply add a few days of waiting — the whole chain tightens at the same moment. Four things explain why the rhythm has to be set in autumn.

  • Schedules lock early: two to four weeks before the holiday, printing, weaving and bag-making lines all run at full tilt and take only orders with confirmed specifications and deposit received; late insertions rarely fit.
  • Freight and space tighten together: sea and air rates hit their annual high in mid-to-late December, space goes to whoever books first, and a backlog queues up for re-shipment after the holiday.
  • The approval cycle cannot be compressed: three to seven days for samples, plus your internal sign-off and foreign-language label proofing, usually eats more calendar time than production itself.
  • Restart is a ramp, not a switch: the first one to three weeks after the holiday go into returning staff and training new hires, so early output is often only fifty to sixty percent of normal.

2. The Three Phases of the Holiday Shutdown

Split the holiday into three phases and your actions become obvious: which orders must ship before it, and which can wait for the restart.

Phase Window What the supplier side actually looks like What it means for you
Pre-holiday rush 2 to 4 weeks before Schedules full, overtime everywhere, only confirmed orders and long-standing clients get in Orders placed now rarely make a pre-holiday shipment
Shutdown 3 to 10 days before, until 5 to 10 days after Workshops stop; most factories keep only duty staff, though some suppliers still answer email remotely Sampling, production and shipping all pause; only communication continues
Restart ramp-up 1 to 3 weeks after Staff return gradually, new hires are trained, machines are re-set Plan lead times at roughly 1.5 times normal

The date follows the lunar calendar; Chinese New Year 2027 falls in early February. The official holiday length and make-up working days are published by the State Council in November or December of the previous year, and normally total seven to eight days. Note that the real shutdown window is often longer than the official holiday — lines stopping early and restarting late are common — so plan against the factory calendar, not the statutory one.

3. Backward Planning: Start From the Arrival Date You Need

Do not count forward from the order date; count backward from the arrival date you need. The lead times below assume shipping from Dongguan to an overseas retail on-shelf date and can be adjusted to your transport mode:

Milestone Suggested lead time
Required arrival date (baseline) 0 — every other date is counted back from here
Shipment date 25 to 40 days for sea; book air 5 to 10 days ahead with 2 to 5 days in transit
Bulk completion date 10 to 25 days of production, depending on technique and quantity
Production start date For a post-holiday start, add another 2 to 3 weeks of ramp-up buffer
Sample approval 3 to 7 days for sampling plus 3 to 7 days for your approval
Order and deposit Execute as soon as the sample is approved, to lock the schedule

Example: for an on-shelf date of 1 March 2027, a sea shipment has to leave around 15 January, so bulk goods must be finished in late January — which means the order is placed in early December. Move the same order to post-holiday production and arrival typically slips to April. If the launch date is fixed, shipping before the holiday is the safe route.

4. Four Risks and How to Handle Them

  • Capacity squeeze: the two to four weeks before the holiday are the tightest window. Pay the deposit early to lock a slot, and flag the lines that must ship before the holiday so routine orders do not push them out.
  • Rate spikes and rolled cargo: rates climb through mid-to-late December and space goes first-come. Book early, and consolidate trim shipments with the garment cargo so you pay one customs entry and one freight bill.
  • Uncertain restart: return rates vary year to year and recovery is slower when hiring is tight. Ship launch-critical items before the holiday, treat post-holiday dates as contingency only, and keep a 1.5x buffer.
  • Weather and ports: winter gales and fog delay sailings and jam terminals. Keep five to seven days of slack in the shipping plan and use air freight to cover the critical milestones.

5. Which Trims Are Worth Stockpiling Before the Holiday

Two criteria decide it: how stable the version is, and whether you can carry the tied-up cash.

  • Good to stock three to six months ahead: care labels, neck labels, woven labels, standard poly bags, strings and tag fasteners. Versions rarely change and they store compactly, so producing early avoids holiday price rises and queues.
  • Poor candidates for big stock: hang tags carry prices, promotions and seasonal messages and change often. Lock the paper stock and finishing slot first, then ship in quarterly batches.
  • Ask for a confirmed annual quantity with staged delivery: fix the year's volume in one agreement, ship against your launch calendar, and you keep the bulk price without warehousing everything yourself.

6. Pre-Order Checklist

  • Required arrival date and latest shipment date, and who signs off on them
  • Whether the sample is approved and all label wording, including foreign languages, is final
  • Quantity and delivery split: one drop or several
  • Transport mode and rate expectation: sea, air or courier
  • Priority: which items must ship before the holiday and which can wait
  • Backup plan: agreed substitute materials and colours if a component runs short or a design changes
💡 Tip: the only certainty about Chinese New Year is that it comes every year, while the official holiday dates are published late. Treat pre-holiday shipment as the default and post-holiday dates as contingency — that order is far easier to defend. Our schedule is usually full a month before the holiday, so aim to confirm spring order specifications by mid-November.
