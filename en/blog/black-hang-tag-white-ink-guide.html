White Ink on Black Hang Tags: Opacity, Scuff and Edge Show-Through

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 20, 2026

A solid black hang tag with white type is one of the most requested premium treatments of the past two years: far more arresting on a rail than white card, and it photographs well. It is also the treatment that most often fails at mass production. The sample the brand approved was brilliant white; the bulk shipment is grey. The white letters scuff at the edges after a few passes against other tags inside the poly bag. The die-cut edge shows a ring of white core, so the tag reads as black paper pasted onto white board. None of these is a printer cutting corners. They are physical limits of white ink on a black substrate, and all three can be controlled in the specification.

1. The Most Expensive Mistake: One Hit of White

White ink is not a coat of white paint. It is titanium dioxide pigment suspended in a binder, and its hiding power is finite. Printed on white card the sheet is already white, so one hit is enough. Printed on black board the ink has to bury the black completely before it reads as white. A single hit usually lands in the grey zone: measured on a spectrophotometer under D50, lightness around L* 70–80. Two hits, with drying in between, reach L* 88–93, which is the white a brand recognises. A third hit is not better: the ink film gets thick enough to pile, to crack open along fold lines, and it slows the delivery date.

One line for the purchase order: white ink in two hits with intermediate drying, white areas at L* 90 or above measured on a spectrophotometer at D50 and 2 degrees. Without that number, make it whiter means something different to each side, and the incoming inspection becomes an argument.

2. Board Construction Decides the Edge: Three Kinds of Black

The same phrase, black card, can mean three different materials. The first is single-side coated black card: black surface, grey-white core, cheapest, but the die-cut edge always shows a pale ring and any scratch goes straight to white. The second is black-through board, dyed through the pulp so the edge is black as well; 15 to 25 percent dearer and the only construction that truly reads as premium. The third is grey-back coated board printed solid black plus white ink: lowest cost, grey edge, the standard for fast fashion and domestic retail. All three work in the usual 350–450 g/m² and 0.45–0.60 mm window; what separates them is the edge and what happens after a scuff.

Board Die-cut edge Fits
Single-side coated black card Pale core shows Mid-price domestic work where the edge is not inspected
Black-through board Black throughout Premium tags, gift sets, close-up merchandising
Grey-back board printed black Grey core Fast fashion, high volume, cost first

If the first construction is the only one in budget but the edge still has to look clean, there are two partial fixes. Edge painting runs ink around the cut side to match the face; it costs tens of renminbi per thousand and resists wear only moderately. The other is design: inset the die by 0.3 mm so the black extends past the fold line and the pale core hides inside the folded flap. The first needs to be specified up front; the second costs one die change and gets expensive the later it is raised.

3. Production Details: Line Width, Knockout and Barcodes

White ink is a thick film and its dot gain is far worse than process black, which fixes a set of minimum dimensions. The thinnest white rule is 0.25 mm at two hits; below that the ink spreads sideways and letters close up. White type should be 6 pt or larger and set in semibold or bold: a light face fills in at two hits. Keep at least 0.3 mm between two white elements, otherwise the black between them is eaten from both sides. Black type on white must be knocked out, meaning the white is left open and the black printed into it. Printing black directly over white ink gives a washed grey with fuzzy edges.

Barcodes are what black tags ruin most often. An inverted code with white bars on black will not scan on most POS equipment and GS1 does not accept it. The method is a white panel on the black, then a normal black-on-white symbol, with quiet zones of at least 10X on each side, where X is the module width: 0.33 mm for EAN-13 at nominal size, 0.264 mm at 80 percent magnification. Give the white panel 2 mm of margin so the scanner does not read the black edge as a bar.

4. Scuff Resistance: What Each Finish Really Costs

White ink without a protective coat wears badly: stacking at the printer, stringing, bagging and weeks of sea freight all leave grey marks along the letter edges. The three routes have different costs. A water-based matte varnish is the cheapest, keeps the matte feel, gives moderate rub resistance and does not interfere with paper recycling. UV varnish is hard and glossy but cracks along fold lines and shows fingerprints badly on black. BOPP or PET lamination at 15–18 µm protects best, yet it takes the sheet out of paper recycling almost entirely, which matters for EU shipments under the recyclability requirements of PPWR (EU) 2025/40, and it adds roughly 3 percent to the weight of every tag.

A practical rule for choosing: for domestic programs, courier delivery and price-sensitive tags, water-based matte varnish is enough. For premium tags that get handled repeatedly and sit on a rail for months, take 18 µm matte lamination and accept the recycling trade-off. For EU shipments that also carry environmental claims, use FSC board with water-based varnish and soy-based inks, because a laminated sheet is hard to defend in a packaging recyclability claim. Test scuff with a 500 g load over 100 cycles on a Sutherland-type rub tester; the pass line is no black showing through and no blurred letter edges.

5. Compliance: Ink, Mandatory Information and Legibility

White on black does not invalidate mandatory information, but it makes it harder to read. In the EU, fibre composition must be declared under Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011, and the General Product Safety Regulation (EU) 2023/988 requires a responsible economic operator in the Union plus traceability. The ink itself falls under the restricted substances in REACH (EC) No 1907/2006, which covers heavy metals and certain azo dyes; childrenswear trims also face the migration limits of EN 71-3. The practical rules: nothing mandatory below 1.5 mm cap height, roughly 6 pt bold; composition and origin never squeezed as reversed-out small type into a corner; and never placed on a fold line or a die-cut edge, where ink wears off first.

  • Japan: the Household Goods Quality Labelling Act requires composition and handling instructions in Japanese, with care symbols following JIS L 0001. Fields printed too small are effectively not labelled.
  • Korea: under the Electrical Appliances and Consumer Products Safety Control Act, the safety standard for household textiles puts composition, care instructions and country of origin on an attached label, not only on the hang tag.
  • Taiwan and mainland China: Taiwan's Commodity Labeling Act requires the product name and main composition in Chinese; GB 5296.4 sets explicit type height and durability requirements, so white on black must survive washing without losing letters.

6. Incoming Checks: Four Numbers on One Sheet

Check Method Pass limit
White lightness Spectrophotometer, D50, 2°, three points per white area L* 90 or above
Rub resistance 500 g load, 100 cycles No black show-through, no blurred edges
Fold 180°, five cycles, inspect the white film No cracks, no black exposed
Barcode grade ISO/IEC 15416 verifier, five samples C (1.5) or better

Four checks sound like work, but a lot takes fifteen minutes and removes the argument before it starts. The expensive outcome is not the second white hit or the extra varnish pass. It is 5,000 tags sitting at a garment factory, waiting to be attached, when someone notices the white has gone grey. A reprint takes seven to twelve days and the shipping window does not wait. These four lines on an inspection sheet cost one sheet of paper and lock the quality of the whole black-tag program.
