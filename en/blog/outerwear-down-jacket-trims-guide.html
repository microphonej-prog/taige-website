Outerwear and Down Jacket Trims: Warnings, Load and Packing

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 18, 2026

For outerwear and down jackets, the trims — hang tags, neck labels, care labels, poly bags — have to do more than look good: they must survive washing, hanging and packing on thick fabric, coatings, fillings and multi-layer construction. When warning information or load-bearing is wrong on a down jacket or shell, the after-sales cost dwarfs the trims themselves.

This guide follows three threads — wash resistance, warnings and load — through neck labels, care labels, hang tags and fasteners, and packing, and ends with a specification sheet ready for your enquiry.

1. Outerwear Trims: Wash, Warn, Bear Load

  • Wash: outerwear is washed less often but far more harshly — bulky loads, machine cycles, coatings — so labels must survive the whole garment tumbling and spinning
  • Warn: do-not-bleach, low-heat tumble dry, do-not-iron and professional dry-clean warnings carry return liability, so the wording must match fabric test results
  • Load: outerwear tags are larger, thicker and often carry security labels or several cards, so string and fastener must be chosen against total weight

2. Neck Label and Size Label on Heavy Fabric and Linings

  • Stitch to the lining, not the coated face: punching holes through a waterproof or coated shell damages the barrier, so place the label on the lining or the neck binding
  • Stitch density and thread: on heavy fabric tighten the stitch density and use a matched polyester thread to stop needle holes opening up and seams snapping
  • Size label placement: outerwear usually carries the size inside the collar or at the side seam — keep it consistent with the hang tag and care label, especially on export orders
  • Hanger loop: down jackets and coats ship on hangers, so the loop and binding must carry the full garment weight — confirm the pull strength

3. Care Labels: Warnings for Down, Coated and Water-Repellent Fabrics

The most common failure on outerwear care labels is a mismatch between symbols and wording: a tumble-dry symbol with do-not-tumble-dry text, or symbols alone with no note about fabric softener or about shaking the garment back to loft after drying. The table below summarises typical routes by fabric type; the final wording must follow your fabric tests and destination regulations.

Fabric typeTypical care directionCare label watch-outs
Down and down blends30°C gentle cycle, low spin, low-heat tumble or flat line dryMatch any do-not-dry-clean wording to the fabric; note low spin and loft recovery to avoid down-leak complaints
Coatings (PU, PVC) and waterproof breathable membranesGentle wash at or below 30°C, no bleach, no high-heat drying or ironingState no fabric softener; heat damages coatings and seam tape, and the symbols must agree
Wool and cashmere coatsWool cycle or professional dry clean, dry flat, never wringFibre percentages must match tests and symbols; premium coats often need multilingual care labels
Padded jackets with synthetic fillNormal machine wash, low-heat dryingDifferentiate from visually similar down styles so customers are not confused by down-specific wording

4. Hang Tag and Fastener: Weight and Snagging on Heavy Coats

  • Size the string to total weight: one tag is not the same as tag plus security label plus belly band — confirm cord type, core and length against real weight
  • Plastic versus metal fasteners: plastic suits most outerwear; metal fasteners fit premium coats but check fabric snagging and nickel-allergy requirements
  • Load point: on heavy coats attach the tag at the sleeve or a dedicated loop rather than the neckline, which distorts under load
  • Abrasion and creasing: outerwear tags get crushed in transit — laminate or varnish them and keep die-cut edges free of sharp notches

5. Packing: Stop Down Leakage, Limit Compression, Block Moisture

  • Down leakage: one garment per bag, and keep hard-edged cards or sharp tags from pressing directly on the shell to avoid leakage and snagging in transit
  • Venting: trapped moisture inside sealed bags makes down clump, so add vents or micro-perforated film — agree hole size and position with your buyer
  • Compression: heavy compression flattens loft, so match the carton to the garment instead of vacuum packing as a default
  • Moisture: on long sea freight add a barrier inner bag and desiccant — damp down develops odour
  • Split packing and barcodes: pack by store, colour or size with barcode labels and the buyer's inbound sorting speeds up markedly

6. Export and Freight: Cartons, Hanger Packs and Marking

  • For hanger packs, confirm hanger type, shoulder width and bag opening direction so the goods can go straight on the rail
  • Carton size must match container stacking; down jackets are bulky and a poor carton shape eats load factor
  • Print shipping marks and carton labels to the buyer's format, keeping them consistent with care-label languages and hang tag barcodes
