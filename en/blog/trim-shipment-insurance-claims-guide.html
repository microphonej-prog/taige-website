Trim Shipment Insurance and Cargo Damage Claims: Cover, Value and Documents

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 16, 2026

Garment trims are cheap per piece but the journey is long: from a Dongguan factory to a European or American warehouse the shipment passes through trucking, container consolidation, sea or air freight, devanning at the destination port and inland delivery. Any link can leave you with crushed cartons, water damage or a whole box missing. Because trims carry low value while freight takes a large share of the invoice, many buyers skip cargo insurance or declare a rough figure — and find the claim route closed when something actually happens. This guide sets out insurance, documents, damage handling and claims in order.

It runs in six parts: who insures and what cover is being bought, why trims are so often under-insured, how to choose the cover, the documents and endorsement, the order of action when damage occurs, and finally claim documents, deadlines and the usual reasons a claim is refused.

The table shows common practice only; the contract, the letter of credit and the policy wording govern. Different versions of the Incoterms differ in detail, so check clause by clause before they go into the order — above all the insured period and the cover required.

2. Why Trims Are So Often Under-Insured

  • Light cargo, heavy damage: hang tags, paper bags and woven labels are cheap to insure by weight, but they crush and damp easily, so the damage rate runs far above metal parts of the same weight.
  • Low goods value, high freight share: on a shipment worth a few thousand dollars with a few hundred dollars of freight, the insured value tends to be guessed — and the payout follows the lowest reading.
  • Many cartons, mixed batches: an order often runs to dozens of cartons across sizes and colours; the moment the packing list disagrees with the actual count, the settlement is discounted carton by carton.
  • Groupage is the highest-risk step: sharing a container with garments often puts trim cartons at the door or on the bottom, handled most often and most exposed to crushing and scuffing.
  • Split carriage blurs liability: trucking, sea freight and delivery are separate carriers with separate terms, so after an incident it is often impossible to pin down who caused the damage.
  • What works in practice: declare the insured value from the actual invoice amount including the costs of getting the goods to destination, and where trims share a container, note the carton numbers and their position in the packing list.

3. Choosing the Cover: Basic and Additional Risks

  • Three levels of basic cover: free of particular average, with average, and all risks, each wider than the last — read the policy wording rather than relying on the name.
  • Add cover to match the goods: paper and hang tags suffer most from rain water, damp and heat; poly bags and webbing from theft and tearing; metal parts from crushing and distortion.
  • War and strike risks: add them when the destination port or route carries risk; for some ports the importer writes the requirement into the letter of credit.
  • Work out the deductible first: policies usually carry one, and losses below it are not paid — for small, frequent shipments this has to be priced in from the start.
  • Match cover to value: for metal parts, leather patches and foil-stamped hang tags take all risks plus the relevant additions; for ordinary paper goods cover rain water, damp and breakage at the very least.

4. Documents Before Shipment and Endorsement

  • Insurance application and policy: goods, quantity, value, vessel or flight, origin and destination, the insured period (warehouse to warehouse or port to port), cover and deductible, item by item.
  • Commercial invoice and packing list: state the contents, count, gross and net weight and carton number of every box so the claim can be checked box by box; a vague packing list is a voluntary surrender of part of the settlement.
  • Bill of lading or air waybill: check consignee, notify party and freight terms, so the goods do not arrive at destination with nobody able to release them.
  • The policy must be endorsable: under CIF and CIP it has to be endorsable to the buyer, otherwise the buyer receives damaged goods with no right of claim.
  • Shipment notice: send the policy number, cover, deductible and claims contact with the shipping notice — not once the damage has already happened.

5. When Damage Happens: the Order of Action Decides the Claim

  • Step one, photograph the same day: carton exterior, seals, damaged areas, stowage position and the damaged items themselves, all in their original state.
  • Step two, hold the goods: keep the original packing, cushioning and labels; speak to the insurer or surveyor before reworking or discarding anything.
  • Step three, give the carrier written notice: sea bills of lading set a clear deadline for written notice, usually a few days after discharge, and air and express services have their own; a phone call does not count.
  • Step four, ask for a joint or third-party survey: larger losses need a survey report, and having the consignee present with a signed record saves a full round of correspondence.
  • Step five, separate the three causes: damage in transit, damage after opening and damage from inadequate packing demand completely different evidence, so agree on the cause with client, carrier and insurer before acting.
  • Step six, cost the repair first: where rework or replacement is needed, document the cost before anything else — never burn the evidence by using the goods anyway.

6. Claim Documents, Deadlines and Five Common Refusals

  • Usual documents: letter of claim, policy, bill of lading or air waybill, commercial invoice, packing list, survey report, photographs, the carrier's written reply and proof of rework or replacement cost.
  • Deadlines: written notice to a sea carrier has the shortest window, while the time limit for claiming on the insurer sits in the policy; checking and notifying on the day of receipt is always the safest route.
  • Goods never arriving, or a whole carton lost: obtain written confirmation from the carrier first, then follow the non-delivery or total loss route in the policy — never reship on your own without a written record.
  • Refusal one: the insured value was declared by weight or by guesswork below the invoice amount, so any settlement is reduced proportionally.
  • Refusal two: the packing did not meet the contract or normal industry practice and was ruled inadequate — the single most common refusal on paper goods.
  • Refusal three: no written notice to the carrier within the deadline, so the chain of liability breaks at the first link and no later evidence can repair it.
  • Refusal four: the original packing was thrown away, so transit damage cannot be told apart from damage after opening and the loss becomes a contest of opinions.
  • Refusal five: the policy was never endorsed, so the buyer has a loss but no standing to claim and comes back to the shipper instead.
💡 Practical tip: three things before shipment decide whether a claim can ever succeed — declare the insured value from the invoice, state the contents and carton numbers on the packing list, and build the cartons to survive the journey. They cost almost nothing and prevent most damage and most arguments; add photographs and written notice on the day of receipt and you will not lose on deadlines either.
