Cord Locks, Toggles and Drawstring Hardware: Diameter, Safety and Inspection

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 15, 2026

Cord locks, toggles and cord ends are the smallest hardware on a hooded jacket, a pair of joggers, a raincoat or a backpack, and they decide three things at once: whether a hood stays cinched in wind, whether the cord still slides after five washes, and whether the shipment clears children's drawstring rules. This guide covers part types, cord diameter matching, child safety, wash and corrosion resistance, and what to check on arrival.

1. Know the parts: spring lock, springless lock, toggle, cord end, eyelet

The barrel cord lock is the default: shell plus spring plus press button, one-handed adjustment, right for hoods and waistbands. Springless locks hold by shell elasticity or an inclined friction face: simpler, cheaper, one grade weaker, fine for light shells. Toggles are usually two-hole or three-hole shaped pieces where the cord provides friction by path, decorative and more expensive per piece. The cord end (aglet) contains the yarn so the tip does not unravel and sets how smoothly the cord feeds; the eyelet is the exit through the fabric, and a hole 0.5–1 mm larger than the cord runs best.

Part Cord diameter Typical material Typical failure
Spring cord lock, single hole 3–6 mm POM, nylon Spring seizing, button snapping
Springless lock 3–5 mm POM, ABS Cord slipping loose over time
Toggle, two or three hole 4–8 mm POM, zinc alloy Burrs abrading the cord, coating wear
Cord end, metal or plastic 3–5 mm Nickel-plated brass, zinc alloy, TPU Crimp loosening, nickel release over limit
Eyelet 4–8 mm inner Brass, stainless steel Plating flaking, fabric tearing

2. Cord diameter is the only entry dimension, and getting it wrong scraps the batch

A lock sold as 4 mm may actually accept 3.6–4.3 mm, and cotton and hollow polyester cord of the same diameter differ a lot in density: a 5 mm cotton cord flattens easily while a solid 4 mm polyester cord may not go in at all. Measure the cord after the real process — after pre-shrinking, after pressing, after filling — and take the thickest of the three as the entry dimension, with a tolerance of 0.2 mm. Shell material decides feel too: POM self-lubricates and wears well, nylon stays tougher in the cold and still works at minus 20 °C, ABS is cheaper but goes brittle in winter.

3. Child safety: drawstring rules are the hard line for this hardware

Europe judges children's cords under EN 14682: no freely moving cords at the neck or hood, waist adjusters protruding no more than 14 cm on each side of the channel exit, and hem cords kept inside the garment. The US rule, 16 CFR 1120, bars drawstrings at the hood and neck on children's upper outerwear in sizes 2T to 12, and limits waist and hem drawstrings to 7.5 cm beyond the garment when laid flat. Both point straight at part selection: anything destined for a hood should move to a concealed adjuster or be removed, not worked around with a removable lock.

  • Under-threes: no drawstring at the neck at all. Use a mock cord or a fixed bow instead of a working one.
  • School-age: put the waist lock on the inside, keep exposed cord within 14 cm, and crimp the cord ends so nothing frays.
  • Korea and Japan: children's drawstrings fall under child product safety rules as well, with KC certification and mandatory marking in Korea, so certificates and labels must ship with the goods.

4. Washing, corrosion and pressing

Metal parts rarely fail in a salt-spray cabinet first; they fail in a domestic drum machine with detergent. A zinc alloy cord end in chlorine-bearing detergent at 40 °C shows white corrosion after about twenty washes. Nickel-plated brass holds better, but plating under 3 μm exposes the base metal at the edges first. For Europe, test metal parts for nickel release to REACH Annex XVII entry 27 by the EN 1811 method, and print on the care label that metal parts should be washed inside out and not soaked. Plastic locks are more fragile in pressing: POM softens around 150 °C, and steam pressing directly onto a lock leaves a permanent mark.

Test Standard or basis Suggested limit
Nickel release EN 1811, REACH Annex XVII entry 27 0.5 μg/cm²/week or less for prolonged skin contact
Cord retention In-house pull test 50 N held for 10 seconds without slip or deformation
Cycle life In-house cycling Still locks after 3,000 presses
Appearance after wash Five washes at 40 °C No corrosion spots, no plating loss, no dye transfer

5. Colour, coating and colour matching

The usual failure with drawstring hardware is a correct colour code with the wrong surface. The same code reads matte on a moulded POM part, semi-gloss on a painted zinc alloy part and mirror-like on a plated one, and the three side by side at one neckline read as three colours. Settle the finish first, then match: compare matte parts to a colour chip at grade 4 on the grey scale, and for plated or painted parts ask for a physical sample rather than ordering on a code. Ask whether the colour is masterbatch or dye-based: masterbatch black is far more UV-stable, while dyed navy tends to shift red within six months.

6. Ordering, packing and inspection

Unit prices are low and quantities are small, so the real cost sits in sorting and replacement. Order from 1,000 pieces per colour and size where possible, which matches the 500–1,000 piece masterbatch minimum most factories run, and take delivery at 1.05 times the garment count so the extra 5% absorbs line losses. Bagging 100 pieces per bag with the colour code and size printed on it is the most practical format; mixed bulk is cheaper, but one picking error puts the wrong colour into a whole run. Inspect to AQL 2.5 and check three things: whether the bore passes a go-gauge for the matching cord, whether the press action is smooth, and whether there is flash or sink marking.

Three details that get skipped. First, EU-bound styles: GPSR (EU) 2023/988 requires an economic operator in the EU and traceability, and although trim never appears on the product label, purchase records must trace back to a batch. Second, the care symbols have to match what the metal actually survives: printing machine washable while using a zinc alloy cord end that cannot take it manufactures its own complaints. Third, Japan and Korea size and mark differently: Japan states finished measurements in cm with country of origin, Korea uses Korean sizing such as 55, 66 or 77 with mandatory care marking, so confirm label language before finalising packaging.

Reduced to a purchase sheet, all of this is five columns: part type, entry diameter with tolerance, shell material and finish, wash and metal test requirements, and packing plus label language. Get those five right and the sample approves in one round, and the line does not stop in peak season over twenty cord locks. Drawstring hardware is under one percent of garment cost and yet it generates the most complaints and the least recoverable damage, which justifies ten more minutes before the order is placed.
