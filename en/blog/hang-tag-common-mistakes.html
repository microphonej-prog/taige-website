Hang Tag Ordering Mistakes to Avoid: 10 Common Problems and Fixes

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 12, 2026

Hang tag customisation looks simple — pick a paper, print a logo, punch a hole, add a string. But any brand that has actually placed an order knows the mistakes tend to surface only when the cartons arrive, or worse, when the tags are already on garments in store. This guide collects the ten most common hang tag ordering mistakes in the apparel trims industry and gives you fixes you can act on immediately, plus a pre-order checklist.

Most problems are not about what a factory can produce — they come from details that were never written down before the order. Confirm each of the checkpoints below and you will avoid more than nine out of ten rework or top-up situations.

1. Artwork: Bleed, Safe Margin and Resolution

Artwork errors are the most frustrating category, because nothing looks wrong on screen — the problem only appears on the printed sheet.

  • No bleed — die-cutting and trimming shift by 1–3 mm, so edge-to-edge colour gets a white sliver. Leave 3 mm of bleed as standard.
  • Text too close to the edge — keep at least 3 mm from the trim line, or letters get sliced in half.
  • Low-resolution images — printing needs an effective 300 dpi. Pictures saved from websites or messaging apps are usually 72 dpi and turn blurry when enlarged.
  • Designing in RGB — screens are RGB, printing is CMYK. Fluorescent RGB colours simply cannot be reproduced in CMYK and will darken and dull after conversion, so set up the file in CMYK from the start.

2. Size and Punch Holes: Two Numbers Everyone Forgets

  • No hole position on the drawing — if you only say 'punch a hole', the factory centres it or places it by habit, and the tag hangs crooked or off-balance. Specify the distance from hole centre to the edge and the hole diameter (3–5 mm is standard).
  • Approximate sizes — tag width changes the whole hanging impression. 35×70 mm reads delicate, 54×90 mm reads bold, and the same size feels different on different paper weights, so confirm with a sample first.
  • Ignoring the attachment thickness — thick cotton cord or a metal eyelet demands a larger hole and stronger tag stock; a hole placed too close to the edge tears easily.

3. Mismatched Materials and Finishes

Materials and finishes are not freely interchangeable — the wrong pairing weakens the result or simply cannot be produced.

Wrong pairing What goes wrong Better approach
Kraft paper + large solid dark blocks Kraft is brown-toned, so colours turn muddy Use one ink or a white-based design on kraft
Thin paper (under 250 gsm) + large foil area Heat and pressure cause bubbling and warping Use 300 gsm or heavier for foil, or shrink the foil area
Matte lamination + spot UV, applied in the wrong order UV first, film second — the film kills the gloss contrast and the extra cost is wasted Laminate matte first, then apply spot UV
Cotton or textured paper + fine small type The texture swallows detail and small type fills in Keep textured paper for logos and large type only
Recycled paper + tight colour standard Recycled stock shades unevenly; deviation is wider than on white board Accept a wider tolerance, or switch to white board

4. Underestimating Colour Deviation

  • Treating an on-screen colour as the standard — the same file looks different on every phone and monitor. Supply a Pantone number or a physical colour sample instead.
  • Forgetting how stock and finishing shift colour — the same Pantone reads differently on coated, kraft or uncoated paper, and matte lamination deepens the tone slightly.
  • Treating all deviation as a defect — offset printing has a normal batch-to-batch tolerance. Agree on an acceptable range up front (for example within ΔE 2–3) rather than arguing after delivery.

5. Missing Information and Compliance Content

  • No size, fibre content or country of origin — many markets mandate these on garment labelling; a missing element can block customs clearance or delist a product.
  • Barcodes or QR codes printed too small or too close to the edge — a small barcode will not scan, and a QR code without a quiet zone fails too. White space around the code matters more than size.
  • Wrong or missing contact details — a hang tag is brand collateral; an incorrect email, phone number or URL loses customers, and details are often left stale after a rebrand.
  • Multi-language versions out of sync — a tag aimed at export markets that only carries Chinese, or French and Spanish handled by raw machine translation, reads as unprofessional.

6. MOQ, Sampling and Lead-Time Management

The last category hurts the most: a tag that misses the on-shelf date. Sampling usually takes 3–7 days and bulk production 10–25 days, and special papers, foil dies or custom cutting dies add tooling and material lead time on top. Work backwards from your shipping date and lock the sample approval and tooling confirmation early, or the factory ends up rushing and quality slips.

💡 Tip: Put hole position and diameter, paper weight, Pantone references, barcode size and packing method into the order confirmation, and have the factory sign it back. These five items prevent the vast majority of rework.

7. Pre-Order Checklist

  • Artwork has 3 mm bleed, text sits ≥3 mm from the trim line, images are 300 dpi and the file is CMYK
  • Hole count and diameter are dimensioned on the drawing, and the attachment has been confirmed
  • Paper, weight, lamination and Pantone references are confirmed in writing
  • Size, fibre content, origin, and barcode or QR size meet the target market's rules
  • Lead time is planned backwards from 3–7 days sampling plus 10–25 days production, with shipping and customs buffer
