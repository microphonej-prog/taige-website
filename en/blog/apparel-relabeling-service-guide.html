Apparel Relabeling Guide: Swapping Hang Tags, Care Labels and Barcodes

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 13, 2026

Apparel relabeling is the operation of replacing or supplementing the hang tags, neck labels, care labels, size labels and barcode price tickets on garments that are already made, so they match a new market, channel or brand requirement. It is triggered when a brand moves from contract manufacturing to its own label, from domestic retail to cross-border e-commerce, from full-price to off-price channels, or when the labelling rules of a target market change. It looks like swapping one hang tag, but it actually moves four things at once: fabric, stitching, inventory systems and compliance liability. Done well, it gives tens of thousands of finished pieces a second life; done badly, a whole shipment stalls at the warehouse or at the channel door.

This guide has five parts: first the situations that force a relabel, then the six trim items a relabel actually touches, then a comparison of removing the old label, overlabelling and full rework, then the six details that go wrong most often, and finally how to structure the quote and how to inspect.

1. Six Situations That Force a Relabel

  • A change in market rules: the target market has added mandatory fibre content, importer details or care symbols, and the old label is no longer compliant. This relabel is not optional — compliance comes before selling.
  • A channel switch: stock moving from full-price stores to off-price or a cross-border platform must shed the original price information, store codes and promotion marks, otherwise it undermines the full-price structure.
  • Moving from contract work to your own label: the factory neck label and the previous customer's hang tags all have to go, so no other brand enters your channel — the most common relabel request of all.
  • A size-system switch: Asian size labels must become EU or US sizes, or one garment carries two size logics across two lines. Size labels and hang tags change together, or return rates climb noticeably.
  • Platform and retail-system demands: an e-commerce warehouse wants a scannable barcode price ticket, a retail customer wants SSCC carton marks and store-level barcodes — all of it must be applied before the goods are received, because changing it inside the warehouse costs far more.
  • Wear and tear on the labels themselves: clearance stock, showroom samples and rental or multi-brand garments come back with washed-out care labels, torn hang tags and loose stitching — they need new labels or restitching before they can go back on the shelf.

2. A Relabel Is a Whole Trim Set, Not One Hang Tag

Buyers usually ask for the hang tag alone, yet what actually stalls a delivery at the warehouse or the store is nearly always one of the items nobody mentioned. The table below lines up the six trim items with the points most often missed.

Hang tagReprint the whole set including barcode, price field and string; keeping colour, size and hole position unchanged saves the most workOld and new strings mixed, and one old tag left inside the carton
Neck labelUsually unpicked and resewn; check first whether unpicking leaves needle holes at the necklineA wrinkled neckline or holes in the fabric; neck label and care label showing different sizes
Care labelRemade for the target market's languages and symbol set, including composition, care, origin and importer detailsSymbols contradicting the text, a missing language version, composition percentages copied from the old label
Size labelChanged in the same place as the neck label, and never two size logics inside one batchPosition drift, and two size systems found in one carton

Packing and seal labelsBags, barcode stickers, tamper seals and carton marks all updated with the new channelThe old channel name, address or hotline still printed on the bag

3. Three Ways to Do the Job

  • Unpick and replace: the cleanest look, suited to markets with strict brand-ownership expectations. It also costs the most in labour and damage, and unpicking can leave needle holes — knits and light fabrics need real care.
  • Overlabelling: a new label placed over the old one — fast and cheap, well suited to functional content such as barcodes, prices and channel data. Thickness, edge feel and whether it survives washing must all be checked, and the target market has to accept the practice in the first place.
  • Full rework: the last resort, for labels that cannot be removed (debossed leather patches, heat transfers, laser marking) — effectively running the whole trim process again. Such stock is scheduled like a new order, samples and lead time included.
💡 Tip: build the relabel checklist from the physical garment, not from the drawing. Change all six items on a sample, wash it once and measure thickness again before writing the bulk work standard — that half day usually saves a whole rework.

4. Six Details That Go Wrong Most Often

  • Old labels not fully removed: relabelled goods are most likely to hide another brand or an old price inside a cuff, side seam or inner pocket. Spot-checking physical pieces per carton before shipping costs far less than a channel return.
  • Damage while unpicking: a seam ripper that slips or moves too fast scores the fabric or widens needle holes. Knits, silk, coated fabrics and light chiffon need slower work and finer tools, plus a trial unpick on a sample first.
  • Position and stitch inconsistency on resewing: a care label resewn 3mm off, or with a tighter stitch, is hard to see — yet returns often start exactly there. The work standard must fix position, stitch length and thread, with a reference piece hanging at the workstation.
  • Barcodes out of step with the system: the goods get new labels while the stock system keeps old item codes, sizes and carton specs, so scanning fails or the receipt does not match the order. Build the old-to-new code map first and verify by scanning before bulk application.
  • Compliance done by halves: language versions, fibre content, origin and importer details form one set — missing any one of them is non-compliance. Multilingual labels most often lose the smaller languages, so put the language list in the specification from the quote stage.
  • Authorization and liability left vague: relabelling can touch the original trademark, customer authorization and liability boundaries. Confirm the manufacturing relationship and the authorization scope in writing first, so helping a client relabel never turns into an infringement risk; for own-brand relabels, keep a record of how the old labels were disposed of.

5. How to Read the Quote, How to Inspect the Goods

A relabel quote is normally the sum of two lines: the trim cost itself (hang tags, care labels, barcode stickers priced by material and finishing) and the processing fee (per piece, driven directly by how hard the removal is and whether sewing is involved). Answer four questions clearly and quotes become comparable.

  • Relabel volume and how it splits into cartons: whether one batch must be divided across several channels and label sets.
  • The type of old label: paper, woven, leather, heat transfer or sewn — this sets the labour and the damage rate.
  • Target market and language list: how many language versions, and whether importer details and care symbols are included.
  • Lead time and where the work happens: relabelling locally in Dongguan, or after the goods move to a third-party warehouse.

Inspection runs on sampling: set the ratio first (usually sampling by both cartons and pieces), check the six trim items for appearance, position, content and scanability, and keep pass and fail pieces apart. Archive one reference piece and the work photos, so any later dispute has evidence.
