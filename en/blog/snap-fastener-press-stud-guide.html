Snap Fasteners and Press Studs: Sizing, Holding Force and Setting

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 16, 2026

Snap fasteners, meaning ring snaps, spring snaps, prong snaps and sew-on snaps, are the most used closure on kidswear, down jackets, workwear and bags, and the easiest to get returned. Too loose and the placket pops open when the wearer lifts an arm; too tight and it cannot be closed one-handed; the wrong prong length and it works loose after two washes. This guide turns snaps from a matter of feel into numbers you can put in a specification: sizing systems, holding force, materials and plating, setting, and acceptance.

1. Four Families, Sorted Before You Specify

A ring snap carries a spring coil, holds its force over thousands of cycles and is the first choice for kidswear and workwear. A spring snap uses a stamped spring leaf: cheapest, but the force fades fastest. A prong snap pierces the fabric and clinches without a die-set base, which suits light fabric and small runs. A sew-on snap is stitched in place and is the only safe option on knits and materials that must not be pierced. They are not interchangeable: prong snaps set into heavy denim bend their prongs, and spring snaps on jersey tear the fabric before they release.

  • Knit, wool, silk: sew-on only; a pierced hole is permanent damage
  • Down and padded jackets: spring snap with a backing plate, to avoid tearing the shell
  • Kidswear and babygrows: prefer ring snaps, and confirm pull strength and small-parts safety

2. Sizing: Ligne Converted to Millimetres

The trade still sizes snaps in ligne, where 1 L equals 0.635 mm. It is a historical unit, not an exact diameter: two 15 L caps from different makers can differ by 0.3 mm in outside diameter, and that 0.3 mm decides whether the cap covers the existing hole. So specify 15 L with a 9.5 mm cap diameter and a tolerance of plus or minus 0.15 mm. Writing 15 L alone writes nothing.

Size Cap diameter (approx.) Typical use
12 L 7.6 mm Babygrows, light pocket flaps
15 L 9.5 mm Shirt plackets, kids jackets
20 L 12.7 mm Workwear, down jackets, bags

3. Holding Force: Turning Feel Into Newtons

Holding force is measured in two directions: pull-apart and shear. Placket snaps mostly see shear and must be specified on that value; collars and pocket flaps mostly see pull-apart. A 15 L garment snap typically lands between 20 and 45 N pull-apart, and a 20 L between 35 and 60 N. Kidswear should sit lower so a parent can open it one-handed while a toddler cannot. Always fix the figure against a sealed sample with a measured value, and specify a range, not a single number.

Holding force drops with washing. Require a re-test after five home washes and change the spec or the supplier if it falls by more than thirty percent; that single rule prevents most complaints six months later.

4. Material and Plating: Nickel Release Is the Hard Gate

The usual materials are brass, which is durable and takes fine threads; stainless steel, the most corrosion resistant and the most expensive; zinc alloy, cheap and suited to larger parts; and POM, a plastic snap with no metal-allergy risk but poor heat resistance. Where metal sits against skin for long periods, Annex XVII of REACH (EC) No 1907/2006 restricts nickel release, screened by EN 1811 and re-tested after wear simulation to EN 12472. For the US, CPSIA and the small-parts rule at 16 CFR 1501 apply. For Japan, textile goods carry fibre composition and care labelling under the Household Goods Quality Labelling Act, while metal parts fall under product liability. Nickel-free routes such as tin plating, copper-nickel with an organic topcoat, or POM need a third-party report, not a sample.

5. Setting: Die, Prong Length and Three Trial Strikes

Loose snaps are mostly a setting problem, not a snap problem. Three points: the die must match the snap brand, since a one or two degree difference in the curve angle between makers crimps them crooked; prong length must equal material thickness plus 0.8 to 1.2 mm, measured at three different points first; and strike three trials on real material before production, checking that prongs roll outward evenly, the cap sits flat, and the action has no grit. Keep the trial records and compare them when the fabric or season changes.

6. Acceptance: Five Checks Are Enough

  • Pull: ten pieces per lot, holding force within fifteen percent of the sealed sample
  • Wash decay: re-test after five washes, no more than thirty percent loss
  • Nickel release: a third-party report inside its validity and matching this batch
  • Appearance: no die marks, no colour drift, no burrs, no exposed substrate
  • Function: still opens and closes one-handed after 500 cycles, without jamming
