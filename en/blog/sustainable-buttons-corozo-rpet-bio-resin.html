Sustainable Buttons: Corozo, Recycled PET and Bio-Resin Compared

By TAGE Packaging · Updated November 25, 2026

A shirt carries eight to eleven buttons. On a 180 g/m² cotton shirt they weigh under twelve grams in total, and they are still the first component a buyer asks about when a sustainability brief lands. We make buttons and trims in Dongguan, and the question has changed shape in two years: it used to be whether the Pantone could be matched, now it is whether the material holds up in the brand's own report. This guide compares the three materials that survive a sample round — corozo, recycled PET and bio-resin — and sets out the certificates, tests and wording that keep a custom button order from turning into a claim problem.

1. Three Materials That Survive a Sample Round

Corozo is the seed of the tagua palm, cut and polished like a plastic button but with a grain no mould reproduces. Recycled PET comes from bottle flake, regranulated and moulded on the same presses as virgin resin. Bio-resin usually means a starch or PLA blend where part of the carbon is plant-derived. All three can be dyed, laser-engraved and barrel-polished to a matte or gloss finish; they behave very differently in a wash drum and on an audit desk.

Corozo (tagua nut) Natural, no recycled-content figure Warm grain, natural figure Good in wash; dry below 80 °C 1.2–2× resin Batch colour shift, origin paperwork
Recycled PET (rPET) GRS chain of custody, content on the TC Close to standard resin, gloss adjustable Same as virgin resin 1.2–1.6× resin Claim needs a TC, not a logo

Recycled wood or coconut shell Natural material Rustic, visible grain Hand wash only 1.5–2× resin Wide tolerance, cracking

2. The Certificate Is Not the Product

A GRS certificate proves chain of custody, not performance. It says the recycled content was tracked from the recycler through the button factory to your carton. It says nothing about whether the dye bleeds at 40 °C or whether the moulded edge cracks after a drop. Buyers conflate the two constantly, and a supplier who lets them is storing up a complaint.

  • GRS scope certificate (site and product scope) plus a transaction certificate per shipment. Look the number up in the certification body's public database; a PDF in an email proves nothing on its own.
  • OEKO-TEX STANDARD 100 for the finished button, ECO PASSPORT for dyestuffs and auxiliaries. Check the product class — anything a baby may mouth needs Class I.
  • Childrenswear buttons need a third-party EN 71-3 (migration of certain elements) report, dated within twelve months.
  • For dyed corozo or bio-resin, add azo dye statements and an ISO 105-E04 perspiration fastness report.
  • Ask which plant actually moulded the button. If the answer is a trading company, the certificate scope probably does not cover the order.

3. Two Tests That Decide the Sample

Buttons fail in the wash in two ways: colour leaves the button and lands on a pale garment, or the button cracks. Both are testable before bulk, and both cost less to test than to replace.

  • Five domestic washes at 40 °C following the care label, then grade colour change on the grey scale against the original — grade 4 or better.
  • ISO 105-E04 perspiration fastness, acid and alkaline, grade 4 or better.
  • ASTM D5171 impact resistance for plastic sew-through buttons; agree drop height and weight with the buyer instead of accepting a generic pass.
  • Measure ten buttons with a micrometer. Button size is quoted in ligne, one ligne being 0.635 mm, and a resin button usually holds ±0.2 mm.
  • Check hole spacing and rim thickness, not only diameter: cracks almost always start at a rim that is too thin.

4. The Wording Problem: Biodegradable Button

There is no product standard for a biodegradable button. EN 13432 covers packaging, and it tests disintegration under industrial composting conditions around 58 °C — an environment no wardrobe reproduces. A PLA button will not disappear in a drawer, and it will not disappear in landfill either. Writing 100% biodegradable on a hang tag is therefore a claim with nothing behind it, which is exactly the sentence regulators in Europe and North America now pursue.

Replace the adjective with a figure: 60% plant-based material, supplier-declared, or recycled PET, GRS certified, TC no. 12345. A number survives an audit; an adjective does not.
  • Recycled content: state post-consumer or pre-consumer, and the percentage the TC actually covers.
  • Bio-based: state the plant-carbon share and the test method (ASTM D6866 measures biobased carbon content), not the word eco.
  • Recyclable: only if a collection route exists for that size and material where the garment is sold.

5. Market Notes

In the EU, PPWR (EU) 2025/40 governs packaging, not buttons, so a button claim lives under the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive and, once in force, the Green Claims Directive. In the US, the FTC Green Guides require recycled-content claims to be qualified. Both regimes ask the same thing: keep the evidence next to the claim, and put the weight in g/m², the diameter in mm and the care-label composition on the same specification sheet.

6. What to Send for a Quote

  • Material, and the certificates you must be able to show: GRS TC, OEKO-TEX, EN 71-3.
  • Size in ligne and in mm, plus hole count and spacing — a two-hole blank is not the same as a four-hole.
  • Colour reference: Pantone for dyed buttons, a physical sample for corozo, which never matches exactly.
  • Finish: barrel polish, matte, laser logo, engraved rim.
  • Quantity per colour; custom dye usually starts at 3,000–5,000 pieces per colour.
  • Care label wash temperature and destination market, because the test plan follows both.
