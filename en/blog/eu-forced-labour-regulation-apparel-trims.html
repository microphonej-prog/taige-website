The EU Forced Labour Regulation and Apparel Trims: What to Prepare Before 2027

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 11, 2026

The EU Forced Labour Regulation, Regulation (EU) 2024/3015, entered into force on 13 December 2024 and its product ban applies from 14 December 2027: products made wholly or partly with forced labour may not be placed on the EU market, made available there, or exported from it. Unlike the US UFLPA it is not a presumption at the border — national authorities investigate and then ban specific products. For apparel trims suppliers this means that before 2027 EU customers will attach supply chain questionnaires, origin declarations and labour records to the purchase contract. Building the document system now turns next year into maintenance.

1. The Regulation Targets Products, Not Company Size

  • Three prohibitions: placing on the EU market, making available there and exporting from it — which together covers exporters and importers
  • No size threshold: the Regulation applies to all economic operators with no turnover or headcount limit; SMEs get support measures and guidance rather than an exemption
  • A different burden of proof: the EU side opens investigations and issues decisions; suppliers are not the enforcing target, but once a decision names a product the customer can only switch supplier or material
  • Guidance before enforcement: the European Commission is to issue implementation guidelines before the ban applies, and national authorities will work from them — so customer requests will follow the same logic

2. How It Works: From Risk Database to Product Withdrawal

  1. The European Commission builds a forced labour risk database and a single portal, flagging risk by geographic area and product category as the basis for authorities and companies
  2. Member states designate competent authorities that assess risk, open investigations into suspect products and can require companies to submit supply chain information and evidence
  3. If forced labour is established, the authority issues a decision banning the product from being placed, made available or exported, and requiring withdrawal, recall or disposal as specified
  4. Companies must comply within the period set in the decision, generally up to 30 working days; failure to comply triggers penalties under national law, with severity set by each member state

3. How It Divides Work with UFLPA, CSDDD and Social Audits

4. Why Trims Suppliers Get Asked Too

  • The order is assessed as a garment: risk lists are built around the finished product, and hang tags, woven labels and bags ship with the garment, so they enter the same questionnaire
  • Long process chain: spinning, weaving, dyeing, printing, plating and moulding may be outsourced, and any unexplained step has to be filled in
  • Complex employment forms: seasonal workers, agency labour and piece-rate teams are exactly where risk flags land and where documents are most often missing
  • Upstream matters too: pulp, cotton yarn, recycled feedstock and packaging material origin fall inside the review, and trims buyers often ask in passing

5. What Customers Request, and Where Files Fall Short

DocumentWhat the customer wants to seeCommon gap
Supply chain mapSupplier name, site, process and share of work at every tierOnly tier one is listed; outsourced and agency units are missing
Raw material origin declarationOrigin and sourcing statement for yarn, pulp or chemical feedstockOnly made in China, without supporting documents or upstream statements
Employment and working hour recordsAttendance, payroll, employment contracts and evidence that overtime is voluntaryAttendance and payroll do not reconcile, or only totals exist without detail
Audit and system certificatesBSCI or SMETA reports, or ISO 9001/45001 system documentsThe report has expired or does not cover the actual production site

6. Six Things to Do Before 2027

  1. Build one supply chain map per main product category — hang tags, woven labels, care labels, bags — carried down to dyeing, printing, plating and outsourced steps
  2. Register agency and temporary labour properly: agency name, headcount, induction training and how contracts are signed
  3. Make attendance, payroll and working hour records reconcile, with voluntary overtime signed off or logged in the system
  4. Request origin declarations and compliance commitments upstream, especially written statements for pulp and cotton yarn
  5. Keep audit reports within validity and make sure the audit scope covers the real production site
  6. File shipment documents by batch, sharing one numbering system with your UFLPA traceability file, and run a pre-shipment check
💡 Tip: the EU ban only applies in 2027, but customer questionnaires usually arrive a year or two earlier and are copied to the end brand. Preparing one reusable pack — supply chain map, origin declarations, labour records and audit reports — is far less work than assembling documents in a hurry.
