EN 14682 Drawcord Rules for Childrenswear

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 18, 2026

One drawstring in a hood is enough to hold a shipment at EU customs or to trigger a CPSC recall in the United States. Cords on children's clothing are not a design preference; they are a safety specification with fixed numbers. EN 14682 reduces the whole argument to three figures — 134 cm, 140 mm and 75 mm — that decide which zone, which size and how much length is allowed. This guide reads those numbers from a trim supplier's side and shows how to lock them into a spec sheet, a measurement routine and a test report before the goods leave the factory.

1. Scope: Ages 0 to 14, Stature up to 170 cm

EN 14682 covers cords and drawstrings on children's clothing: hood drawcords, waist cords, hem drawstrings, decorative ribbons, zipper pulls, toggles and danglers. The trigger is stature, not the age label — an 8-year-old style cut in 164 cm falls into the 134–170 cm band and is treated differently from the same style in 120 cm. In practice this lands on the trim supplier's desk as a request to state, in the spec sheet, which zone the cord is used in and the maximum free-end length in millimetres — not just tape width and colour.

2. Zone A, Hood and Neck: Zero Tolerance up to 134 cm

  • Stature 134 cm and below (roughly 0 to 7 years): no cords, drawstrings or tape of any kind in the hood and neck area, including purely decorative ribbons.
  • Stature 134–170 cm: drawcords are allowed, but with no free ends, no toggles, cord locks or danglers, and no loops projecting beyond the garment edge.
  • Hard pulls in the hood and neck area, such as zipper sliders, are capped at 75 mm; longer means you have handed a child something to catch on.

3. Zone B, Chest and Waist, and Zone C, Below Hip: the 140 mm Free End

Zone Free end limit Hardware
B: chest and waist ≤140 mm per end at maximum garment size No toggles or danglers on free ends
C: below hip and hem Must not hang more than 140 mm below the hem No tying at the back of the garment
Cord locks (Zone B) Only on the fixed end, never forming a catchable loop Metal or plastic parts must pass small-parts pull testing

The 140 mm figure is easy to get wrong: it is not the length showing when the garment lies flat, but what still hangs out at each end once the garment is expanded to maximum size. Measure with the waist pulled to its largest setting and the cord hanging naturally, then read from the fabric edge to the tip. The same cord can differ by more than 30 mm between flat and expanded, which is exactly the difference between a pass and a fail.

4. ASTM F1816 in the United States: a Second 75 mm Line

The United States has no single regulation mirroring EN 14682; in practice ASTM F1816 plus the CPSIA substantial product hazard provision do the work. On upper outerwear in sizes 2T to 12: no drawstrings in the hood and neck; waist and hem drawstrings must not protrude more than 3 inches (75 mm) when the garment is expanded; no toggles or other attachments on drawstring ends. The consequence is not a correction notice but a published CPSC recall — the most expensive line item a single trim component can create.

5. Japan and Korea: No Equivalent Mandatory Standard, but Labels Still Apply

Japan and Korea have no mandatory cord standard bearing the EN 14682 name, so buyers usually write “EN 14682” into the spec sheet to keep one global supply chain. Label duties are not waived, though: in Japan the Household Goods Quality Labelling Act requires fibre composition, care labelling and country of origin, and any change of cord material forces an update of the care label. In Korea, household goods fall under the Electrical Appliances and Consumer Products Safety Control Act with its safety criteria and labelling duties, while children's products are additionally subject to safety confirmation under the Special Act on the Safety of Children's Products, with fibre content, care labelling and origin as standard fields.

6. Putting It into a Spec Sheet, a Measuring Routine and a Test

  • Pin three fields in the spec sheet: zone (A, B or C), maximum free-end length in mm, and hardware type, and state which EN 14682 size band you are designing to.
  • Measure in two states: flat and expanded, and judge on the larger value. Sample five pieces per lot and record the actual millimetre reading from fabric edge to tip.
  • Component safety: cord locks, danglers and metal ends need small-parts pull testing, with reports and lot numbers kept. A part that can be classed as a detachable small object jumps a risk level immediately.
  • Do not forget packaging and hang tags: a hang tag is itself a detachable object on childrenswear, so its string length and attachment method belong in the spec too — never a long string looped at the neck.
Practical note: the EN 14682 figures are not recommendations. EU market surveillance measures shipment samples directly and can demand withdrawal on the spot. If one cord is shared by a 120 cm and a 150 cm version of the same style, you need two specs — the most common design gap we see in childrenswear trims.
