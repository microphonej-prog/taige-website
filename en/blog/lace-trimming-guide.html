Lace and Trim Guide: Constructions, Materials and Quality Control

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 4, 2026

Lace and decorative trim are what turn a basic garment into something considered — and they are also the trim category that causes the most trouble: snagging, curling, edge fraying, shrinkage and wash deformation nearly all start here. Choose lace by construction, material, end use and care conditions, not by the pattern alone.

This guide covers five parts — construction, material, end use, fibre labelling and procurement — and ends with an acceptance checklist you can put straight to work.

1. Five Main Lace Constructions and Where They Differ

  • Warp-knit lace (Raschel): knitted directly on a warp machine, with good body and dimensional stability and high output — used for hems, lingerie trims and home textiles
  • Water-soluble lace (embroidered): embroidered onto a soluble backing that is later dissolved away — dimensional motifs with clean edges, standard in bridal and couture
  • Embroidered trim on mesh: embroidered on tulle or chiffon then cut into strips — rich layering for occasionwear, childrenswear and decorative panels
  • Knitted rib trim: an elastic ribbed band for necklines and cuffs that is decorative and functional at once — its shrinkage must match the fabric
  • Crochet and hand-look trim: a vintage look with clear layering, common on loungewear and childrenswear — check yarn pilling and wash resistance closely

2. Material and Stretch: Polyester, Cotton, Nylon and Spandex

The same motif in different yarns gives completely different price, hand feel, shrinkage and compliance. For export orders, confirm that the fibre content can support the label claim you want to make.

Material Characteristics Typical Use
Polyester Strong, low cost, wash-resistant, slightly firm hand Fast fashion and high-volume trims
Cotton / Cotton Blend Soft against skin with a natural hand, but shrinks and fades Childrenswear, underwear, loungewear
Nylon / Polyamide Fine, smooth, excellent elastic recovery, higher cost Premium lingerie and stretch lace
Spandex Blend (Stretch Lace) Provides recovery, usually paired with polyamide, and resists rolling next to the skin Stretch underwear and sport trims
Silk / Mulberry Silk Best lustre and hand feel, high cost, usually dry-clean only Bridal and luxury brands

3. Work Backwards from the Garment

  • Underwear and loungewear: choose stretch lace first, focusing on softness against skin and wash resistance, and avoid hard edges or coarse mesh
  • Dresses and occasionwear: focus on drape and pattern repeat — seams must align, otherwise the flounce twists after washing
  • Childrenswear: avoid snag-prone structures, hard decorations and small parts, and meet the small-parts and cord safety rules of the destination market
  • Bridal and eveningwear: water-soluble and embroidered lace dominate — always pre-shrink, and budget the hand-sewing time and waste
  • Decorative panelling: keep lace consistent with your woven labels, hang tags and packaging so the branding reads as one language, not scattered accents

4. Lace Changes What Your Care Label Must Say

  • Fibre content: where lace is a separate component above the disclosure threshold, most markets require its fibres to be listed in the care label
  • Care instructions: silk and some water-soluble lace need dry-clean or hand-wash symbols, and the symbols must match what the lace actually tolerates
  • Languages: export care labels must use the destination market's language, and EU rules can require a combination of official languages
  • Best practice: confirm lace composition and after-wash behaviour during sampling, then write the care label — so you are not reprinting labels after bulk production

5. Procurement and Sampling: Name the Variables

  • Width and weight: width drives layout and consumption, weight drives body and drape — both hit unit cost directly
  • Pattern repeat: the repeat size drives seam matching and waste, so custom work needs a repeat unit that tiles without a visible join
  • Colour fastness: check wash, rub and light — and for pale and off-white shades confirm there is no yellowing after washing
  • Shrinkage and pilling: ask for the pre-shrink process and the expected after-wash dimensional change, and confirm pilling with a standard test
  • Sampling: send fabric swatches and the target position, sample per garment position, and write the acceptance criteria and test method onto the approval sheet

6. Incoming Acceptance Checklist

  • Pattern direction and repeat are consistent, with no mismatched seams
  • Edges lie flat — no curling, no fraying, no loose threads
  • No snags, broken yarns, oil marks or colour spots
  • Colour matches the standard under a controlled light source, with no shade variation within the lot
  • After washing, dimensions, appearance and hand feel stay within the agreed range
