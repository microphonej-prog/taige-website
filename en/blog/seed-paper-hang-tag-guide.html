Seed Paper Hang Tags: Choosing Paper, Seeds and Process

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 20, 2026

Most hang tags go straight from the garment to the bin. A seed paper tag turns that last step into planting: real seeds sit inside the sheet, and once the tag is torn up and buried, herbs or wildflowers come up in two or three weeks. For a brand that talks about sustainability, it is one of the few green claims a customer can verify with their own hands. It is not just a different paper, though: process temperature, germination rate and cross-border plant health rules each have to be handled, or the tag is merely expensive paper.

This guide covers five things: how a seed paper tag is built and what grammage it needs, how to pair seeds with paper stock, which printing and finishing processes kill seeds, how to write germination and shelf-life commitments, and the plant health rules that apply when shipping to the EU, Australia and New Zealand, or the US.

1. Structure: How Paper, Seeds and Coating Stack Up

Seed paper is usually made from recycled or cotton pulp, with seeds stirred into the wet stock before the sheet forms; as it dries, fibres lock the seeds inside the sheet. The surface is rarely heavily coated, which keeps the torn edge and fibre texture. Grammage runs 250–350 gsm, heavier than a normal tag: too thin and seeds fall out at the die-cutter, too thick and it degrades slowly in soil, delaying germination. Seed density is quoted per sheet or per square inch, commonly 5–15 per tag; the denser the seeds, the weaker the sheet and the rougher the die-cut edge.

  • Stock: recycled, cotton or hemp pulp, usually natural or pale; dark shades need dyeing, and the dye must be non-toxic to seeds
  • Seeds: herbs such as basil, mint or parsley; wildflowers such as cosmos or zinnia; vegetables. Large seeds like sunflower or beans do not suit thin sheets
  • Coating: none or water-based; solvent-based coatings damage the seeds directly
  • Size and shape: standard tag formats behave best; on long strips and unusual shapes, first confirm the seeds are evenly distributed

2. Pairing Seeds With Paper: Four Workable Combinations

The pairing decides three things: how well the seeds germinate, how the tag looks, and whether it can be exported at all. The four combinations below cover most briefs; before ordering, have twenty tags of each germinated side by side — far more reliable than a sample folder.

3. Printing and Finishing: Which Processes Kill Seeds

Seeds are alive. Sustained heat above 60–70°C cuts germination sharply, so conventional foil stamping at 130–160°C, thermal lamination, strong UV varnish and laser engraving all need care, and a cold alternative is preferable wherever possible. Offset and digital printing do not raise temperature much, but inks and varnishes must be low-migration and non-toxic to seeds. Deep embossing crushes seeds, so press shallow or not at all; and use fresh die blades, because a dull rule lifts seeds off the sheet.

  • Safe: offset, digital, water-based varnish, shallow emboss, hand-threaded string
  • Handle with care: hot foil, thermal lamination, strong UV, laser cutting and engraving — always run a germination comparison first
  • Never: solvent coatings, treatments containing insecticide or biocide, hot calendering
  • Always sample: print 50–100 tags and germinate them beside unprinted sheets; a gap over fifteen points means changing the process

4. Germination and Shelf Life: Putting Numbers in the Order

Germination depends on the seed lot, the storage conditions and the process — all three. A common commitment is at least 70–80% out of the factory measured against unprinted sheets, with a permitted drop of 10–15 points after printing. Write the test method into the order at the same time: tear up 20–30 tags, soak them, cover with 3–5 mm of soil, keep moist and count germination over 7–21 days. On shelf life, sealed and dark at room temperature usually gives 12–24 months; in a hot, humid warehouse two months can halve the rate.

  • Packing: foil or heavy PE bags with desiccant, kept away from direct contact with corrugated board
  • Storage: cool, dark, away from heat, solvents and odours, stacked no more than five high
  • Customer copy: print the four planting steps on the tag — tear, soak, cover, water and light. The clearer the wording, the fewer complaints
  • Disclaimer: results vary with climate and season; state that germination depends on storage and planting conditions

5. Cross-Border Compliance: Seeds Are Quarantine Goods

This is where projects fail. Seeds are plant propagating material, and most countries impose quarantine and import permit rules. The EU requires phytosanitary certificates for certain species; Australia and New Zealand are the strictest, and mixed wildflower packets are routinely held; the US adds rules for some vegetable and grass seeds. Whether a seed-bearing paper product is regulated as seed varies by jurisdiction — the safe route is to have the customer confirm the permitted species list for the destination before shipping, buy locally sourced seed where needed, or switch to a recycled board tag in the same shade.

💡 Tip: seed paper suits domestic brands and eco-conscious premium labels in Europe and North America best. If the customer ships to Australia, New Zealand or another strict market, confirm the species before quoting — a held shipment costs far more than the tags.
