Neutral Packaging for Trims: Brand Secrecy, Carton Codes and Reconciliation

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 17, 2026

Neutral packaging is the request almost every brand client makes after the first order: no brand name on the carton, no logo on the inner bag, just a code on the box. It sounds like a change of carton, but it touches four things at once — splitting deliveries, reconciliation, grey-market leakage and confidentiality terms. Done well, the client hands you the whole season; done carelessly, one mislabelled carton can stop a garment line for half a day.

This guide covers three things: how neutral the packaging actually has to be, how to code and reconcile split deliveries, and where confidentiality really leaks.

1. Three Situations, Three Levels of Neutrality

  • OEM confidentiality: the brand ships trims straight to its own garment factory and does not want the brand or style name visible there. The highest level — not even the inner bag carries a mark.
  • Split across factories: one style divided between two or three garment factories, each seeing only its own share, so nobody learns the others' volumes or prices. Medium level — codes on the carton, factory marks allowed inside.
  • Trading layer: an intermediary does not want the end client to know who produced it. The level depends on the contract; usually only the trader's reference goes on the carton, and documents carry the trader's letterhead.

Establish which of the three applies before fixing the packaging plan. Carton, inner bag and shipping documents differ completely between them; one template forced onto all three will fail somewhere.

3. Split Deliveries: How to Number Batches and Cartons

Under neutral packaging the failure mode is not the packaging but the numbering. With no brand name to recognise, a vague code rule leaves the factory guessing.

  • Three-part codes: style + factory letter + carton sequence, for example T24-07 / A / 001. The client assigns the factory letters, so the code itself leaks nothing.
  • One size run per carton. Mixed sizes and styles save cartons but make sorting errors invisible on an unmarked box.
  • Send a factory allocation sheet with the goods: which batch to which plant, carton ranges, quantities, signed and kept by both sides. That sheet is the root of the whole reconciliation.

4. The Three Documents for Split Shipments

  • Packing list: size, quantity, gross and net weight by carton number. The neutral version swaps the brand for the client code, but drops no data.
  • Receipt sheet: the receiving plant counts by carton number and signs, noting arrival date and actual quantity. Check cartons before opening any inner bag.
  • Discrepancy sheet: shortages, wrong sizes and damage logged on the spot with photos and carton numbers. Under neutral packaging claims rest almost entirely on this sheet; later write-ups are usually refused.

5. Where Confidentiality Actually Leaks

A blank carton solves only the surface. The leaks that actually trigger a brand's complaint are in files and images: quote headers, invoices, courier waybills, the filename of the PDF sent to the factory, and layer names left inside the artwork.

💡 Practical tip: strip the brand from filenames sent to garment factories and use your own order number instead; when saving the PDF, remove hidden information and rename layers to something generic. It costs nothing and prevents most after-the-fact disputes.

6. Cost and Handling Details

  • A plain carton is usually cheaper than a printed one, but it adds a manual labelling step, so the per-carton cost lands roughly level.
  • Split shipments raise freight and the number of customs operations. A few cartons sent ad hoc can cost several times the unit rate of a full container, so price it in up front.
  • The suffocation warning and size sticker on the inner bag are not brand information and must not be dropped in the name of neutrality.
  • Neutral is not the same as misdeclaring. Customs descriptions, quantities, values and HS codes must be true; a confidentiality request never justifies understating value or renaming goods.

7. Six Checks Before You Accept the Order

  • Confirm the level: OEM confidentiality, split factories or trading layer.
  • Confirm the coding rule and factory letter table, and put both in the order notes.
  • Confirm the document templates, especially the neutral packing list and invoice header.
  • Confirm the split plan: which batch to which plant, by what mode, and who pays freight.
  • Confirm what the confidentiality clause covers: drawings, samples, photos, volumes and prices.
  • Confirm the discrepancy window: how many hours after arrival, and what happens past it.
