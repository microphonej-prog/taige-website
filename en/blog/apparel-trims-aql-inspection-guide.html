AQL for Apparel Trims: Levels, Defect Classes and How Many to Pull

By TAIGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 10, 2026

Almost every trim inspection report carries AQL 2.5 in the corner, and both sides usually read it as something it is not: the buyer hears a permitted defect rate of 2.5%, the supplier hears that up to ten defects still passes. Neither is the whole story. This guide explains what AQL allocates, works through a 5,000-piece woven label lot, and lists the three mistakes that cause most disputes.

AQL is not a permitted defect rate, it is risk allocation

AQL is defined so that a lot whose true defect rate equals the AQL has about a 95% chance of being accepted. Flip it around: a lot running at 2.5% defects will still be rejected roughly one time in twenty. AQL is therefore a target for the supplier's process capability, not a guarantee handed to the buyer. Two numbers decide the actual risk — sample size and acceptance number — and both come from lot size and inspection level.

Inspection levels: I, II, III and the special levels

Level II is the default; at the same lot size it samples roughly 1.6 times as much as level I and about half of level III. The choice is practical: would a defect in this trim stop the sewing line or trigger a recall? Then use III. Three consecutive stable lots, low unit value, easy visual 100% check? Use I. The special levels S-1 to S-4 exist for destructive tests — wash durability on a woven label, tear strength on tag board, salt spray on metal hardware — where the sample is consumed, so the sample must stay small.

Critical, major, minor: how to classify trims

  • Critical (AQL 0, one occurrence rejects the lot): nickel release above the limit on metal hardware, a broken needle inside a label pack, wrong fibre content printed on a care label, cord length non-compliant on childrenswear.
  • Major (usually AQL 1.0–2.5): colour off the approved swatch, wrong size printed, missing care symbols, print too smeared to read, woven label width off by more than 2 mm.
  • Minor (usually AQL 4.0): slight edge fray, dimensions drifting within ±1 mm, carton mark off position, inner bag fold uneven.

A 5,000-piece lot: how many to pull and when to reject

Lot size Level II code Sample AQL 2.5 accept/reject AQL 1.0 accept/reject
501–1,200 J 80 5 / 6 2 / 3
1,201–3,200 K 125 7 / 8 3 / 4
3,201–10,000 L 200 10 / 11 5 / 6

Take 5,000 woven labels at 30 mm width: the lot falls in 3,201–10,000, level II gives code L, so 200 pieces are pulled. Major defects are judged at AQL 2.5, where Ac = 10 and Re = 11 — ten or fewer passes, the eleventh rejects the lot. Critical defects never enter that table: one finding rejects the lot regardless of AQL. Minor defects run at AQL 4.0 (Ac = 14 at this sample size) but can never be traded against majors.

Which sampling standard applies where

The sampling standards share one origin. China uses GB/T 2828.1, the US and most of the world use ISO 2859-1 (ANSI/ASQ Z1.4 in US practice), Germany DIN ISO 2859-1, Japan JIS Z 9015-1, Korea KS Q ISO 2859-1, Brazil ABNT NBR 5426 (expressed as NQA), France the French version of ISO 2859-1, Spain UNE 66020. One acceptance table can be recognised across all of them, provided the contract states the three variables: level, AQL and defect classes. Compliance of the content itself — fibre percentages, care symbols — is not a sampling question at all; it sits in GB 5296.4, Regulation (EU) 1007/2011, JIS L 0001, KS K 0021 or the consumer code.

Recording, traceability and the supplier score

An inspection record has to answer three questions: which lot, which defect, how many. So it needs the lot and production date, the sample size and AQL applied, counts kept separate by defect class, and photographs of every nonconformity. Accumulating defects per 10,000 pieces by quarter gives the hardest column in a supplier scorecard; a supplier trending up for two quarters is worth intervening on earlier than a single rejected lot. If the same defect appears in three consecutive lots, ask for an 8D rather than tightening the sampling — tightening only pushes the problem into the next lot.

The three most common misuses

  • Using one AQL for every defect class. Nickel release, stray needles and childrenswear cords are critical and must be AQL 0.
  • Sampling from a single carton. Stratify across inner bags and production dates, or batch-to-batch variation disappears into the average.
  • Reading pass as zero defects. A passed lot can still hold up to Ac defects; plan rework and spare pieces on the sewing line accordingly.
Put three lines in the purchase contract: AQL, inspection level, defect classes. Most disputes are not about the count, but about which rules were agreed.
