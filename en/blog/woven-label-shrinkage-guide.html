Woven Label Shrinkage: Dimensional Stability After Washing

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 29, 2026

Shrinkage, wrinkling and drifting print on woven labels and care labels are the defects that quietly pass inspection and then surface after two washes: a 30mm neck label that was perfect at delivery measures 28mm, the print sits off-centre, the edge ripples and it pulls against the neckline seam. Shrinkage is not random error — it is a physical result of material, edge finish, post-treatment and washing conditions. This guide covers where shrinkage comes from, how the common options differ, how to measure after washing and how to convert a washed dimension into a weaving size, and it ends with clauses you can put straight into your specification.

1. Where Shrinkage Comes From: Three Mechanisms

Two labels woven from the same yarn batch can shrink at double the rate, because three different mechanisms are at work. Naming the mechanism is what makes a remedy possible: natural relaxation can be handled by resting the goods before washing, whereas thermal contraction from hot cutting can only be managed by changing the edge finish itself.

  • Relaxation of weaving tension: warp yarn is held under tension on the loom and needs time to recover after weaving. Labels cut and sewn immediately after weaving shrink more; pre-shrinking or a full conditioning rest cuts this component sharply.
  • Thermal contraction from hot cutting and heat setting: hot-cut edges, heat setting and pressed folds introduce local contraction. The closer the cutting temperature runs to the melting point of the yarn, the wider the affected zone and the more the edge ripples.
  • Wet-heat shrinkage in washing and drying: fibres rearrange under moist heat. Polyester and nylon move most when washed hot and tumble dried, while cotton and regenerated cellulose swell and then contract. Wash temperature, tumble drying and fabric softener all change the outcome.

2. How Material and Edge Finish Change the Rate

The table below gives typical ranges for common combinations. Actual figures depend on loom tension, setting process and wash conditions, so always work from measured data agreed between both parties. Note that width usually shrinks more than length, because the warp direction is affected more directly by tension.

Material and edgeTypical shrink after 30°C gentle washBehaviour and notes
Satin label, hot-cut edge1–2.5% length, 2–4% widthSmooth surface and crisp type; hot-cut edges curl after washing, so control blade temperature
Plain-weave label, hot-cut1–3% length, 2.5–4.5% widthSoft and cost-effective; shrinks slightly more than satin, well suited to care labels and inner labels
Heavy jacquard, cut and folded2–4% length, 3–5% widthRich relief but thick, and the logo can drift after washing; folded edges add local contraction on top, so allow separately
Double-layer selvedge label, no cut edge0.5–1.5% length, 1–2% widthThe most stable option with no fraying or curling, but widths are fixed and layout freedom is limited — best for large runs of standard sizes
Coated printed care label1–2% length, 2–3% widthHolds a lot of information legibly; the coating and base shrink differently, cracking at folds, so avoid hot tumble drying

3. How to Measure After Washing: Three Washes, One Datum

Shrinkage only means something when it is measured — a verbal promise of no shrinkage carries no weight. The method below is practical, and international buyers normally cite ISO 6330 (domestic washing procedures) or AATCC 135 (dimensional changes after home laundering), both of which require a measurement before and after washing and a calculated change rate.

  1. Take three full labels from the same batch, not offcuts, and keep one unwashed as the control for both size and colour comparison.
  2. Before washing, mark a fixed datum on each piece with a fade-resistant pen: for example a 50mm or 100mm line along the length and another across the width, with the ends on flat areas away from folds and holes.
  3. Run the cycle the destination market expects: normally 30°C or 40°C, standard detergent, three washes. Do line drying and tumble drying as separate runs, because the results can differ by more than one percentage point.
  4. After washing, condition the pieces at the same temperature and humidity and re-measure the same datum. Shrinkage = (before minus after) divided by before, times 100. Report length and width separately for all three samples and design to the worst case.

4. Allowance: Turning a Washed Size Into a Weaving Size

What the buyer wants is the washed dimension; what the loom produces is the weaving size. The relationship is a division, not a multiplication: weaving size = washed size divided by (1 minus shrinkage). If you need 30mm after washing and measured shrinkage is 3%, weave 30 divided by 0.97, about 30.9mm. Multiplying by 1.03 gives the same answer near 3%, but the error grows once shrinkage reaches 8%.

  • Test the fabric before fixing the label: if the garment fabric shrinks 5% and the label only 1%, sewing it on creates wrinkles. Keep the gap between label and fabric shrinkage within about two percentage points; if it is wider, switch to an applied or heat-transfer label.
  • Never hide the compensation inside the finished dimension: state both the weaving size and the washed size in the specification, with the wash conditions. With a single number, each supplier applies its own convention and the two sides inevitably disagree.
  • Allow separately for folds and holes: local contraction at a fold or a turned edge adds to the overall rate, and the position of holes and print relative to the centre has to be converted too — otherwise the washed label reads as off-centre, with the hole sitting high.

5. Neck Label and Care Label on the Same Garment: Why It Creases

A frequent complaint is that the neckline wrinkles and the care label bubbles after washing, yet nothing shows at inspection. The cause is usually two labels in different materials with different finishes, shrinking two or three percentage points apart. Sewn into the same seam, one contracts and the other does not, and the excess has nowhere to go but into a crease.

  • Choose the same material family and the same pre-shrinking process for the neck label and care label of one style, keeping the difference within about 1.5 percentage points.
  • Leave the care label a little freedom in sewing: avoid overlocking the entire edge with dense stitches, keep a 5–8mm free run in the middle, or secure it at a single point so both ends can move.
  • For care labels with a lot of information, prefer a textile base over a coated paper-like substrate — it shrinks closer to the woven label and crease complaints drop sharply.

6. Six Clauses to Write Into the Specification

ItemWhat to write
Dimension basisState weaving size and washed size separately, e.g. 31mm / 30mm ±1mm
Tolerance directionState whether it is one-sided, e.g. width may only run minus, so it cannot overflow when sewn
After-wash requirementGive the wash conditions and the shrinkage ceiling, e.g. three washes at 30°C, shrinkage 3% or less
Measurement methodCite ISO 6330 or AATCC 135, and state the drying method and how the datum is marked
Fabric matchSend a fabric swatch with its washed shrinkage and require the label to stay within two points
Inspection ruleFix the sample size, how out-of-tolerance lots are handled (concession, rework or replacement) and the retention period
