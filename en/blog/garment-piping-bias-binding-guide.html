Garment Binding and Piping Guide: Bias Cut, Width and Durability

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 5, 2026

Bias binding and piping are the trims that decide whether an edge looks right: necklines, armholes, hems, plackets, seams and inside edges all rely on them for a clean finish. They are a tiny share of the bill of materials, yet they show the level of a garment's workmanship most clearly — puckered edges, wavy stitching or a burst seam after washing drag the whole piece down.

This guide has five parts: it separates the three easily confused processes (binding, piping and facing), then compares materials and constructions, covers bias versus straight cutting and width specs, deals with stitching and durability, and sets out acceptance criteria — ending with a spec list you can paste straight into a quotation request.

1. Separate Binding, Piping and Facing

The three are often lumped together, yet their function and pricing work in completely different ways.

  • Binding — a bias-cut strip folds over and encloses the raw edge, visible on both sides; common on knit necklines, and on the plackets and hems of collarless tops.
  • Piping — a cord is wrapped in a fabric strip and sewn into the seam, leaving a fine rounded ridge; used to highlight structural lines on tailoring, outerwear and loungewear.
  • Facing — a same-colour piece cut to match the edge shape is turned to the inside and is invisible from the outside; common at necklines and armholes.
💡 Tip: pricing works differently — binding and piping cord are quoted per metre, facings per piece. Be explicit about which one you need, or quotations for the same garment can differ by twofold.

3. Bias or Straight Cut, and How to Set the Width

  • Only a 45° bias cut has the give to follow curves such as necklines and armholes; straight-cut tape suits straight edges only.
  • Common finished widths: 12 mm and 18 mm single-fold; 12 mm, 18 mm, 25 mm and 32 mm double-fold. Work backwards from the seam allowance and the visible width you want.
  • Allow 2–4 mm of loss in the strip itself so the visible width after sewing matches the design value.
  • Join strips with a 45° lap and press flat; no visible joint is acceptable on the finished garment.
  • Keep one dye lot and one batch per order where possible, so the rolls do not differ in shade.

4. Stitching and Durability: No Puckering, No Burst Edges

  • Match the shrinkage: the binding must shrink like the shell, or be pre-shrunk, otherwise the edge puckers after washing.
  • Control the tension: too much tension on the binder pulls the strip thin and leaves a wavy edge.
  • Reinforce stress points: add a visible or hidden stitch at underarms, plackets and hems to stop the edge bursting.
  • Wash test: run one wash or dry-clean cycle at the garment's care level and check for fraying, colour loss and distortion.

5. Acceptance Criteria for Ordering

  • Width and fold: ±0.5 mm tolerance on the finished width, even folding, no double edge or curled edge.
  • Composition and weight: must match the approved sample, with the poly-cotton ratio stated in writing — no verbal promises.
  • Colour fastness: washing, rubbing and perspiration must match the shell's grade, particularly on dark shades.
  • Shade: keep ΔE within 1.0 against the approved swatch and within 0.8 batch to batch, tightening further if the brand requires it.
  • Rolls and metres: label the net metres per roll, keep joins per roll within the agreed limit, and no short measure.
  • Environmental and testing: childrenswear and EU-bound orders need OEKO-TEX or an equivalent harmful-substances test report.
💡 Tip: confirm the shrinkage of the binding together with the shell fabric. Many 'the edge puckered after washing' complaints are not a stitching fault but a shrinkage mismatch. One wash test up front costs very little and can save an entire batch of rework.
