Trim Order Quantities and Unit Conversion: Yards, Metres, Dozens and Rolls

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 26, 2026

The most common mistake when ordering trims is not price, it is quantity. A run of 3,000 garments ordered with exactly 3,000 woven labels runs short the moment a label is miscut, a size is swapped or a panel is re-cut; and quoting yards to a factory that prices in metres quietly loses about 8% of what you expected. This guide explains how to calculate consumption per garment, how the common trade units convert, how much waste allowance to add, and how minimums and over or under shipment should be handled, ending with a quantity confirmation sheet you can copy straight into an enquiry.

Getting quantity wrong usually costs more than the unit price does: a top-up order means a fresh production slot, a possible shade mismatch against the first batch, and another lead time. So the first step before ordering is to put three numbers on one sheet — usage per garment, total required, waste allowance — and check them yourself before the factory sees them.

1. Why Trim Quantities Go Wrong More Often Than Prices

  • Small parts, big ratio: trims are cheap, so a few hundred short goes unnoticed — yet 50 missing labels can strand 50 finished garments, and the smaller the run, the worse the impact
  • Units are not standardised: the same item may be quoted per piece, per dozen or per roll — different bases guarantee arithmetic errors
  • Too many counting bases: hang tags by sheet, woven labels by piece, care labels by strip, poly bags by piece or by kilo, paper bags by piece — five bases on one purchase order is normal
  • Hidden cost of a top-up: woven labels need the loom set up again and hang tags need colour re-approved; a few hundred extra usually costs at least 1.5 times the original unit price

2. Unit Conversion: Align the Basis Before Discussing Price

Unit Typically used for Conversion
Yard Woven labels, tapes, roll care labels 1 yard = 0.9144 m
Metre Same items; standard in Europe and China 1 m = 1.0936 yd
Roll Woven labels, care labels, tapes Commonly 100 yd, 100 m or 200 m — the roll length must be written into the order
Dozen Woven and care labels packed in bundles 1 dozen = 12 pieces
Sheet / ream Hang tags, cards, stickers 1 ream = 500 full sheets; finished tag count depends on how many per sheet
Kilogram Bags quoted by weight Confirm grams per piece first, then convert to a piece count
The costliest confusion: yards and metres differ by 9.36%, which is a real amount on a large order. Never write something ambiguous like 1000 yd/m in the order file; write 1000 m (1093.6 yd) or put the unit on its own line, and verify roll length and label length at the sample stage.

3. From the BOM to the Order Quantity

Consumption per garment is simply how much trim one finished piece uses. The base formula is one line: order quantity = pieces to produce × usage per garment × (1 + waste rate). That looks trivial, yet the usage term is where errors hide: one care label or two per garment, one hang tag or a front and back pair.

A worked example for 3,000 pieces

Trim Usage per garment Waste reference Suggested order
Neck label (woven) 1 3% - 5% 3,100 - 3,150
Care label 2 (side seam + back neck) 3% - 5% 6,200 - 6,300
Hang tag 1 plus a spare tag 5% - 8% 3,200 - 3,300
Poly bag 1 3% - 5% 3,100 - 3,150
Paper bags (retail) Counted separately per store need 5% - 8% List as its own line by store allocation
  • By colour and size: neck labels and hang tags are usually one version per style, while care labels and bags split by size or colour must be allocated by ratio — and each colour-size still carries its own minimum
  • List spares separately: spare labels, spare tags and spare buttons belong on their own line rather than hidden inside the waste rate — they usually need separate packing and marking
  • Roll arithmetic: when woven labels ship on rolls, pieces per roll = roll length ÷ label length, so an offcut is inevitable; if you buy by piece but receive by roll, state the permitted offcut ratio in the order

4. How Much Waste Allowance: It Depends on the Category

Category Usual range Main source of loss
Hang tags, cards 5% - 8% Print overs, die-cut waste, punching and stringing damage
Woven and printed labels 3% - 5% Slitting ends, sewing rejects, roll offcuts
Care labels 3% - 5% Sewing rejects, print misregistration, roll ends
Poly bags 3% - 5% Machine set-up, first sealing pieces, transit crushing
Paper bags 5% - 8% Bonding faults, handle rework, transit dents
Buttons, metal parts 2% - 3% Plating faults, dropped pieces, needle-detection rejects
More waste is not more safety: ordering 20% extra woven labels feels prudent but turns into dead stock the moment the logo changes next season. A better routine is to take 3%-5% on the first run while archiving the artwork, colour sample and application settings, so a top-up can continue seamlessly.

5. Minimums and Multiples: What to Do Below the MOQ

A minimum order is not an arbitrary gate: it is the point where plate, die and machine set-up costs spread thin enough to make sense. A tag version needs print overs and a woven label version needs the loom threaded; that fixed cost must be absorbed by a certain quantity. Below it, unit price climbs steeply — which is why engineering the quantity matters more than haggling on price.

  • Merge versions: multilingual, multi-size or multi-colour tags that share one layout can run on a single plate, letting one version number absorb the whole quantity
  • Start with a trial run: when the result is unproven, order a trial quantity — roughly 2,000-3,000 tags, 1,000 woven labels or 300-500 care labels are common floors — then scale after approval
  • Share tooling across styles: tags in one collection that use the same die and dimensions differ only in print content, which lowers the minimum for every style
  • Order in multiples: find out the carton quantity, roll length and pieces per sheet, then round up to a multiple so no undeliverable odd count (say 37 extra) remains

6. Over and Under Shipment: How to Accept What Arrives

Trims are small parts counted in bulk, so counting every piece is impractical and the trade accepts a stated percentage of over or under shipment. Ambiguity here is what causes arguments at goods-in, so the order should fix three things: the permitted range, whether invoicing follows actual or ordered quantity, and how a short final carton is handled.

  • Agree ±3% to ±5%: standard for small trims; tighten to ±2% to ±3% for precious-metal parts, irregular die-cuts or special processes
  • State the check method: woven and care labels can be verified by weight or by counting sample bundles; tags by weighing and converting, with one or two cartons counted in full
  • Label the odd carton: keep full cartons standard and mark the actual count on the last part carton, so the warehouse does not book it as a full box
  • Time limit for shortfall claims: raise a shortage within 3 to 5 working days and agree whether it is topped up or credited; after that most factories treat the goods as accepted

7. Quantity Confirmation Sheet to Copy Into Your Enquiry

  • Pieces to produce, size and colour ratio, including spares
  • Trim usage per garment, stated per style with the exact count
  • Unit of measure and price basis: piece, dozen, roll or metre
  • Waste rate, and whether spares are inside the quantity
  • Permitted over or under range and the basis for invoicing
  • Packing and carton quantity: per carton, per roll, per bundle
  • Top-up minimum and expected lead time, usually faster than the first run
