Apparel Hangtag Barcode Guide: Choosing & Printing EAN-13, UPC and ITF-14

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 10, 2026

The barcode on a hangtag is the data interface between your brand and the retail system: receiving, shelving, checkout and stocktaking all rely on it. The usual consequence of a badly printed barcode is not a fine but a scanner that will not read it and a batch sent back for relabelling. This guide covers how to choose the right barcode type for apparel hangtags, sizing and quiet-zone rules, colour and material choices, variable-data printing and sample approval.

Start with the conclusion: a barcode is not decoration — it is a machine-readable area with strict size and contrast requirements. In design and printing it deserves higher priority than the logo or the copy. Any attempt to shrink it, recolour it or lay it over artwork for the sake of looks can make the whole batch fail at the checkout.

1. Why Hangtags Need Barcodes

  • Retail checkout: department stores and chains pull price and stock from the POS system via the barcode; if it will not scan, staff must key it in manually, slowing every transaction;
  • Warehousing and logistics: receiving, picking, packing and shipping are logged by scanning, so a poor barcode corrupts inbound and outbound data;
  • E-commerce and omnichannel: marketplace warehouses, ship-from-store and returns all need a unique, traceable item barcode;
  • Category management: brands track SKU sell-through by barcode, so inconsistent formats make the data impossible to aggregate.

    • 2. Choosing the Right Barcode Type

    Barcode typeTypical digitsBest for
    EAN-1313 digitsRetail items in the EU, Asia-Pacific and most other markets — the global default
    UPC-A12 digitsUS and Canadian retail; usually mandatory for North American stores
    ITF-1414 digitsCartons and case-level logistics; often printed on corrugated boxes, usable as a secondary code on hangtags
    Code 128VariableInternal codes, batches and management codes; dense and alphanumeric-capable

    The rule is simple: for consumer-facing hangtags, use EAN-13 or UPC-A according to the target market; for internal or case-level codes, use Code 128 or ITF-14. Two codes on one hangtag is common, but each needs its own clear, non-interfering zone.

    3. Size, Quiet Zones and Print Quality

  • Magnification: retail codes are usually scaled between 80% and 200% of nominal size; below 80% read failures rise sharply, so when space is tight, change the layout instead of shrinking the code;
  • Quiet zones: leave blank space at both ends of the symbol — typically at least 10 narrow-module widths — with no text, border, pattern or trimmed edge inside it;
  • Bar height: a code that is too short gets only partly scanned by a handheld gun; keep generous height at the till and never squash the symbol;
  • Print precision: bar and space edges must be sharp with no feathering or fill-in, and ink density even; offset, digital and thermal transfer all work — resolution and pressure control are what matter.

    • 4. Colour and Material Choices

    Barcode scanning relies on the difference in reflectance between dark and light under red or infrared light, so colour is a readability question, not an aesthetic one. The rule is dark bars on a light background: black on white is safest, while blue, green and dark brown usually work. Red, orange and gold bars have very low contrast under red light — avoid them. Glossy lamination creates glare at the scanner; matte or uncoated surfaces read better.

    On material, art paper and white card are the safest printable surfaces; on kraft, whose base is darker and textured, print a white patch under the code first. For PVC or waterproof tags, choose a matching ink so the code is not scratched or bleeds. If the tag will be washed, dry-cleaned or left in the sun, verify rub and light resistance of the barcode area at sample stage.

    5. Variable Data: One Code Per Item

    When a brand needs unique codes — anti-counterfeiting, serial numbers, batch traceability — the barcode becomes variable-data printing: every hangtag carries a different code, usually produced by digital printing or inkjet. Three things matter. First, the data source must be a verifiable spreadsheet or database, checked digit by digit, including check digits, before printing. Second, the barcode must match the serial number and QR content on the same tag. Third, for large runs confirm the imposition, numbering rules and waste replacement with the factory in advance, or errors will appear across whole sheets.

    6. Sampling and Approval Checklist

  • Ask design or the buyer for a vector barcode file (AI/EPS/PDF) — never scale down a screenshot or bitmap;
  • On receiving the sample, scan it with a real POS gun or scanner app several times, at different angles and light levels;
  • Check that the barcode value, the printed text and the back-end system all match;
  • Confirm magnification, quiet zones, bar height and colour meet target-market rules, and keep the approved sample and test record on file.
