Clothing Tagging Guns and Swift Tacks: Sizes, Fabric Safety and Breakage Control

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 15, 2026

A tagging gun and a box of swift tacks are the last thing that happens to a garment before it is packed, and the easiest line item to buy on price alone. A 25 mm tag pin through a hang tag and a neck label looks like it is only holding a card in place. Choose the wrong needle gauge and silk keeps a permanent hole; choose too short a pin and tags fall off in transit; break a needle and a fragment travels to the customer inside the carton. This guide covers pin sizes, which fabrics should never be pierced, breakage control, and what each market actually requires on a hang tag.

1. Tag pin sizes: length, gauge and pin type

Three numbers define a tag pin, and all three decide whether it works or leaves a mark. Length is the distance from the face of the hang tag to the point of entry: 25 mm suits a single card plus a thin neck label, 35 mm clears a denim waistband or a stack of cards, and 50–65 mm is for knitwear sets that carry a tag, a spare-button bag and a fibre card. Gauge is normally 0.9 mm; fine pins of 0.6–0.7 mm are for silk, chiffon and fine knits, and only tight woven fabric needs anything heavier. Type splits into standard pins, loop locks that can be removed and refitted, and locking pins with a one-way barb.

Pin type Common length Gauge Where it belongs
Standard pin 25 mm 0.9 mm One card plus neck label on woven and mid-weight knit garments
Medium pin 35 mm 0.9–1.0 mm Denim, heavy outerwear, stacked hang tags
Long pin 50–65 mm 1.0 mm Knitwear sets with tag, spare-button bag and belt
Fine pin 25 mm 0.6–0.7 mm Silk, chiffon, fine knits, lace
Loop lock 25–35 mm 0.9 mm Retail pieces that get tried on, sample room use

The usual procurement mistake is treating length as the whole specification. A fine pin needs a different gun head, and plenty of suppliers quote only per thousand at 25 mm, so the mismatch shows up on the line rather than in the quote. Put four things on the purchase order: gun model, pin type, gauge and length. Keeping one box of each pin in the sample room also means a style change does not wait for a shipment.

2. Fabric fit: ask whether the cloth can take a hole

A tag pin works by forcing a hole between yarns. Woven cloth closes behind it because the yarns spring back; knits and filament fabrics let the yarns slide aside, and the hole grows with every wash. Five materials should not see a pin at all: silk and satin, where a broken filament never recovers; fine knits and cashmere, where the stretched loop stays stretched; coated and waterproof shells, where the hole breaks coating continuity and leaks; leather and synthetics, where the hole is permanent and tears along the puncture; and high-density down fabric, where every hole becomes a leak point. Use a woven tape or cotton string through the tag and around a button or belt loop, or a loop lock gripping the edge of the neck label instead.

A one-line test: if the cloth needs an interlining before you can even sew it — silk, coated shells, fine knits — it cannot hold a pin. Fabric that needs support to take a stitch will not take a puncture.

3. Broken needles: the most expensive failure in this step

A needle tip snaps when it meets heavy layers, zip teeth or metal hardware. A fragment left inside the garment is a complaint on a good day and a recall on a bad one. Three controls: pick the right length, because forcing a pin through with the gun always raises the break rate; check every gun head for a rolled tip at the start of each shift, since a rolled tip is the warning before the break; and run every tagged garment through needle detection, keeping both the detection log and a breakage log with time, station, pin count and recovered fragments. Japanese childrenswear and innerwear buyers ask for the detection report directly; European and US buyers write it into the vendor manual.

A breakage log is the cheapest insurance you can buy: five needles broken in a week with only three fragments recovered means the shipment does not leave. That call needs arithmetic, not a standard.

4. What each market requires: a hang tag cannot replace the care label

Many brands print the legal information on the hang tag only, which fails in both the EU and the US. Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011 requires fibre composition to be attached permanently to the product, and GPSR (EU) 2023/988 requires a responsible operator inside the EU; France additionally requires mandatory labelling in French, and Spain sets the fibre labelling method in Real Decreto 928/1987. In the US, 16 CFR 423 requires care instructions attached to the product. Japan requires the fibre mix, the care symbols under JIS L 0001 and the country of origin in Japanese; Korea requires Korean marking and KC certification information. A hang tag carries the barcode, the price, the size and the brand story — not the only copy of a legal duty.

Market Must be attached to the product The hang tag can carry Most common mistake
European Union Fibre composition under (EU) 1007/2011, EU responsible operator under GPSR Barcode, price, multilingual product copy Composition printed on the tag only, care label left blank
United States Care instructions under 16 CFR 423, country of origin Price, size, brand story Symbols with no wording at all
Japan Fibre mix, care symbols under JIS L 0001 and origin, in Japanese Barcode, size in 号 or cm, material highlights English composition with no Japanese label
South Korea Fibre mix, care marking and KC certification information in Korean Barcode, price, size by 호수 Missing KC number, or marking in the wrong language

5. Buying: pins by the thousand, guns by the piece

Pins are consumables priced by the thousand; the gun is a tool priced per piece, with spare heads billed separately. Consumption is easy to estimate: one pin per garment plus 3% loss, so ten thousand garments is 10,300 pins. Price moves with gauge and length, and fine and long pins cost more than standard ones. Buy full cartons (commonly 5,000–10,000 pins) and ship by sea to spread the freight; on an urgent order air freight is the only option, and since pins are low-value, light goods the freight often exceeds the goods value — say so before the order, not as a surprise invoice before dispatch.

6. Pre-production checklist

  • Gun model, pin type, gauge and length written on both the purchase order and the spec sheet
  • Silk, chiffon, cashmere, coated and leather styles moved to a hole-free method, with the reason noted on the spec
  • Gun heads checked for rolled tips each shift, with a breakage log for time, station, count and fragments
  • Every garment through needle detection, report shipped with the carton or archived for at least 12 months
  • Hang tag and care label roles written down: legal information attached to the product, the tag only supplements
  • Each market language complete on its own — French, Spanish, Japanese, Korean — never English standing in
