Hang Tag Ink Safety: Migration, Odour and EN 71-3 Compliance

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 22, 2026

Ink safety is the most overlooked part of hang tag packaging: buyers audit the paper and the finishing, but rarely ask about the ink system. Pigment-bound heavy metals, azo colorants, plasticisers and residual solvents can nonetheless migrate onto garments by contact, stacking or odour — and become the weakest link in a compliance audit.

This guide covers six parts — ink types, migration routes, regulations and testing, low-migration options, odour and lamination details, and the information to send before sampling — so ink safety becomes a few tickable lines on your inquiry sheet.

1. Choosing Among Four Common Ink Systems

Ink system Main traits Best fit Watch out for
Vegetable-based offset ink Low odour, renewable content, stable colour Volume offset runs on standard paper tags Slower drying, slightly lower saturation than solvent ink
UV ink Instant cure, abrasion resistant, supports gloss and thick film Dark solid areas and tags needing rub resistance Under-cured film can leave photoinitiators and persistent odour
Water-based ink Low VOC and odour, closer to eco-packaging requirements Paper bags, eco tag ranges and inserts Weaker water resistance — confirm the surface treatment
Solvent-based ink Strong adhesion, vivid colour, works on non-absorbent substrates Synthetic paper, PVC tags, coated substrates Residual solvent odour and restricted substances — request test reports

2. How Migration Happens: Four Routes

  • Direct contact: tags resting against garments for weeks, especially in a warm warehouse, transfer ink components onto the fabric
  • Set-off in stacks: bundled before full drying, the front ink transfers to the back of the next tag and later to garments
  • Odour uptake: solvent vapour trapped in sealed poly bags is absorbed by the fabric, worst with dark solvent ink plus lamination
  • Mouthing: children's wear tags may be mouthed, which shifts the focus from skin contact to migrated element limits

Of the four, set-off and sealed-odour are the two a factory can control most easily and the two that generate the most complaints; contact and mouthing need material choice and testing as backstops.

3. Regulations and Tests Worth Knowing

  • EU REACH Annex XVII: restricts azo colorants releasing certain aromatic amines in textile and leather articles and sets limits on cadmium and lead; tags ship as accessories and buyers usually require them to comply as well
  • EN 71-3: the migration of certain elements test from toy safety, frequently applied by children's wear brands to trims and packaging — it measures heavy metals released under simulated mouthing
  • US CPSIA and California Prop 65: the first governs lead and phthalates in children's products, the second drives exposure warnings — dark inks and plastic parts are frequent triggers
  • Buyer RSL: a brand's restricted substances list is often stricter than the law — ask for it at inquiry stage, which costs far less than reworking later
  • System certifications: printing chemicals can follow a route such as OEKO-TEX ECO PASSPORT, while paper trims add FSC and recycled-content claims that pair with the ink declaration

4. Building a Low-Migration Setup

  • Start with ink systems using heavy-metal-free pigments, and ask for the pigment list plus test reports rather than a bare claim of eco ink
  • Reduce large dark solids: shift to light backgrounds, white space or spot colour and both migration and odour drop
  • Avoid systems containing phthalate plasticisers, above all PVC tags and plastic parts — this is the group buyers screen hardest
  • Weigh the finishing: lamination or varnish forms a barrier layer, yet the adhesive itself needs an odour and migration check
  • Test at sample stage rather than after bulk production, then freeze the passing specification so every later run uses the same formulation

5. Odour and Lamination: Three Details Easily Missed

  • Solvent odour is amplified inside sealed poly bags — leaving time to air out before shipping beats fixing it later
  • Under-cured UV ink brings odour and set-off together; controlling curing energy works better than laying down more ink
  • With recycled paper plus dark ink the perceived odour is stronger; if unboxing smell matters to the channel, move dark ink onto coated stock

6. Six Things to Tell Your Supplier Before Sampling

  • Target market and sales channel, and whether the item is children's wear — this decides which limits apply
  • Which standard governs testing — EN 71-3, CPSIA or your own RSL — and who submits the samples to the lab
  • Any preference or restriction on paper and ink system, for example whether solvent ink is acceptable
  • Printed area and colour distribution — whether large solids and dark tones are involved
  • Finishing list: lamination, varnish, foiling, spot UV, and whether each one touches the garment
  • Packing method and documents: whether bags are sealed, and which test reports or declarations you need
Limits, test scopes and applicability follow the current law of the buyer's market and the laboratory reports; this article is practical guidance, not legal advice.
