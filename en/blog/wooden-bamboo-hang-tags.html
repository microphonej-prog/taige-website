Wooden and Bamboo Hang Tags: Material, Thickness and Engraving

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 8, 2026

Wooden and bamboo hang tags have spread fast in designer labels, denim and workwear, fragrance and gift categories. Not because they are inherently more premium than paper, but because the material itself carries information: the grain of a solid wood blank, the straight figure of a bamboo strip, the scorch left by a laser all speak for the brand. Wood and bamboo are not paper, however. Thickness is measured in millimetres, moisture content decides whether a tag cracks, grain direction decides whether it cups, and engraving depth exposes the level of the workshop. Copying paper hang tag parameters onto wood is the most common way this category goes wrong.

1. Separate the Five Wood and Bamboo Substrates

Substrate Usual thickness Character Watch out for
Birch plywood 1.5–3 mm Even face, easy to machine, controllable cost; suits bulk runs and die-cut shapes Delamination and exposed glue lines at the edge
Solid wood blank 2–4 mm One-of-a-kind grain and the best hand feel; walnut and maple are the common picks Wide colour variation, prone to cracking, limited yield
Bamboo strip and reconstituted bamboo 2–5 mm Clean straight figure, high hardness, a strong sustainability story; bamboo fibre paper is a different product Sugars and mould risk; carbonisation treatment needed
MDF with wood-effect face 2–4 mm Cheapest and flattest surface; good for complex artwork in bulk Swells with moisture; it is a paper tag with a veneer

2. Thickness, Moisture Content and Grain Direction

Paper tags are quoted by grammage, wood and bamboo tags by millimetre. The two are not interchangeable. What decides whether problems appear after delivery are the three parameters below; all of them belong in the quotation request, not in a discussion after the goods arrive.

  • Thickness and tolerance: 2, 3 and 4 mm are the usual steps, with a commercial tolerance of about ±0.2–0.3 mm; engraving, cut-outs and sanding reduce it further, so state whether the tolerance applies to the blank or the finished tag.
  • Moisture content: 8%–12% at despatch is the safe band. A board that leaves the factory too wet keeps losing moisture in a sea container, a dry warehouse or an air-conditioned room, and then cups, cracks and sheds its coating. Ask for drying and conditioning time, and for a measured moisture figure where it matters.
  • Grain direction: wood is strong along the grain and fragile across it. Drill the hole perpendicular to the grain, keep at least 3–5 mm from hole edge to tag edge and 8 mm from hole to the end of the tag, otherwise the cord tears straight through the hole under load.
  • Face versus back: the reverse of wood and bamboo usually carries knots or glue marks. Before ordering double-sided engraving or printing, confirm that the back face is acceptable rather than assuming both sides are alike.
💡 Tip: the grain, hand feel and engraving depth of a wood or bamboo tag cannot be confirmed from a photo or a PDF. Insist on a physical sample or a same-batch blank before bulk; a picture confirms layout, not depth or touch.

3. Engraving and Printing: Choosing the Marking Method

Four marking methods dominate wood and bamboo tags: laser engraving, laser-cut apertures, screen printing and foil stamping, and UV printing. They are not competitors; each answers a different question, whether the priority is texture or colour, a single tone or near-photographic artwork.

  • Laser engraving (CO₂): the mainstream route. Power and speed are combined to burn a recess, usually 0.2–0.5 mm deep. Nothing can rub off and there is no consumable to fade; the trade-off is smoke staining and charring on pale timber, plus fine dust that must be cleaned before packing.
  • Laser cutting and apertures: nothing is faster or more accurate for shaped outlines and cut-outs, but the kerf carries a charred layer. Pale stock must be sanded or oiled, or the customer's fingers come away black.
  • Screen printing and foil: screen printing suits large flat colour areas and light base coats, with adhesion depending on sanding and a wood-specific ink. Gold and silver foil hold reasonably on a sanded face but skip and flake on coarse grain, so do not make foil the main graphic.
  • UV printing: colour, gradients and near-photographic artwork, and the first choice for small runs in colour. It wears less well and is sensitive to hand oils, with edges chipping white, so add a protective varnish or use it as an accent, and agree whether an abrasion test is required.

4. Edge, Surface and Protection Against Moisture

  • Chamfer and sand: an edge rounded to R0.5–1 mm neither cuts the hand nor chips easily. Square laser-cut edges must be treated, all the more so for childrenswear and next-to-skin categories.
  • Three surface options: wood wax oil (natural matt, best hand feel, but the brand must accept occasional re-oiling), varnish or nitrocellulose (a real film with good water and soil resistance, gloss adjustable) and UV topcoat (fast in bulk and abrasion resistant, though it feels plasticky).
  • Be realistic about water: wood and bamboo are not waterproof and will distort and mould in prolonged damp. Demanding 24 hours in water with no distortion is an over-promise; the practical route is a sealed surface with adequate thickness, or switching to PVC, metal or a paper-plastic composite.
  • Odour and cleaning: new wood tags can smell of glue, acid or smoke. For next-to-skin and baby categories, air them before packing. Engraving dust must be brushed or blown out; never pack a tag still covered in powder.

5. Holes, Hardware and Cords

Item Usual spec Recommendation
Hole 3–5 mm diameter 3–5 mm from the edge, 8 mm or more to the tag end
Eyelet and metal fitting Brass, 304 stainless Protects the hole from wear; watch EU nickel release and salt spray
Cord Cotton, waxed or hemp cord, 1.5–2 mm Pairs best with wood and bamboo; avoid over-thin nylon that cuts the hole
Safety pin and swivel hook Metal swivel hook, plastic clip Heavy tags need a swivel hook; a cord alone will distort under weight

6. MOQ, Cost Structure and Lead Time

  • MOQ runs higher than on paper: board purchasing and material waste push wood and bamboo to a typical 1000–3000 pieces. Engraved samples can be small, but composite or shaped constructions are hard to run below 100 pieces.
  • Cost structure: material plus laser time, surface finishing, hardware, cord, hand sorting and packing. Unit price depends strongly on size, engraved area, number of colours and surface treatment, so a specification-based quote is far more reliable than asking for a price per wooden tag up front.
  • Sampling and lead time: laser samples usually take 3–7 days and need vector artwork (AI, EPS or SVG) plus a note on engraving depth and whether the grain should remain visible. Bulk normally runs 10–25 days, longer in peak season or for laminated and metal-inlay constructions.

7. Acceptance Criteria and Quotation Checklist

  • Must-check list: moisture figure, measured thickness, warping when laid flat (no more than 1 mm by eye), colour variation within the batch, consistency of engraving depth, level of smoke staining and charring, hole diameter and edge distance, and surface scratches or dust.
  • For a quote: substrate and thickness, finished size and shape, surface method (engraving, screen, foil, UV), vector artwork, quantity, cord and hardware requirements, target-market compliance (EU nickel release, REACH) and whether individual packing or a retail header card is needed.
  • Bundling advice: wood tags are usually ordered with woven labels, care labels, stickers and packaging bags. Confirming colours and weights as one set avoids later rework, and if paper hang tags are also in the season, align the colour references across both.
