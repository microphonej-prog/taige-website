Garment Label Configuration: How Many Labels a Garment Needs and Where They Go

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 17, 2026

Exactly how many labels a finished garment should carry is the question most often glossed over before a trims order goes out. The brand sends a design file, the factory sews labels the way it always has, and only at retail does anyone notice: the fibre label is missing a language, the care label is stitched under the neck label and cannot be flipped out, the size label is a different shade from the neck label, and there is no country of origin at all. Reworking that costs far more than settling the configuration up front.

This guide sets it out in six steps: what each of the four label families does, which ones the law requires, where they are sewn and in what order, how six common garment types differ, the seven items most often missed, and the spec sheet that lets you order everything in one batch.

1. Four Label Families, Four Jobs

Labels are not one product. Split them by function and the configuration sheet stops leaking: regulatory labels answer what it is, how to wash it and where it was made; identification labels answer whose goods and which size; information labels answer which batch and which order; retail labels answer at what price.

FamilyTypical labelsThe question it answers
RegulatoryFibre, care and origin labelsFibre content, care, country of origin
IdentificationNeck label, size labelBrand recognition and size sorting
InformationStyle, batch and barcode labelsTraceability, stock and returns
RetailHang tag, price ticket, security tagPricing, promotion, anti-counterfeiting

2. What the Law Requires and What Is Your Choice

Only the regulatory family has a hard floor; the rest is the brand's call. But regulation is not one standard: the same garment sold into different markets needs separately prepared label content.

  • United States: the TFPIA requires fibre content, country of origin and the business identity; the FTC Care Labeling Rule requires workable care instructions in English.
  • European Union: Regulation (EU) 1007/2011 requires fibre composition in the language of the member state of sale; care symbols follow ISO 3758, voluntary in most countries but with national rules in France, Belgium and others.
  • China: GB 5296.4 requires fibre content, size designation, care method, origin and the producer's name and address.
  • Japan: the Household Goods Quality Labeling Act requires composition, care method and the labeler's name, plus country of origin for imports.

For one style sold into three markets, the cheapest route is a single multilingual care label rather than three separate batches. The precondition is listing each market's languages and mandatory fields before ordering; reprinting later almost never makes the ship date.

3. Where Each Label Goes and in What Order

Get the position wrong and correct content is wasted: a care label sewn permanently under the neck label cannot be flipped out, and a size label inside the waistband is invisible when someone tries the garment on.

LabelUsual positionSewing points
Neck labelInside centre back neck, side seam or hemSingle-needle or twin-needle overlock; the centre-back label must fold out, with no stiff edge against skin
Size labelSewn in with the neck label, below or beside itSame batch and dye lot as the neck label; size facing the same way on every piece
Fibre labelMerged with the care label into one stripLanguage per market; blend tolerance typically ±3%
Care labelWearer's left side seam, or folded into the hemOne-edge stitch flips out for reading; four-edge lasts longer but needs shrinkage allowance
Origin labelMerged into the neck or care labelThe US requires a permanent attachment, not only on a removable hang tag
Hang tagNeck, armhole or waistbandRemovable, so it never substitutes for permanently attached legal information

4. How Six Garment Types Differ

  • T-shirts and hoodies: three to four (neck, size, care, hang tag). Neck openings stretch, so choose a soft label or heat transfer.
  • Shirts: four to five (neck, size, combined fibre and care, origin, hang tag). Dress shirts often add a collar or pocket label.
  • Trousers: four (neck or waist label, size, care, hang tag). The size label inside the waistband must resist abrasion; care labels usually go in the side seam.
  • Knitwear and sweaters: four (neck, size, care, hang tag). The neck label must be soft with no stiff edge; itching is one of the main return drivers in knits.
  • Underwear and bras: three to four (tagless brand mark, size, care). Use heat transfer or printed labels at seams to cut friction.
  • Childrenswear: four to five (neck, size, care, origin, tracking label). US CPSIA requires a permanent tracking label with batch and producer details.

5. The Seven Items Most Often Missed

  1. Care label languages do not match the market — English only in a Spanish-speaking market.
  2. Neck and size labels made as two separate batches, so shade and weave drift apart.
  3. Fibre percentages rounded past the ±3% tolerance.
  4. Origin label says Made in China but omits the producer or importer details.
  5. Care label stitched on all four edges, so the inner page cannot be read.
  6. Hang tag contradicts the care label — 100% cotton on the tag, 95% on the label.
  7. Childrenswear missing the tracking label, or the tracking data not matching the actual batch.

6. The Spec Sheet That Orders It All at Once

Compress the sections above into one sheet and send it in a single go: neck labels, size labels, care labels and hang tags can then be produced and colour-matched as one batch. Six rows are the minimum — garment type and style, the label list per piece, size and material per label, languages and mandatory content, sewing position and method, quantity per piece and total.

💡 Practical tip: keep neck and size labels in the same batch and dye lot — splitting them is the most common cause of shade mismatch. Sample the care label and the hang tag in sequence instead: lock the care-label wording first, then approve the tag layout. Sampling takes 3–7 days and is far cheaper than reworking bulk.
