Shed From Labels, Elastic and Thread: What Can Actually Be Measured

By TAGE Packaging · Updated November 28, 2026

A polyester woven label is about 0.16 g of yarn; a care label holds less. On a 200 g cotton tee the trims add up to well under half a percent of the garment's mass, which is exactly why the microfibre discussion skipped them for years. That is changing, and not because the chemistry changed: France has required microfibre filters on new domestic washing machines sold there since 1 January 2025, brands have moved shedding questions into supplier questionnaires, and a label is the one component whose specification can still be changed late in the season without touching the garment.

This article does three things: it puts the mass in perspective, explains what the two parts of ISO 4484 measure and where they stop, and gives a specification and evidence list a supplier can sign today.

1. Two questions that get mixed up

“Does this label shed microplastics” hides two questions. The first is mass: how many grams of fibre leave the garment in one wash. The second is fate: whether those fibres settle in sludge, stop at a filter or reach a river. A supplier can answer only the first, and only for the component it makes. Any sentence that implies the second — “this label does not pollute water” — is a claim no supply chain can substantiate.

2. Run the mass first: trims sit under one percent

Take a common women's tee: 180 g/m² single jersey, 1.1 m² of fabric, about 200 g. Add a 40 × 20 mm main label at roughly 200 g/m² — 0.16 g; a 30 × 60 mm care label in coated nylon tape at about 60 g/m² — 0.11 g; and 1.5 m of 30 tex sewing thread — about 0.05 g. Total trim mass: under 0.4 g, roughly 0.2 percent of the garment.

Put another way: even if the trims shed ten times as much fibre per gram as the body fabric, the garment total would move by a fraction of a percent. So when a brand asks a woven label supplier for a shedding figure, the honest answer is that the number will be small, that the test is a proxy, and that the decision worth making is about wash durability and edge finish, not grams. That is not a deflection: fixing a fraying edge removes more fibre than testing for a 0.1 percent accounting line.

3. What the two parts of ISO 4484 actually cover

ISO 4484-1:2023, Textiles and textile products — Microplastics from textile sources — Part 1: Determination of material loss from fabrics during washing, washes a specimen under controlled conditions, filters the wash liquor and weighs what it catches; results are reported in mg per kg of fabric or in g per m². ISO 4484-2:2023 covers the analytical side: how collected particles are characterised and counted, and how fibre fragments are separated from other material. Together the two answer “how much, and what” — not “where it went”.

Two limits matter for trims. First, both standards are written for fabric: there is no ISO method for a sewn-on label as an assembly, so a laboratory can only run the label fabric as the specimen, and the result describes the fabric rather than the finished edge. Second, the cut edge — fused, ultrasonically sealed or bare — is not part of specimen preparation unless you ask for it, and the cut edge is exactly where a woven label loses most of its fibre.

If a laboratory offers “microplastic testing for labels”, ask three things: which standard, what specimen size, and whether the cut edge is included. A report that answers all three is usable. One that answers none is a logo.

4. The one variable a supplier really controls: the cut

A woven label is woven as a tape and then cut into single pieces, and how it is cut decides how many loose filaments sit on the finished edge. The difference between the four common methods is large enough to feel with a fingertip: same fabric, same basis weight, a different cut, and after ten washes the fraying sits a whole grade apart.

Cut method Edge condition Fraying risk after washing Where it fits and what it costs
Cold cut (die or guillotine) clean line, loose filaments exposed highest cotton and paper-based labels; cheapest, not advised for synthetic woven labels
Hot-knife sealing melt-sealed, hard bead low, but the bead can scratch skin polyester and nylon satin; medium speed, temperature control critical
Ultrasonic sealing fused and soft, no bead lowest the default for synthetic woven labels; needs a tool, slightly higher unit price, lower throughput
Laser cutting sintered edge with charring and slight yellowing low, but charring shows badly on dark grounds small runs, shaped labels and perforations; highest unit cost

Yarn choice matters as much as the cut. A staple-spun yarn — short fibres twisted into a thread — keeps releasing short fibres from a bare edge; a filament yarn does not, because there are no short fibres to release. At the same basis weight, a satin or twill label in filament polyester sheds far less from the cut than a soft brushed spun label — and the softness that makes a label feel expensive is precisely what makes it fray.

5. An evidence ladder you can put in a spec sheet

  • Level 1 | Material declaration on supplier letterhead: fibre type, yarn type (filament or spun), basis weight in g/m², cut method and width tolerance.
  • Level 2 | Wash-durability data: 5, 10 and 20 cycles following the ISO 6330 domestic washing procedure, recording fuzzing, loose ends and edge splitting, with before-and-after photos and post-wash dimensions.
  • Level 3 | Proxy test report: ISO 4484-1 run on the label fabric, with specimen size, basis weight and whether the cut edge was included. When you quote it, say “label fabric”, not “finished label”.
  • Level 4 | Third-party certification: none exists for trims today. If an organisation claims one, ask for the standard number, the certification scope and the laboratory name — what cannot name all three is marketing collateral.

In practice the first two rungs are enough to answer a brand questionnaire: they are cheap, a supplier can sign them today, and they are the only evidence that answers the question actually being asked — whether this batch will fray. Level 3 is for when a brand's legal team insists on third-party paperwork, and Level 4 cannot currently be bought in a genuine form.

6. What the local rules actually require

No market has written a microplastic rule for trims. What exists is adjacent: the EU GPSR (EU) 2023/988 requires a traceable responsible operator and contact details; France's AGEC law and the Refashion textile EPR scheme require declarations and the Triman mark; Spain's Ley 7/2022 and Real Decreto 1055/2022 govern packaging waste and producer responsibility. None of them authorises a “zero microplastic” line on a label — EU green claims rules demand verifiable, scoped wording instead.

Asia takes a different route. Japan's Household Goods Quality Labelling Act requires fibre composition, care labelling and country of origin, with JIS L 1096 supplying the physical test methods. Korea governs packaging and recycling through its resource recycling act, with fibre composition and care marking following the KS K 0021 care-symbol system. China's GB 18401-2010 sets basic safety requirements for formaldehyde and pH, and GB/T 24067-2024 the rules for quantifying a product carbon footprint — neither touches microplastics, so “zero microplastic” is equally unsupported in that market.

7. Cost and minimum order

The cut is a tooling and process choice rather than a material change, so it usually leaves the minimum alone: our woven labels start at 3,000–5,000 pieces per style depending on size and colour count. Ultrasonic sealing needs a tool, and the sample fee is credited against bulk, but wash validation has to be repeated after a cut change — that is the hidden cost most often underestimated. Third-party testing is priced per specimen and method and usually costs more than the tool itself, so settle which evidence level the brand actually needs before commissioning anything.
