Button and Buttonhole: Where 0.6 mm Decides Whether It Closes

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 22, 2026

Buttons and buttonholes are the least discussed pair of components on a garment and the fastest way to fail an inspection table. The button is a standard part, sized in ligne, abbreviated L. The buttonhole is fabric, cut and stitched to a dimension that only a formula and a trial seam can control. Buy the wrong button size and a mechanic can enlarge the hole; cut the hole one millimetre short and the whole run will not close. Which is why the order matters: fix the buttonhole formula first, then choose the button.

1. What a Ligne Is: One Unit of 0.635 mm

Ligne comes from the old button trade: 1 L equals 0.635 mm, which is 1/40 inch. The trade quotes L rather than diameter because the old gauges were graded in lignes. Convert by multiplying: 18 L is 11.4 mm, 20 L is 12.7 mm, 24 L is 15.2 mm, 30 L is 19.1 mm, 36 L is 22.9 mm and 40 L is 25.4 mm. These are nominal outside diameters, and real parts run to plus or minus 0.2 mm; the same lot in different colours can differ by 0.3 mm. Measure the actual part with calipers before you cut a buttonhole, never the nominal number alone.

2. Match the Size to Fabric and Placket

Position Usual size Selection logic
Shirt front placket 18 L (11.4 mm) Placket 30 to 35 mm wide, button about one third of that
Cuff 16 L (10.2 mm) One fabric layer only, so go one size down to avoid a hard lump
Jacket or blazer 20 to 24 L (12.7 to 15.2 mm) Proportional to lapel width, spacing 8 to 10 cm
Coat or trench 30 to 40 L (19 to 25 mm) Thick fabric needs a shank button to absorb the cloth thickness
Knit cardigan 18 to 20 L, flat back Flat back with a stay tape so the button does not sink into the knit

3. The Buttonhole Formula: Diameter Plus Thickness Plus 2 mm

Buttonhole length equals the measured button diameter plus the button thickness plus 2 mm on woven fabric; go to 3 mm on heavy cloth or on garments that are opened often. That 2 mm covers the stitch bead and fabric recovery, and more is not better, because too much clearance lets the button slide out by itself. A horizontal hole, cut across the placket direction, should sit with its centre 2 to 3 mm on the overlap side of the centre line so the button does not press on the placket edge once closed; a vertical hole sits on the centre line. Button spacing is normally the hole length plus 6 to 8 mm.

One formula worth memorising: an 18 L button at 11.4 mm plus 3 mm thickness plus 2 mm gives a 16.4 mm buttonhole. Short and it will not close; long and it opens itself.

4. Knits and Childrenswear: Two High-Risk Cases

Buttonholes in stretch fabric have two enemies: stretch and edge chewing. The inside of the placket needs a 30 to 40 g/m² fusible or a 10 mm stay tape, otherwise every closure stretches the hole a little and within a month it is an oval. Stitch the buttonhole at 12 to 14 stitches per centimetre with a 1.5 to 2 mm bartack at both ends; without the bartacks the ends split. On childrenswear the issue is small parts: buttons under 21 mm in diameter must pass a pull test, and the European children s clothing guidance CEN/TR 16792 works to 6 kg or 90 N, applied to every accessible small part on the same garment.

5. Compliance and Labelling: Nickel, Lead, Fibre

Metal buttons are a high-risk item for nickel release. REACH (EC) 1907/2006, Annex XVII, entry 27 requires metal parts in prolonged direct skin contact to release no more than 0.5 micrograms per square centimetre per week, tested to EN 1811, with EN 12472 wear simulation first on coated parts, so a lacquer over plating is not an exemption. In the United States, CPSIA caps total lead in accessible substrates of children s products at 100 ppm, and phthalate limits apply to items mainly for children under twelve. In the EU, the General Product Safety Regulation (EU) 2023/988 additionally requires a responsible economic operator and traceability information for every product, which for a small part like a button means a documented link to the supplier lot in the technical file.

Fibre labelling goes back to the regulation itself: under (EU) No 1007/2011 the percentages on the label count textile fibres only, so metal, urea resin or corozo buttons are non-textile components and are not listed, but the care symbols under EN ISO 3758 and the other mandatory information still have to state any restriction on use. Where buttons are of animal origin, such as horn or mother of pearl, the EU also requires non-textile parts of animal origin to be labelled. France goes further with a trial environmental display under the Code de la consommation, for which buyers ask suppliers directly for material and weight data.

6. Five Numbers for the Purchase Order

Item How to write it Verification
Size 18 L, measured outside diameter 11.4 mm plus or minus 0.2 Twenty pieces per lot with a micrometer
Thickness 3.0 mm plus or minus 0.2, giving a 16.4 mm buttonhole Confirm together with the trial buttonhole
Pull strength Button attachment 90 N or more, same for childrenswear Five pieces per colour on a tensile tester, record the peak
Nickel release 0.5 micrograms per square centimetre per week, EN 1811 after EN 12472 Third-party report, once per colour and plating lot
Packing and marking 100 per bag, bag marked with size, colour and lot Check three bags against the contents

Put those five numbers and the buttonhole formula into the same tech pack and the sample stage already proves the closure closes, holds and stays closed after washing. A button costs very little, but a buttonhole is cut into fabric, and a wrong one means replacing the whole placket. That is why two extra lines of dimension on a purchase order beat an argument at the inspection table.
