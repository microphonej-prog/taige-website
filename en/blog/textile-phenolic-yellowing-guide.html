Phenolic Yellowing in Apparel Trims: BHT, Packaging and Storage

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 25, 2026

White or pale trims — woven labels, care labels, cotton cord, paper tags — turn yellow after a few weeks in the warehouse, and it is usually neither dirt nor mould: it is phenolic yellowing. The antioxidant BHT in the packaging film migrates onto the textile and reacts with nitrogen oxides in the air to form yellow nitro compounds. The trouble is that the fabric was never washed, the garment was never worn, and the customer rejects it anyway. This guide explains the mechanism, the materials that are most vulnerable, the packaging and storage points where it can be stopped, and how to test and accept.

1. How Phenolic Yellowing Happens

Phenolic yellowing needs three things at once: a phenolic antioxidant on the material (classically BHT in polyethylene packaging film), nitrogen oxides in the environment (engine exhaust, gas heating, welding, some industrial and warehouse settings), and enough time plus humidity and heat. Remove any one and it does not happen. Control is therefore never a single measure: manage packaging material, storage environment and stock age together.

2. Which Materials Are Most Vulnerable

  • Nylon and elastane: the most sensitive group — white nylon care labels, elastic tape and bindings can show yellowing within weeks
  • White and pale materials: off-white, pale pink and pale blue carry the same risk; dark materials hide it, but pale piping and white stitching give it away
  • Optically brightened whites: white fabric treated with fluorescent whitening agents shows the yellowing contrast more starkly and fails inspection faster
  • High-risk packaging: recycled-content bags, PE film with antioxidants, rubber bands and some carton liners and adhesives are common BHT carriers
  • Tell it apart from other yellowing: mould shows filaments and smell, oil yellowing clusters where grease touched, perspiration yellowing sits at underarms and collars — phenolic yellowing is an even, all-over cast with no pattern

3. Choosing Packaging Material: The Most Effective Barrier

  • Specify BHT-free film: state in the purchase order that the antioxidant must not be BHT, and ask for a written declaration plus batch-linked test data
  • Be wary of recycled content: recycled PE has mixed provenance and an uncontrollable antioxidant package; for white and pale trims use virgin resin or paper instead
  • Paper instead of plastic: tissue paper, glassine and paper interleaves contain no BHT and are the safe choice for highly sensitive whites
  • Avoid mixed packaging: rubber bands, reused cartons and unknown liners can all introduce phenolics; give whites their own clean packaging

4. Intercepting It in Storage and Transit

  • Stay away from NOx sources: diesel forklifts, boiler rooms, welding bays, copier rooms and food-curing or fertiliser storage areas are no place for white trims
  • Control temperature and humidity: aim for 45–60 % relative humidity and 15–30 °C; heat and humidity accelerate yellowing and add mould risk on top
  • Shorten stock age: phenolic yellowing grows with time, so schedule whites to order and apply FIFO — do not pre-produce bulk just to spread plate costs
  • Mind the sea container: long voyages mean heat and humidity inside the box, so add desiccant and a neutral paper liner for long routes and unpack as soon as the goods land

5. Testing and Acceptance

  • Use the phenolic yellowing test: ISO 105-X18 is the industry method — the specimen is sandwiched with phenolic material and exposed to nitrogen oxides, modelling exactly the packaging plus storage combination
  • Test with the packaging: testing the textile alone proves nothing, because the variable is the packaging — include the actual bag and carton materials in the test
  • Grading and limits: read against the grey scale; white and pale styles are commonly held to grade 4 or better, but brands differ in both limit and conditions, so read the customer standard before setting your own
  • Sampling and retained samples: test finished goods from each lot together with their packaging, and keep one unopened sample cool and dark so any later dispute can be traced

6. What to Do When It Has Already Yellowed

  • Do not count on washing it out: the compounds usually form inside the fibre, so washing lightens it slightly at best; heavy yellowing should be written off as non-reworkable
  • Locate the source first: split the same lot by packaging group — BHT-free bag, ordinary bag, paper — and test each; you can pin down the culprit within a day
  • Re-test after corrective action: once packaging is BHT-free and storage improved, run the phenolic yellowing test again and issue a report — far easier for a customer to accept than a promise
  • Responsibility and communication: who supplied the bag and under what conditions the tests were run decide liability; lead with test data and a correction plan rather than an argument
