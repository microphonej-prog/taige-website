Self-Adhesive Woven Labels: Adhesives, Release Paper and Bonding

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 13, 2026

A self-adhesive woven label — also called an adhesive-backed or sew-free woven label — is a woven label coated with adhesive on the reverse and covered with release paper. Peel the liner, press it on or heat seal it, and no sewing is required, which makes it the least labour-intensive labelling option for caps, bags, footwear and seamless garment projects.

But self-adhesive is not a single glue: it covers three adhesive systems with very different behaviour. Choose the wrong one and the usual outcomes are edges lifting after two washes, or a label you simply cannot remove. Below we cover adhesive types, release paper, bonding methods and sampling.

1. Three Adhesive Systems: Heat Seal, Pressure-Sensitive and Removable

AdhesiveHow it bondsWash performanceTypical use
Heat seal (hot-melt film)Heat pressed for a few seconds, then cooledBest; survives normal home launderingActivewear, seamless underwear, cap linings
Pressure-sensitive (water-based acrylic)Bonds with hand pressure at room temperatureModerate; low-temperature gentle washing is saferBag linings, insoles, samples and displays
Removable (low tack)Peels and re-sticks, leaves no residueNot intended for washingPrice tags, POP displays, temporary marking
💡 Note: heat-seal adhesive needs a heat press, while pressure-sensitive labels can be applied by hand — but never heat press a pressure-sensitive label. Heat softens the adhesive prematurely and actually weakens the bond.

2. Choosing the Release Paper

  • Glassine — the most common choice: smooth, consistent in calliper, runs reliably on automatic applicators, ideal for small and medium labels
  • Kraft release paper — stiffer and tear resistant, good for large or long labels and less prone to curling when peeled by hand
  • PET release film — dimensionally stable and moisture resistant, with high die-cutting accuracy for precision and automated lines
  • Also check the backing itself: label thickness consistency (which affects how flush it sits), adhesive coat weight (which drives initial and lasting tack), and whether you need continuous roll die-cutting or individual cut pieces

3. Where Self-Adhesive Woven Labels Work Best

  • Caps and beanies — the inside of a cap is hard to reach with a sewing machine, so adhesive labels are the standard solution
  • Bags, footwear and accessories — linings, insoles and tongue interiors, where visible stitching would spoil the look
  • Seamless underwear and activewear — heat-seal versions can be bonded in the same step as the garment construction, with no scratchy stitching against the skin
  • Displays and pop-ups — removable adhesive suits samples, showroom displays, price tags and POP material, and peels off cleanly afterwards

4. Adhesive Versus Sewn-On Labels

FactorSelf-adhesiveSewn-on
ProcessNo sewing; by hand or heat press, fast cycle timeNeeds lockstitch, overlock or similar and a sewing operator
AppearanceNo needle holes or perforation marks, good for light and sheer fabricsVisible stitching; very secure but leaves needle holes
DurabilityDepends on the adhesive; heat-seal is durable, but heavy repeated washing can still lift edgesThe most durable; stands up to repeated industrial and home laundering
Suitable fabricsCotton, polyester, blends; silicone or water-repellent finishes and high-stretch fabrics need a trial firstAlmost any fabric, including coated and high-stretch materials

A practical rule: where a garment is washed repeatedly, or the fabric is heavy, sew the label on. Where sewing is awkward, appearance matters and wash intensity is controlled, use adhesive. For childrenswear, check the attachment-strength requirements too — adhesive labels must pass the relevant pull tests.

5. Heat Sealing: Settings and Pitfalls

Heat-seal results are set by temperature, dwell time and pressure together. Exact values depend on the adhesive, label thickness and fabric, so always run a trial on the real fabric first. A few rules of thumb save a lot of trial and error.

  • Too hot and the adhesive bleeds into the label and stiffens it; too cool and initial tack is too low, so the label peels off once cool
  • Fabrics treated with silicone, water repellents or softeners are hard for adhesive to wet, so bond strength drops noticeably. Run a wash test before committing.
  • Control tension on high-stretch fabrics: after bonding, the fabric springs back and lifts the edges, so bond close-fitting or cut after bonding
  • Let the bond rest and cure fully before the next operation or packing

6. Sampling and Specifying Your Labels

  • State the use case first: what fabric, whether it is washed, at what temperature and how often, and whether applicators are automated
  • Confirm adhesive coat weight and peel values, and ask the supplier for the adhesive type plus environmental compliance notes
  • Storage: keep sealed, away from light, heat and humidity; store rolls flat and use within the adhesive's shelf life
  • In sampling, test both steps: bond the label, then wash 3–5 times at the target temperature and check for lifting, stiffening or residue
