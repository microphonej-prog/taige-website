The Price Sticker Is the Smallest Trim, and the First One Questioned

By TAGE Packaging · Updated November 26, 2026

A garment leaves the factory with two to four stickers on it: price, barcode, size, origin. Their combined area is smaller than one hang tag, yet almost all of them stay stuck to the poly bag, get peeled off by the buyer and end up either in paper recycling or in incineration. Because they are small, almost nobody audits their chemistry — until a brand's legal team asks whether the sticker contaminates the customer's recycled pulp. This guide covers the three variables in a thermal sticker: developer, adhesive and liner, and how each affects recyclability, cost and the evidence you should hold.

1. What Is Actually in the Coating

Direct thermal paper works simply: the coating holds a colourless leuco dye, a developer and a sensitiser. The print head heats the coating, the developer melts and reacts with the dye, and the image turns black. What decides the environmental profile is not the paper but the developer. Bisphenol A was the classic choice — cheap, dense, weather-resistant — and it is restricted in the EU by REACH Annex XVII entry 66 at 0.02% by weight in thermal paper, applicable since 2 January 2020. Three routes replace it: bisphenol S (still a bisphenol, merely not named in that entry), phenol-free developers of the Pergafast type, and zinc-salt or ascorbic-acid systems.

2. Why the Developer Decides Recyclability

Paper recycling runs through repulping, screening, deinking and papermaking. Bisphenol developers survive that line almost intact and are not carried off by deinking: they stay in the pulp and reappear in recycled paper products. That, not a vague claim about being ungreen, is why mills refuse phenol-containing thermal paper. EN 13430 defines recoverability by material recycling as the ability to be turned into a product with existing processes, and repulpability can be assessed with the wet-disintegration test in ISO 5263. In other words, whether a sticker is recyclable is a question test data can answer, without adjectives. That is the value of a phenol-free developer: it makes the sentence the sticker goes into recycling with the bag factually true.

3. Adhesive and Liner: The Other Two Variables

  • Removable water-based pressure-sensitive adhesive: peels cleanly, leaves no residue on fabric, suits high try-on and return rates
  • Rubber hot-melt: strong initial tack and cold resistance, but strings when peeled and marks fine fabrics
  • Glassine liner: the silicone coating makes it nearly impossible to repulp — the real bottleneck for sticker recycling
  • Linerless: silicone is coated on the reverse of the thermal face, removing the liner layer, its freight and its waste

Most accusations that stickers are not eco-friendly aim at the wrong part. The problem is usually the thin glassine liner, not the sticker itself. A silicone coating cannot be repulped, and one A4-sized sheet of liner can wipe out the recycling value of the whole stack. There are two practical answers: switch to linerless rolls, which requires a compatible printer at the store, or collect liner separately from the stickers, which most factories do not manage. If a brand genuinely cares, start with one small question: how many kilograms of waste liner does this sticker order generate.

4. What Must Be Printed: Price Display Is a Legal Question

What goes on a price sticker is not a design choice but statutory labelling in each market. France requires the retail price to be shown to consumers inclusive of tax and in euros under the Code de la consommation. Spain requires the final sale price, tax included, under its retail commerce law (Ley 7/1996). Japan's Act against Unjustifiable Premiums and Misleading Representations governs dual pricing: a crossed-out former price needs a defensible comparison basis and period, or it becomes a misleading representation. Korea's Act on Fair Labelling and Advertising covers exaggerated claims. One thing often confused: fibre composition and care symbols belong to the care label under (EU) No 1007/2011 in Europe and the Household Goods Quality Labelling Act in Japan; a price sticker cannot replace that legal label.

5. Five Numbers to Put on the Purchase Order

  • Grammage: 70–80 g/m² is standard; thinner stock curls on high-speed applicators
  • Developer class and measured content: ask for an HPLC test report, not a verbal assurance
  • Initial tack and 180° peel (N/25 mm), with the test fabric named
  • Roll width and labels per roll: 40 mm and 58 mm are not interchangeable
  • Storage: below 35 °C and away from plasticiser-containing flexible PVC, which fades the print early

When a customer asks whether the sticker is eco-friendly, answer three things first: is the developer phenol-free, what happens to the liner, and can the sticker go into paper recycling with the bag. All three answer with data, so no adjectives are needed.

6. Where the Cost Diverges

For the same sticker size, the price gap comes from the developer (phenol-free costs more than bisphenol), the adhesive (removable water-based costs more than general-purpose hot-melt) and whether the roll is linerless (saves liner cost, needs new equipment). What widens total cost is rarely the unit price but returns and complaints: stickers that tear, leave residue, or fade after three months in a warehouse cost far more to handle than the per-sticker difference. If a brand is filing packaging EPR declarations — Spain's Real Decreto 1055/2022 or Germany's LUCID register — the sticker's grammage and material also enter the declared weight, which is why the spec sheet must state grammage rather than just thermal paper.
