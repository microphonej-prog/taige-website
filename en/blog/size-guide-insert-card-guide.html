Size Guide Insert Cards: The Cheapest Defence Against Apparel Returns

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 21, 2026

The most common reason an apparel order comes back is not quality. It is fit. The shopper sees an M, L, XL table on a phone, then receives a garment with its own block, its own shrinkage and its own way of being measured. The gap opens the moment the bag is unzipped, and the return process starts. A size guide card in the packaging answers the question in centimetres, before the garment is even unfolded, which makes it the cheapest defence available and the one that damages conversion least.

1. Card Formats: Two Sizes Cover Most Programmes

The two workhorses are 85 × 54 mm, credit-card size, which drops into a tag bag or a chest pocket, and 90 × 50 mm, half a business card with room for more on both sides. Choose 300–350 g/m² coated board on both sides: stiff in the hand and hard to dog-ear in transit. For humid markets or heavy handling, add a matte lamination to stop fingerprints and moisture pick-up. If the card doubles as a hang tag, allow a 3 mm drilled hole and do not substitute a punched sticker.

Size Board Fits
85 × 54 mm 300 g/m² One style, minimal content, tag bag
90 × 50 mm 350 g/m² Multi-market charts, both sides
100 × 70 mm 350 g/m² Kidswear, needs a measuring diagram

2. Four Blocks: A Measuring Lesson on One Card

A card holds very little, so keep four blocks: a measuring diagram, a size table, a cross-market comparison and fit notes from the model. The first shows how to measure chest, waist, hip and garment length, with arrows that mark start and end points. The second lists body measurements and flat measurements in centimetres as separate columns: mixing the two is the single largest source of complaints. The third maps EU, US, JP and KR codes. The fourth gives the model height, weight and size worn. That last block is the one most often dropped and often the most effective: a shopper of 165 cm and 55 kg who reads that the model is 168 cm in S will make the decision alone.

3. Cross-Market Sizing: Centimetres Are the Common Language

Every market runs its own system. The EU uses EN 13402, height plus body measurements, while French and Spanish stores still speak of taille 34 to 48. Japan uses JIS L 4004 with 7, 9 and 11 go. Korea uses KS K 0050 with 55, 66 and 77 ho. The US leans on numeric 0 to 16 or S to XL. China uses GB/T 1335 size codes such as 160/84A. Putting centimetres in the first column and the local code in the second is the only layout that does not mislead. Remember that no legal equivalence exists between national size codes, so the card must say the table is a reference and the measured dimensions govern.

China EU Japan Korea US
160/84A 34–36 7 go 55 ho 2–4
165/88A 38 9 go 66 ho 6–8
170/92A 40 11 go 77 ho 8–10
Add one line under the chart: this table is a reference, national size codes have no legal equivalence, and the measured centimetres in the table govern. That single line answers both the shopper and the platform: the shopper knows which column to read, and the listing makes no absolute promise it cannot keep.

4. Prepress and Finishing: Three Places It Goes Wrong

First, type height: the smallest number in a size table should be 5 pt, or 2 mm for Chinese characters; below that, matte lamination closes the counters. Second, rule weight: a 0.3 mm rule prints cleanly on 300 g/m² board, a 0.15 mm rule breaks up or prints as a dotted line. Third, dark grounds: keep reversed type above 4 pt light weights and never place it on a fold or a die-cut edge. If a QR code links to the online size chart, keep the module at 15 × 15 mm or larger, leave a 4 mm quiet zone and point it at a stable short URL rather than a link that changes every season.

5. Cost and Inspection: When It Pays

An 85 × 54 mm double-sided card at medium volume usually costs less than the range of a hang tag, yet it attacks costs that sit at order level: return freight, re-pressing, customer service tickets. Three signals say to do it: size dominates your return reasons, you sell across markets or borders, and the service desk answers which size should I buy every day. One of the three is enough; all three and the card should go double-sided with a measuring diagram. Inspect three things: finished size within plus or minus 0.5 mm, type height and rule weight, and whether the centimetres on the card match a real garment. Measure one piece in three sizes.

Check Method Pass limit
Finished size Caliper length and width Plus or minus 0.5 mm
Type height Loupe or caliper on the smallest numeral 5 pt or larger
Rule weight Inspect at 10× No breaks, no filling in
Data match Measure one piece in three sizes Within 1 cm of the card

The best size card is not the most decorated one. It is the one a shopper actually picks up while opening the bag. Keep it in the same typeface and palette as the hang tag, place it in the same spot, and let the unboxing run in order: packaging, then card, then garment. When the fit question is answered at the moment of opening, the most common trigger for a return never fires.
