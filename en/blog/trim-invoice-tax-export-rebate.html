Trims Invoices, VAT Rates and Export Rebate: A Buyer's Guide

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 18, 2026

One hang tag quoted at 0.28 and another at 0.25, and only at contract stage does it emerge that one price includes tax and the other does not — the real gap is not 11% but 13%. In trims purchasing, three things left unsaid at the enquiry stage — the pricing basis, the invoice type and export rebate — always turn into a dispute at reconciliation.

This guide covers five steps: the three bases on a quotation, the difference between special and ordinary invoices plus the applicable rates, who claims the rebate when trims ship with finished garments, the invoicing rhythm of monthly reconciliation, and a tax information sheet you can paste straight into an enquiry email.

1. Three Bases to Separate on a Quotation

Tax-inclusive, tax-exclusive and delivered prices are three different numbers, and mixing them guarantees a wrong comparison. There is one formula: net price = gross price ÷ (1 + rate). At 13%, a gross 11.30 corresponds to a net 10.00; treating the gross figure as net overstates cost by a full 13%.

  • Tax: state either includes 13% VAT or excludes tax. A bare unit price tells the buyer nothing.
  • Freight: trims are often charged by weight or volume, and delivered-to-factory versus ex-works differs by a whole freight leg.
  • Tooling: dies, screens and jacquard plates are usually charged separately on a first order. State whether they are one-off or built into the unit price.
  • Rates are not uniform: paper, plastics and printed goods sit at the 13% goods rate, while design, warehousing and testing services run at 6%. Bundled into one line they never reconcile.

2. Special vs Ordinary Invoices, and Who Can Deduct

The invoice type decides whether the buyer can deduct input tax, and therefore what the trims actually cost in your books. Compare suppliers on net cost after deduction, not on unit price.

Convert every quote to net cost before comparing: net cost = gross price × (1 − rate ÷ (1 + rate)). The factor is about 0.885 for a 13% special invoice, 0.990 for a 1% special invoice and 1.000 for an ordinary invoice. On the same gross 11.30, a 13% special invoice nets about 10.00, a 1% special invoice 11.19 and an ordinary invoice the full 11.30 — a 13% gap, not 1%.

3. Export Rebate: Trims Riding With the Garment, or Shipping Alone

Most trims never ship alone: they travel with the finished garment. The exporter, not the trims factory, is then the party claiming the rebate — the trims supplier's job is to issue the right invoice with the right description.

  • Garment factory exporting directly: the trims invoice enters the garment cost and the exporter applies the exempt-credit-refund method; the trims supplier simply invoices the factory.
  • Trading company buying and exporting: the exempt-refund route applies, and purchase invoices must match the customs declaration line by line on description, quantity and amount.
  • Trims exporting alone: the trims exporter claims, and the rebate rate follows the commodity code — paper, plastics and textiles do not all sit at the same rate.
  • Purchasing without invoices or exporting on someone else's documents leaves no rebate base at all; that cost stays with you and no discount on price makes it back.

The most common rebate problem is a description mismatch across the customs declaration, the special invoice and the foreign exchange receipt. A vague trims description such as hang tag on the declaration against paper hang tag with cotton string on the invoice triggers a request for explanation and costs two or three weeks.

4. Reconciliation and Invoicing Rhythm on Monthly Terms

  1. Sign the delivery note on the spot with date, quantity and any exception — shortages, damage. That sheet is the only primary evidence at reconciliation, and it beats a chat log.
  2. Issue the statement on a fixed day each month and invoice after both sides confirm. The contract must say whether the credit period starts at confirmation or at invoice receipt.
  3. An error found after month-end needs a red-letter invoice, not a void. Voiding across periods leaves both filings out of balance.
  4. Keep invoice, goods and payment aligned: the invoiced party, the delivery address and the paying account should be the same entity, or every later audit needs a layer of explanation.

5. Put the Tax Basis Into the Enquiry Itself

These six lines in the first email save more money than any later price negotiation: they are what makes two quotations comparable at all.
