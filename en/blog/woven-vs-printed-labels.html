Woven Labels vs Printed Labels: Which Is Right for Your Garments?

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated August 15, 2026

Brand labels are a small detail with a big impact — they're on every garment you sell, and customers feel them against their skin. Two main options dominate the market: woven labels and printed (heat transfer / printed) labels. Both have strengths; the right choice depends on your product, budget and brand image.

What Is a Woven Label?

A woven label is created by weaving threads together — usually satin, polyester, or cotton — to form the design. The logo becomes part of the fabric itself, so it never peels, cracks or fades with washing.

  • Satin — smooth, glossy, premium feel; ideal for main labels and size labels
  • Polyester — durable and colorfast, great for activewear
  • Cotton — soft and natural, perfect for organic or baby clothing
  • Taffeta — fine and smooth, common for size labels

What Is a Printed (Heat Transfer) Label?

Printed labels are made by printing the design onto a film or transfer paper, then applying it to the garment with heat and pressure. Some are printed directly on fabric strips.

  • Heat transfer labels — printed on a thin film, pressed onto the garment; great for seamless sportswear
  • Printed fabric labels — ink printed onto woven or non-woven fabric, then folded and sewn

When to Choose Woven

  • Your brand logo is the star — you want it to feel premium
  • Main neck labels that will be washed repeatedly
  • Mid-to-high-end apparel, denim, formal wear

When to Choose Printed / Heat Transfer

  • Care labels with washing instructions (need fine text)
  • Seamless or performance sportswear where tags must be invisible
  • Small quantities or frequent design changes (new collections)
  • Budget-conscious startups testing the market

Many Brands Use Both

The most common combination: a woven main label for the brand name, plus a printed care label with washing instructions and composition. This balances premium feel where customers look, with cost efficiency and legibility where they don't.

Cost Structure: Where the Money Actually Goes

Woven label cost sits in three places: the weaving plate (jacquard programme), the weaving itself, and finishing (cutting, folding, ultrasonic sealing). The larger the run, the thinner the plate cost spreads. Printed labels concentrate cost in printing plus transfer or sewing, with no weaving step, so the curve is flatter. At equal size a woven label usually costs more per piece, but the gap narrows with volume and simpler artwork — and at small quantities the gap is often negligible, because plate cost dominates both.

Setting the Spec: Size, Fold and Density

Main labels are commonly 20×50 mm or 25×60 mm; care labels 20×40 mm or 30×60 mm. Fold style affects cost and feel more than size: folding the same tape twice adds a layer of thickness and consumes more fabric, which is a frequent reason two apparently identical labels quote differently.

Density (needles per centimetre) sets how fine the artwork can go. Low density suits large lettering and colour blocks; high density allows gradients and fine lines, at a higher price and slower weaving. The practical threshold is type height: anything under 2 mm should go to printed or heat-transfer labels, because woven text that small turns to mush — and worse after two washes.

Care and Composition Labels: Regulation Is Harder Than Craft

EU: Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011 requires textile fibre composition to be labelled in an official language of the member state where the product is sold, and care symbols follow EN ISO 3758. This is an obligation, not a recommendation — wrong labels can trigger withdrawal or fines, especially for brands selling in several countries where the same garment may need different label languages in France and Germany.

US: the FTC Textile Fiber Products Identification Act and 16 CFR Part 303 require fibre content, country of origin and the dealer's name; care labelling falls under 16 CFR Part 423. Children's products must also meet CPSIA limits on lead and phthalates, which directly constrains the inks and trims you can specify.

Japan and Korea: Japan's Household Goods Quality Labelling Act requires fibre composition, care instructions and the labeller's name; Korea requires Korean-language labelling and the responsible importer under KS K 0021 and its household-goods safety rules. Both demand a language the local consumer reads — an English-only label is non-compliant.

The practical conclusion: a main label can compromise for brand aesthetics, a care label cannot. If type size, language or symbols are wrong, a whole shipment can be rejected at customs or at the store gate — a loss far larger than the label saving. With a tight budget, fund care-label compliance first, then main-label craft.

Sampling and Approval: Five Checks That Matter

  1. Wash test: three cycles at 40 °C, then check for frayed edges and cracked or whitened print.
  2. Rub test: 50 cycles against the shell fabric to check for colour transfer.
  3. Type and legibility: at actual size, check whether the thinnest stroke still reads — do not judge on a zoomed screen.
  4. Stitch strength: pull-test the seam; thread must not break and the fabric edge must not tear, especially at the neck.
  5. Labelling compliance: check composition, origin, care symbols and language one by one — a missing item means no sign-off.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much more do woven labels cost?

At equal size and quantity, a woven label usually costs more per piece, with the gap driven by weaving and folding labour; larger runs and simpler artwork narrow it. Under 1,000 pieces the gap is often negligible because plate and set-up dominate — at that point the better question is which option expresses the brand, not which is cheaper.

Should the neck label be woven or printed?

The neck label takes constant friction from fingers and skin plus repeated washing, so durability comes first — which is why main neck labels are usually woven. Brands on a seamless or tagless route switch to heat transfer pressed straight onto the fabric; the trade-off is that wash testing becomes mandatory, or the print cracks.

Will a woven care label wash off?

A woven label itself will not wash off; the problem is content. Care labels carry dense fine print in several languages, which weaving cannot reproduce, so printed or heat-transfer labels are the norm. The real risk is not washing off but a print that does not match the fabric — choose a transfer film with the same fibre base as the shell and wash-test first, or you get cracking, whitening or full delamination.

If we use both, can the shipment end up mismatched?

Not if both are ordered from the same supplier against one colour card and spec sheet. The practical method is a single trims specification covering main label, care label, hang tag and poly bag, with size, composition, colour reference, fold and placement — this prevents each factory interpreting differently, which is the most common source of trim errors and buyer complaints.

Matching Materials and Finishes

  • Organic cotton: a cotton woven label stitched with cotton thread — polyester thread undermines the all-cotton story and invites awkward audit questions.
  • Performance sportswear: polyester woven or heat transfer, resistant to sweat and washing; cotton labels absorb water, get heavy and feel uncomfortable against skin.
  • Denim: a heavy woven label with contrast stitching for a rugged look; choose a dark care-label base to limit cross-staining with indigo denim.
  • Underwear and babywear: soft-touch labels or direct heat transfer to avoid scratching skin and faces — no category is more sensitive to label edges.
  • Gift boxes and premium lines: satin woven with foil or high-density jacquard — spend the budget where the customer actually touches, not across the whole garment.

Three Common Misconceptions

  • Comparing unit price only and ignoring changeover: every woven design change needs a new plate, and in quick-response models that can cost more than the fabric.
  • Treating the care label as decoration: composition and care symbols are legal requirements — one wrong symbol can get a listing pulled, regardless of brand taste.
  • Ignoring shrinkage: cotton labels shrink in the wash and pucker if their rate differs from the garment; wash the label together with the fabric during sampling.

On lead time: sampling usually takes 3–7 days depending on process, and production timing is confirmed by the factory against its schedule once the sample is approved. Build buffer into peak seasons (spring/summer development, autumn/winter reorders), especially for changeovers needing a new plate. Ordering a week earlier protects your launch date far better than squeezing the price.

Care Label Content Template: What Must Appear

For EU sales, a care label usually carries fibre composition (highest content first), country of origin, the care symbols (wash, bleach, dry, iron, professional care) and the responsible company name or registration number. Selling into both Germany and France means meeting each language requirement; for multilingual versions, pair symbols with two languages rather than squeezing the type below 1.5 mm.

  • Composition: fibres under 5% can often be grouped as other fibres, but the detail differs by market — verify country by country before shipping.
  • Care symbols: follow EN ISO 3758 order and keep each symbol at least 5 mm; anything smaller becomes unreadable once the label creases.
  • Origin: state Made in China or its local-language equivalent, and keep it consistent with customs paperwork and the certificate of origin — mismatches get questioned at the border.
  • Responsible party: brand or importer name and address, traceable as the EU requires; a brand name alone leaves you unable to answer a complaint.

Small care labels force a trade-off between completeness and legibility. The practical split: composition and symbols on the care label, service contact and origin on the back of the hang tag. That lets a 20×40 mm care label stay uncluttered. If a QR code must lead to a care page, keep it at least 8 mm and print it in high-contrast single colour, or creases will defeat the scan.

If the brand needs proof on file, ask the supplier for fibre-composition test reports (ISO 1833 series) and colour-fastness reports (ISO 105 series); for US shipments, platforms and retailers often also want FTC compliance statements and test records. Paper beats a verbal assurance, especially once a complaint lands.

How to Start: Three Steps to a Comparable Quote

  1. Give the garment type, quantity and target market: the market determines compliance and label language, and skipping it guarantees a re-quote.
  2. Send a vector logo plus preferred size, fold and colour: a vector file (AI or PDF) saves a round of back-and-forth, and a Pantone reference beats saying navy.
  3. Ask for samples and a quote, and confirm whether sampling fees are credited against bulk: send the same spec to two or three suppliers so scope differences do not mislead the comparison.
