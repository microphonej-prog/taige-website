French Clothing Label Rules: French Text, AGEC and the Triman Mark

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 23, 2026

French clothing label compliance rests on three hard lines: the French language, permanence, and traceability. Plenty of exporters assume that stating fibre composition per EU 1007/2011 is enough, then find the goods held in France for relabelling by the buyer or the DGCCRF — and the gap is rarely composition, it is language, origin and producer-responsibility marks.

This guide covers five things: where the French-language requirement actually bites, what a permanent label has to carry, the Triman mark and eco-contribution introduced by the AGEC law, how composition tolerance works, and the six checks to run before sampling. Written for brands, multi-brand retailers and e-commerce sellers working the French market.

1. French Is Not Optional

France's law of 4 August 1994, No. 94-665 (the Loi Toubon), requires product information intended for French consumers to be written in French. Brand names and trademarks may stay in their original form, but descriptive wording — composition, care, origin, importer details — must exist in French. Printing English only and putting a French version on the website is generally not accepted.

In practice, print English and French side by side on the same care label: two blocks, identical type size, with French never smaller or visually subordinate. Use the standard industry wording — Matière, Entretien, Fabriqué en — rather than inventing abbreviations, because retail intake checks and customs look for those exact terms.

2. Four Items a Permanent Label Must Carry

  • Fibre composition: descending by weight, using standard French fibre names rather than trade names.
  • Care instructions: ISO 3758 symbols plus French wording, in the fixed order of washing, bleaching, drying, ironing and professional care.
  • Origin: worded as Fabriqué en Chine, Fabriqué au Portugal and so on, matching the actual production country and the customs paperwork.
  • Responsible party: the EU importer or the French entity answerable for the goods, worded as Importé par ... ; since the EU General Product Safety Regulation took effect this line is checked far more closely than before.

All four can sit on one label, or be split between a main label and a care label, provided the result is permanently attached and still legible after washing. Coated paper labels that blur after three washes are one of the most common causes of French returns.

3. The AGEC Law: Triman, Eco-Contribution and Environmental Labelling

The AGEC law (law of 10 February 2020, No. 2020-105) brings textiles into extended producer responsibility. Whoever places garments on the French market — usually the French brand or importer — pays an eco-contribution to Refashion and files data by category. The exporter does not pay it, but must supply the inputs: shipped quantities, material mix, whether recycled fibre is present. Without those numbers the responsible party cannot file.

On the label side, the requirement is the Triman mark plus Info-tri sorting information, telling the consumer which bin takes the garment, the hang tag and the packaging. When you need to print Triman, ask the client for the authorised artwork rather than downloading an image: wrong size, clear space or accompanying text gets the delivery rejected at intake.

💡 Tip: France's environmental labelling scheme has slipped repeatedly; treat the client's latest instruction as the operative rule. While it is unsettled, do not print an eco-score or a carbon figure on the tag yourself — an unsupported environmental claim costs more than it returns.

4. Composition Tolerance and French Terminology

Under EU 1007/2011, blended declarations carry a tolerance of 3 percentage points; declaring 100% means the whole garment is that fibre, with only minimal process impurities allowed. Note the French typographic convention of a space before the percent sign (5 % élasthanne) — a small detail retail auditors do look at.

5. Five Mistakes That Actually Happen

  • English-only labels with French stickers. Stickers count as temporary marking, retail audits reject them, and the rework costs more than reprinting the whole care label run.
  • Origin text contradicting customs documents. Fabriqué en Chine on the label with an invoice routed through a third country is exactly where a fraud inquiry starts.
  • Triman artwork downloaded from the web. Size, clear space and accompanying text are all specified; the client's file is the only safe source.
  • Illegible after washing. French consumers tolerate a vanished care label poorly; coated paper labels or low-density tape fade after three washes and become the return reason.
  • Composition copied from the tech pack without checking bulk fabric. The spec says 95 % coton, a different yarn lot lands at 92 % — beyond the 3-point tolerance and therefore non-compliant.

6. Six Checks Before Sampling

  1. Agree who supplies and who proofreads the French text — attach it to the order so nobody edits a separate copy.
  2. Position and attachment of the permanent label: side seam, centre back neck or hem, per the brand manual rather than factory habit.
  3. Walk the fibre-name table line by line, especially elastane, viscose and polyamide — the three most often translated straight from English.
  4. Align the origin wording across label, customs declaration, invoice and packing list; one mismatch means stop.
  5. Confirm whether Triman / Info-tri is required, and have the client supply the authorised file and specify where it prints.
  6. Legibility after washing: run the three-wash test at sampling stage, not when bulk arrives at the warehouse.
