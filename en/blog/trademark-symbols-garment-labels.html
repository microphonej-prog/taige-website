Trademark Symbols on Labels and Hang Tags: TM, (R), Copyright and Registration

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 31, 2026

Which symbol goes on a neck label, hang tag or bag looks like a detail, yet it is the only legal statement of brand ownership on the physical product. Put ® in the wrong place and in some countries it is false marking; use ™ where it does not apply and you have said nothing; print ® on a mark still under application and you have kept the risk on your own side.

This article sets out what the four symbols actually mean, the difference before and after registration, practice in the main markets, the legibility floor on woven labels and hang tags, and how to handle a customer's trademark on OEM orders. All of it can be checked in ten minutes before the order goes out.

1. What the four symbols actually mean

Symbol Meaning When it may be used
™ A trademark claim, asserting rights in the mark Any mark in use, no registration needed; it confers no registered rights
® Registered trademark notice Only for marks granted in that country and used on the goods or services covered by the registration
© Copyright notice, for artwork, copy, photography and similar works Unrelated to trademark registration; the notice does not create the right
℠ Service mark notice, used mainly in the United States Used where a service is offered rather than goods, such as retail or design services

2. The risk of using ® before registration

The question that comes up most often is whether ® can be printed while the application is pending. It cannot. A pending application only means the file was filed, not that rights exist, whereas ® legally asserts that registration has been granted.

  • United States: using ® on an unregistered mark is false marking and can expose you to damages; during the application stage only ™ applies.
  • European Union: marking is not compulsory, but putting ® on an unregistered mark can be treated as a misleading commercial practice.
  • China: only registered marks may carry ® or the words for registered trademark; using them without registration is passing off a registered mark and is penalised.
  • Practical approach: use ™ during the application period, switch artwork once the certificate issues, and put that switch in the packaging version log so old and new do not ship together.

3. How practice differs between the main markets

The same shipment sold into different countries may need a different symbol. The table below covers the four most common export markets; a local agent or lawyer still has the final word.

Market Practice Watch out for
United States ® only for USPTO registrations; ™ needs no registration The symbol should sit with the mark; no set size in law
European Union Marking optional; ® for registered EU or member state marks ® on an unregistered mark may be deemed misleading
China Registered marks may use ® or the Chinese wording for registered trademark Use without registration is passing off and is penalised
Japan ™ has no legal status; ® for registered marks Usually placed at the upper right of the mark, consistent with the registration number

4. Layout on woven labels and hang tags: the legibility floor

A woven label is not a screen; it only counts if it is legible at a few millimetres. Many brands approve a beautiful layout on screen, then receive a 3 cm neck label where the ® has become a black dot — the notice is effectively absent.

  • Minimum size: the ® as a superscript needs at least 1 mm diameter; the spelled-out (R) needs more, usually 2 mm character height.
  • Weaving limits: a jacquard woven label cannot render a true superscript; a small capital in a circle or plain (R) is normal, and it must be confirmed at sampling.
  • Colour: keep the symbol in the same colour as the mark; highlighting it in gold reads as decoration rather than a rights notice.
  • Placement: immediately at the upper or lower right of the mark, not in the corner of the layout, where it reads as annotating neighbouring text.

5. Brand name, company name and licensing

Labels often carry both a brand name and a company name, and the two are legally different: the brand carries the trademark, the company carries responsibility. EU product safety rules and most marketplaces require the latter to be traceable.

  • Match the owner: the party placing ® should be the registrant or a licensee, never the contract factory.
  • State the responsible party: name plus a reachable physical address; a brand name alone leaves both regulators and platforms with nobody to contact.
  • Show the licence: in licensed production, a line such as a registered trademark of X, used under licence, is safer than printing two logos side by side.

6. Export and OEM: handling the customer's trademark

On OEM work the trademark on the label belongs to the customer and the factory is only a user, yet the risk of getting it wrong often lands on the factory — especially when customs detains a shipment or a marketplace delists it.

  • Get written authorisation: covering brand, goods class, sales territory and validity period, and keep it on file.
  • Customs recordal: for exports from China, a customs recordal of the IP right lets you produce evidence quickly if goods are detained.
  • Change control: when the customer changes logo colour, adds a sub-brand or issues a new version, require written confirmation and stop shipping the old label.

7. The seven most common mistakes

  • Using ® while the application is pending, claiming a right not yet granted.
  • Using ® in a country where the mark is not registered, for example registered only in China but printed on hang tags exported to the EU.
  • Class mismatch: registered for clothing, yet ® appears on bags, which fall in another class.
  • Changing logo colour or typeface without refiling, so the mark in use no longer matches the registered representation.
  • Missing renewal: protection usually runs ten years, and continuing to print ® after expiry is high risk.
  • Mixing ™ and ® on one label, or running two states for one brand, confuses both consumers and channels.
  • Using the brand name as a generic term, weakening distinctiveness over time and making enforcement harder later.
