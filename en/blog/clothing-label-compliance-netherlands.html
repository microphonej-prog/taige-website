Netherlands Apparel Labelling and Packaging Compliance: Dutch Marking, Textile EPR and Reporting

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 14, 2026

The Netherlands is one of Europe's key import and distribution markets for apparel, with Rotterdam and Schiphol handling large inbound volumes. Brands selling or shipping there also face two local duties that are easy to miss: consumer information has to be presented in Dutch, and the textile extended producer responsibility (UPV Textiel) has applied since 1 July 2023. Mark labels and packaging to another EU country's convention and goods arriving in the Netherlands may need reworking, while online channels will also be asked about registration and reporting.

1. Which Labelling Rules Apply in the Netherlands

  • The Netherlands is an EU member state, so fibre composition follows the EU textile labelling regulation: official fibre names, percentages by weight from highest to lowest, and composition visible to the consumer at the point of sale.
  • Language is the most common trap: fibre composition must appear in the official language of the member state where the product is sold, that is Dutch. A care label in English or German only does not comply in the Netherlands, and Dutch alongside English is the usual practical arrangement.
  • Care symbols and warnings also need to land in Dutch: symbols may be combined with text, and anything carrying a safety warning — children's cords, small parts, poly bags — must be directly understandable to a Dutch consumer.
  • Origin, importer details and barcodes follow channel requirements: Dutch retail and online platforms usually expect the importer or responsible party named on the packaging or tag, with barcodes matching their stock system.

2. Textile EPR (UPV Textiel): Who Is Liable and How to Report

  • Check the scope: the Dutch textile EPR has applied since 1 July 2023 and covers clothing, workwear and household textiles such as bedding and table linen first placed on the Dutch market, whether sold or given away.
  • Liability is not limited to where you are registered: besides Dutch brands and manufacturers, foreign companies selling remotely into the Netherlands, including web shops and cross-border marketplace sellers, are in scope and must appoint an authorised representative in the Netherlands.
  • There are two compliance routes: register directly with the Dutch authority, handled through the Rijkswaterstaat system, or join a producer responsibility organisation. The main Dutch textile PRO is Stichting UPV Textiel.
  • Reporting is annual: by 1 August each year you report the quantity placed on the Dutch market in the previous calendar year together with the results achieved. Where you join a PRO, the PRO files the report, but the volume data still has to come from you.
  • Fees and duties: the PRO charges a management fee by weight placed on the market, published annually and typically in the order of ten-plus euro cents per kilogram. You must also organise and finance separate collection of discarded textiles, ensure reuse or recycling, and tell consumers where and how to return them.

3. Packaging Producer Responsibility and Marking

  • Packaging is a separate line besides textiles: the Netherlands runs its own packaging producer responsibility, so whoever puts packaging on the Dutch market — including poly bags, paper bags, cartons and label backing used with garments — must declare the volume and pay by material category.
  • Declarations are broken down by material: plastic and paper or board are normally listed separately, and weight versus unit conventions affect the fee. For a brand, cutting over-packaging or making the inner pack a recyclable single material reduces both the declared weight and the fee.
  • EU-level packaging rules are tightening design-for-recycling and marking requirements, so material marks and sorting instructions on labels and packaging will gradually align. The Dutch specifics, fee levels and timelines follow the current notices of the authority and the producer responsibility organisation.

4. Putting Dutch Marking onto the Trims

  • Settle the language list for care labels and neck labels first: a Netherlands-only range can carry Dutch alone, while Benelux or wider European distribution needs Dutch alongside French and English. More languages means a tighter layout, so font and symbol sizes must be recalculated.
  • Trademark and responsible-party details must match reality: the importer or responsible party, address and contact details on the hang tag should agree with the Dutch registration or authorised representative, or a platform audit will not reconcile.
  • Packaging marks must match the packaging itself: poly bag warnings, paper bag recycling notes and backing material marks are best printed in one pass, because topping up later is how old and new versions end up mixed in the same carton.
  • Write the Dutch duties into the trim enquiry: note the Netherlands as the market, Dutch marking, and any EPR registration number or responsible-party details in the spec sheet, so the supplier reserves the space before artwork and no second pass is needed.
💡 Tip: the two items most often assumed to be handled are language and the reporting party. Fibre composition in English alone does not comply, and a foreign seller without a Dutch authorised representative cannot complete registration or the annual report. Settle the language list and the responsible party before the packaging artwork.

5. A Five-Step Checklist for Trim Purchasing

  • First, confirm the sales route: Dutch domestic retail, a cross-border platform or European distribution. Each route changes the language list, the responsible party and the packaging marks.
  • Second, check your EPR status: establish whether you are the party first placing goods on the Dutch market, then decide between direct registration and joining a PRO, and pass the registration number to the label and packaging supplier.
  • Third, settle languages and layout: how many languages each care label, neck label, hang tag and pack needs, whether symbols are paired with text, and what font size stays legible — fixed in one session, with room reserved for a future language.
  • Fourth, model the declaration and cost: work out the annual textile and packaging weight placed in the Netherlands, estimate the EPR contribution, then compare it with weight reduction and single-material packaging options.
  • Fifth, archive everything: registration confirmation, annual report receipts, the authorised representative mandate and wash test reports kept together, ready for platform audits, channel spot checks or customer due diligence.
