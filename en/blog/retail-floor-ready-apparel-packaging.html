Floor-Ready Apparel Packaging: Tags, Tickets, Hanging and Carton Labels

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 12, 2026

Floor-ready (or retail-ready) packaging means the goods arrive at the store or e-commerce warehouse needing no further unpacking, ticketing or re-hangering — they go straight onto the floor. For apparel brands selling into European and North American retail, that means hang tags, price tickets, hanging method, poly bags and carton labels all have to follow the retailer's compliance manual. Miss one, and the shipment is either reworked on arrival or rejected outright.

This guide first outlines what a compliance manual usually covers, then breaks down how hang tags and price tickets divide the job, how hanging and flat-packed garments differ, and the key parameters for bags and carton labels — closing with a pre-launch self-check.

1. What Floor-Ready Actually Requires

Retailers start from store labour cost: every minute of manual work on the shop floor costs money, so suppliers are asked to finish whatever can be finished before shipping. Keep that logic in mind and the manuals, however differently worded, line up.

  • Identification done at source: brand tag, retail ticket, composition and care information plus barcode should all be attached at the factory — no re-ticketing or re-labelling in store
  • Presentation already decided: hanging or flat, which hanger, what hanging ratio — all executed before shipment, per the manual
  • Data matching goods: when carton label, packing list and advance shipping notice disagree, that is one of the most common chargeback causes at many retailers

2. What a Compliance Manual Usually Covers

Chapter titles differ from one retailer to the next, but the substance concentrates in the five blocks below. Getting the manual version number and effective date before you sign is far cheaper than guessing at details.

3. What Hang Tags, Tickets and Stickers Each Do

The three carry different jobs in the retail flow, and mixing them up causes store-side rework. The split is simple: the hang tag carries the brand, the ticket carries price and stock, and stickers handle temporary or local needs.

  • Brand hang tag: carries brand and product information, usually designed by the brand, and its position must avoid the reserved areas for tickets and security devices
  • Retail ticket: holds price, size, style number and barcode; customers usually supply the template, so type size, barcode grade and position must follow it exactly
  • Stickers: used for price changes, origin or short promotions; watch adhesion and residue, because a sticker that tears poorly leaves marks in store
  • One device per spot: a ticket laid over the hang tag, or an anti-theft tag covering the barcode, breaks scanning at the till or during stock counts

4. Hanging or Flat-Packed

Hanging suits structured categories — outerwear, shirts, dresses — because shoppers read the shape straight away; flat packing saves freight and hangers and suits knits, T-shirts and underwear. Once a manual sets a hanging ratio, it is an exact figure and cannot be rounded.

  • Hanger model: manuals specify model, length, colour and hook style; mixing hangers is glaringly obvious on a rack
  • Header cards and fasteners: card hole position and fastener needle type must match fabric thickness; on knits, avoid leaving needle marks
  • Bags and warnings: vent hole count and position plus warning language and type size follow the manual, and childrenswear is the strictest
  • Packing sequence: packing by style, colour and size, or by destination store, decides whether the carton can go straight to the shelf when opened
The poly bag warning on childrenswear and babywear is the most commonly missed item: language, type size and position are all specified, and an omitted warning often means a rejected lot. Send us a screenshot of that page of the customer manual to check before the order — far cheaper than reworking later.

5. Cartons and Labels: Making the Data Match

The carton is the only physical unit that travels from factory to forwarder to distribution centre to store. Once a label cannot be scanned, the whole chain stops for manual handling, which is why penalties are usually counted per carton.

  • Label format: common practice is GS1-128 carrying an SSCC serial, on the customer's template; barcodes must be graded, not judged by eye
  • Application position: manuals often fix the face and the distance from the edge; labels on corners or over sealing tape get rubbed off or torn
  • Print once, at the end: label after the carton is sealed; printing first and packing later is how numbers end up attached to the wrong goods
  • Align with the ASN: cross-check carton numbers, quantities and style-colour codes against the ASN before dispatch, and data chargebacks largely disappear

6. Six Checks Before Launch

  1. Manual version: confirm you hold the latest compliance manual and its effective date, and file it with the project
  2. All identifiers present: tag, ticket, composition and care, barcode, security device — tick each one off against the manual
  3. Bag compliance: check thickness, vent holes, warning language and type size item by item; spot-check the warning on childrenswear batches
  4. Scan every carton: scan at least once after applying, log the failures and re-label on the spot
  5. Cross-check the data: carton count and quantity must agree across packing list, ASN and labels, with batches traceable to production records
  6. Reference and change control: keep one complete floor-ready set from the first shipment, and re-confirm the spec whenever the manual or template changes
