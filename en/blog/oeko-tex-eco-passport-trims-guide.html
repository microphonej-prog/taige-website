OEKO-TEX ECO PASSPORT for Trims: It Certifies the Chemistry, Not the Hang Tag

By TAGE Packaging · Updated November 25, 2026

Nobody asks what a hang tag, a spool of sewing thread or a metre of interlining is made of at delivery. The brand's compliance team asks something else: does the ink printed on that hang tag, the adhesive that bonds the interlining or the dye that colours the thread come with third-party proof at chemical level? OEKO-TEX ECO PASSPORT exists for exactly this layer. It certifies the chemicals entering production, not the finished trim. We make custom hang tags, woven labels and care labels in Dongguan, and this guide sets out what the certificate proves, which BOM lines fall inside its scope, and the two documents a buyer should request together.

1. It Certifies the Chemistry, Not the Hang Tag

ECO PASSPORT applies to chemicals, auxiliaries and colorants used in the textile and leather supply chain: printing inks and pigment pastes, dyes for sewing thread, binders coated onto interlining. The process runs in three stages. The first screens every formulation ingredient at CAS number level against the OEKO-TEX limit value lists and the REACH SVHC candidate list; the second has an OEKO-TEX approved laboratory verify the sample analytically; the third is an optional on-site audit of chemical management. A certificate is valid for one year, and any change in formulation restarts the process.

One line to tell the two certificates apart: an ECO PASSPORT is held by the ink maker, adhesive maker or dyehouse, while a STANDARD 100 by OEKO-TEX is held by the textile or trim producer and tests the finished article. Buyers need both; with only one, the chain is broken.

2. Which BOM Lines Fall Inside the Scope

A trim bill of materials usually records item name, specification, grammage and unit price; the chemical layer underneath is invisible. These four groups are the ones buyers chase most often, and they are exactly where ECO PASSPORT fills the gap.

Trim line Chemistry involved Document to request Verifiable test
Hang tag printing ink Soy, water-based or UV pigment paste Ink maker's ECO PASSPORT plus printer's STANDARD 100 EN 14362-1, EN 71-3
Lamination and hot-melt adhesive EVA, PUR, water-based adhesive Adhesive maker's ECO PASSPORT plus migration test report EN 71-3, EN ISO 105-E04
Sewing thread and interlining Dyes, softeners, coating binders Yarn or fabric mill ECO PASSPORT EN 14362-1, GB/T 18401
Metal hardware (tag pin, eyelet) Plating and sealing lacquer Supplier test report plus material declaration EN 1811, EN 12472

3. The Four Tests That Back the Claim

The certificate describes the formulation; the test describes this batch. Brand audits normally want the two to line up, so these are the results that carry most weight on the trim side and the ones most often written into a tech pack.

  • EN 14362-1: aromatic amines released by azo colorants under reducing conditions - the entry test recognised in both the EU and the Chinese market.
  • EN 71-3: migration of certain elements such as lead and cadmium, routinely requested for hang tags and stickers a child might handle or mouth.
  • EN ISO 105-E04: colour fastness to perspiration, which maps directly onto the complaint that ink or dye transfers to skin.
  • EN 1811 with EN 12472 wear simulation: nickel release from metal hardware, relevant to tag pins, eyelets and metal hang tags.
  • GB/T 18401: the basic safety requirements for textile products in China, covering formaldehyde, pH, odour and decomposable aromatic amines.

4. Where the Certificate Stops: Levels and MRSL Cross-Checks

The third stage is optional, which is why three levels appear in the supply chain: screening only, screening plus analysis, and screening plus analysis plus on-site audit. ZDHC MRSL conformance levels 1 to 3 follow similar logic, while bluesign APPROVED speaks yet another language. The practical gaps are not about forged certificates but about three things: the ink maker registered the base ink, yet thinners, hardeners and tack reducers added at press may sit outside that scope; different pigment pastes under the same ink name are registered separately, and buyers often hold only the main colour; and after a supplier change or reformulation, the old certificate stays on the website. Ask for the CAS list and the expiry date along with the certificate.

5. Market Notes

  • EU: REACH (EC) 1907/2006 Annex XVII restrictions and the SVHC candidate list set the floor; GPSR (EU) 2023/988 adds an EU responsible person and traceability information; packaging falls under PPWR (EU) 2025/40.
  • France: the Code de la consommation sets fibre composition and labelling duties, care symbols follow EN ISO 3758, and the AGEC law n° 2020-105 adds the Triman mark with the info-tri sorting notice.
  • Spain: the waste regime of Ley 7/2022 and the packaging requirements of Real Decreto 1055/2022 apply together; trim labels must carry etiquetado and composición, and regional recycling marks vary.
  • Japan: the Household Goods Quality Labelling Act requires composition, blend ratio, care labelling and country of origin, with washing symbols under JIS L 0001; environmental claims fall under the Act against Unjustifiable Premiums and Misleading Representations, where a vague eco-friendly line risks a misrepresentation finding.
  • Korea: the Quality Management and Safety Control of Industrial Products Act requires quality marking, blend ratio and country of origin; environmental claims fall under the Act on Fair Labelling and Advertising; sizing follows KS K 0051, so one block often needs recalculating for Korea.
  • China: GB/T 18401 covers formaldehyde, pH, odour and decomposable aromatic amines, instructions follow GB/T 5296.4, and grammage and calliper are stated in g/m² and mm - a 0.3 mm versus 0.6 mm board feels very different in the hand.

6. What to Send With an Enquiry

This type of enquiry usually takes three rounds for one reason: the chemistry was never stated, so the supplier quotes a price without certification first. Give these six items up front and the first reply can already be comparable.

  • Name the ink system: water-based, soy or UV, and whether mineral-oil-free is required. That decides whether a separately registered ink has to be sourced.
  • Whether the hang tag touches skin and whether it ships with childrenswear. Those two answers push the test class from STANDARD 100 class II to class I.
  • The certificate you need and who must hold it: the ink maker's, or a STANDARD 100 on our finished trim. Validity and renewal cycles differ.
  • Target markets and their labelling duties - Triman with info-tri for France, country of origin and blend ratio for Japan. Changing a fixed label layout means a new plate.
  • Grammage and calliper: hang tags commonly sit at 350 to 450 g/m² and 0.4 to 0.6 mm, and the varnish system affects migration results, so state it too.
  • Quantity, lead time and whether split shipments are acceptable. Certified inks carry minimum order quantities, so small runs usually wait for a slot.
