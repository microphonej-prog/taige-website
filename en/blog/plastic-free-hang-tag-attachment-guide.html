Plastic-Free Hang Tag Attachment: Five Ways to Replace the Plastic Barb

By TAGE Packaging &nbsp;·&nbsp; Updated November 24, 2026

A 400 g/m² recycled board tag with a 25 mm nylon barb: the plastic part weighs under half a gram, less than 3% of the tag, and yet it decides two things — whether the tag can be recycled with paper, and whether the brand can write plastic-free packaging on its own site. Buyers used to ask only whether the board was FSC certified. Now they turn the tag over and look at the barb.

This guide puts five replacements on one table — pull-out force, hole size, cost, recyclability and production fit — and then covers the numbers a specification needs, where tagging guns and retail floors break the plan, and how far the words plastic-free can go in the EU, France, Spain, Japan and Korea.

1. What the Barb Actually Does

A standard barb is 25 mm long with a T-end of roughly 5 × 10 mm; it passes through a 2 mm hole in the tag and anchors into the care label or side seam of the garment. Its job is to hold a 3–5 g tag, survive a rail and a hanging truck, and be applied by a tagging gun at 1,200–2,000 pieces an hour. It fails in only three ways: the T-end slips out of the fabric, the hole tears, or the gun widens the hole into something that grows later.

2. Five Replacements, Ranked by Effort

The ranking is not by which option is greenest but by how much the retail floor has to change. A solution that leaves the store process untouched always lands before one that needs store cooperation.

Option Hold and durability Recyclability When to use
Cotton twine, knotted Strong, but manual Same stream as paper Premium, low volume, gift boxes
Paper toggle Medium, needs a 3 mm hole Same stream as paper Volume paper tags, plastic-free claims
Recycled PP barb High, identical to today Still needs separation If retail must not change
Paper-covered wire High, stiff feel Must be cut and separated Thick tags, denim, outerwear
Retail-applied sticker Low Depends on the liner Only with store control

3. The Four Numbers a Specification Needs

  • Hole diameter: 2 mm for a barb, 3 mm for a paper toggle. Too small and the toggle will not enter; too large and the tag hangs crooked. Keep the hole centre at least 6 mm from the edge.
  • Board grammage: below 350 g/m² a tag with a paper toggle tends to curl and distort at the hole within a week; from 400 g/m² upward it survives volume handling.
  • Pull-out force: there is no single international standard, so define your own acceptance test: pull at 300 mm/min with a force gauge and record the value in N. Suggest ≥10 N for twine and paper toggles, ≥15 N for barbs.
  • Gun fit: changing the attachment means changing the needle and the tension setting. Paper toggles usually cannot be applied by gun at all and need manual or semi-automatic threading, which is what moves the unit price.

4. Plastic-Free Is a Legal Statement, Not a Vibe

Plastic-free sounds safe and is in fact one of the three easiest claims to be judged as exaggerated, because it has to be true of the whole item — tag, cord, barb, outer bag and even the sticker. A tag can honestly be called a plastic-free tag while the outer packaging is PE; the same words become false the moment they move to the product page as plastic-free packaging.

  • EU: PPWR (EU) 2025/40 requires packaging to be designed for recycling, with binding requirements arriving by 2030. Small plastic parts are rejects in the paper stream, and that has to be handled at design stage.
  • France: the Triman mark and sorting instructions introduced by the AGEC law (loi n° 2020-105) are now routine on apparel packaging, and a recyclable claim must match the real sorting stream or it becomes a misleading commercial practice.
  • Spain: Ley 7/2022 and the packaging decree require the material and the sorting route to be stated. Japan: the plastic resource circulation act runs alongside the Act against Unjustifiable Premiums and Misleading Representations, so a claim has to be provable. Korea: the recycling marking under the Act on the Promotion of Saving and Recycling of Resources applies together with the Act on Fair Labelling and Advertising.

5. Where It Breaks on the Floor

  • The gun needle does not fit a paper toggle: needles are designed around barbs, so a paper toggle falls back to manual or semi-automatic threading and throughput drops from 1,500 to 300–500 pieces an hour. Both price and lead time have to be recalculated.
  • Stacked tags: when two tags share one hole, a paper toggle spreads its grip and the outer tag slips. Either give each tag its own hole or move to a cotton cord.
  • Childrenswear: any detachable toggle counts as a small part and will be audited as one. For ages under six, prefer a knotted cotton cord and state non-detachable in the spec.
The decision order is simple: first ask whether retail can keep its process unchanged — if yes, switch to recycled barbs. If the process must stay and the claim is still required, move to twine or paper toggles and budget 20–40% more labour at tagging.

6. Five Steps from Sample to Bulk

  • Choose the attachment before the tag size: hole diameter and position constrain the usable format, so settle 2 mm or 3 mm first and draw the dieline after.
  • Pull-test the sample and record the number: five tags from the same board lot, pulled at 300 mm/min, with the lowest value written into the spec.
  • Run 50 pieces through the real tagging gun: needle type, tension and stacking either work or they do not, and this step catches most bulk problems early.
  • One week on a retail rail: whether the tag hangs straight and whether shoppers pull the toggle off shows up within a week, more reliably than any lab number.
  • Write the numbers back into the spec and the BOM: hole in mm, board in g/m², pull-out in N and the attachment named. Otherwise the next order quietly returns to barbs.
