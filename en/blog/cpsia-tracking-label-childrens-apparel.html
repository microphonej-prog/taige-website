CPSIA Tracking Labels: What Children's Apparel Has to Print

By TAGE Packaging &nbsp;·&nbsp; Updated November 11, 2026

A tracking label is not a branding decision. On children's apparel sold in the United States it is the permanent mark that lets a recall reach one production run instead of a whole season, and CPSIA section 103 requires it to be attached to the product itself. We print thousands of these labels a month, and the same four data points fail in the same order. Here is what has to appear, where it has to sit, and how to fold it into the care label so it costs almost nothing extra.

The four data points CPSIA section 103 asks for

The requirement is deliberately plain: enough information to trace a garment back to a specific batch, not a second marketing block. Four elements are expected — who made it, where, when, and which run. Each one only works if it is a string somebody outside the factory can look up.

  • Who made it — the manufacturer or the private-label owner responsible for the product, printed as registered. A trade name only counts if it identifies one legal entity.
  • Where — city (or state or province) plus country, for example Dongguan, Guangdong, China. A factory code on its own does not satisfy it, because nobody outside the supply chain can look it up.
  • When — month and year at minimum. A week code is better, because the window in a recall is usually days, not months, and a month-only label forces the brand to pull the whole month.
  • Which run — lot, batch or purchase-order number, and it must be the same string that appears on the dye-lot record and on the trim delivery note, or the trace stops at the label.

Print all four in one block. A 20 × 40 mm label at 8 pt carries them with a 2 mm margin, which is the layout we specify when a brand has not decided yet. Anything narrower and the batch line either wraps or drops a character at the edge.

It has to be sewn in, not hung on

The most common mistake we see is a hang tag carrying the tracking data and nothing on the garment itself. A hang tag is removed at retail or at the first wash, so it fails the permanence test outright. The tracking label belongs in a side seam or at the back neck, in the same seam as the care label and with the same thread, so it inherits the durability already proven on that seam.

Attachment point Typical material What goes wrong
Side seam, 5 cm above the hem 100 % polyester taffeta, 15 mm Rubbed by belt loops and bag straps; the print wears out before the garment does
Back neck, under the main label Cotton tape, 15 mm Itch complaints; parents cut it out and the trace disappears with it
Inside the waistband (bottoms) Satin, 20 mm Hidden by the lining on lined styles; the information only exists on paper
Sewn to the care-label tail Taffeta, 20 mm Folded over inside the seam; the second page can never be read

Combine it with the care label instead of adding another one

A separate tracking label costs a second seam, a second inspection point and roughly a third more label material. We normally print the tracking block on the reverse of the care label: fibre composition and care symbols on the face, tracking data on the back. One attachment, one stitch line, both obligations met — and one item fewer to lose in the wash.

Legibility we hold to: 8 pt for the data lines, 6 pt only if the label is 25 mm wide or more, and never white type on a mid-tone ground. Thermal transfer thins out after ten wash cycles at 40 °C, and the block stops being readable exactly when a recall needs it.

Where the artwork usually fails

  • Batch codes nobody can decode. B-04 on the label means nothing in a recall unless the same string sits on the purchase order and the packing list.
  • The country spelled three ways in one order: China, CN, P.R.C. Pick one and freeze it in the specification sheet.
  • Months written as two digits. 11 reads as November in the US and as a day number in most of Europe and Asia; write NOV 26 and the ambiguity disappears.
  • Batch data left to the factory when the brand owns the batch logic. If the brand assigns lots, the brand prints them; a factory cannot invent numbering that matches your ERP.

What EU, Japanese and Brazilian buyers ask for instead

The US system is the strictest on batch traceability, but other markets want different facts in the same seam. Under the EU General Product Safety Regulation (GPSR, EU 2023/988) a product must identify the manufacturer and, where applicable, an economic operator established in the EU, with a postal address rather than a factory code. Fibre composition remains governed by (EU) No 1007/2011 and care symbols by EN ISO 3758. In Japan the Household Goods Quality Labelling Act requires fibre composition, care marking and country of origin in Japanese, with symbols to JIS L 0001. In Brazil the Consumer Defence Code (Lei 8.078/1990) requires clear information in Portuguese plus the importer or responsible party, and children's items additionally fall under the INMETRO conformity rules for children's products.

The practical consequence is label size. A label carrying only US tracking data fits in 20 × 40 mm. Add a GPSR responsible-person block and Japanese origin marking and the same label needs 25 × 60 mm, or two pages. Decide the destination markets before the label is ordered, not after the first carton is refused.

How to put it on a purchase order

  • Give the text block line by line, in capitals, with the character count per line. A screenshot approves a look, not a specification.
  • State the attachment point, seam allowance and stitch density; 8 to 10 stitches per inch is what we run on these labels.
  • Sign a physical sample. Proofing on screen approves the typeface; only a washed sample proves the print survives.
  • Ask for three pre-production samples washed five times at 40 °C. That single test catches soft thread, thin transfer print and the wrong tape in one go.
