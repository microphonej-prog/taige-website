Paper Grain Direction for Hang Tags: Why Tags Curl, Crack and Tear

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 20, 2026

Three of the most common reasons a hang tag shipment comes back from a brand are not colour: the stack has curled, the fold line has cracked open to the white core, and the punched hole tears out as soon as the string is pulled. The reflex is to add weight, moving from 350 g/m² to 400 g/m². The money is spent and the problem stays, because none of the three symptoms is mainly about thickness. They come from grain direction: which way the fibres lie decides which way the tag curls in humid air, whether the fold breaks, and how much pull the hole survives.

1. Grain Is Set at the Paper Mill, Not at the Printer

On the paper machine the pulp flows in the direction of the wire, so most fibres end up parallel to it. That is the machine direction (MD); the perpendicular one is the cross direction (CD). The sheet is not symmetric between the two: tensile strength is higher and stretch lower along MD, and the reverse across it. Hang tags are usually made from coated grey-back board, white card or kraft board in the 300–400 g/m² range, roughly 0.45–0.60 mm thick, and that window is exactly where grain matters most. Below 250 g/m² the difference fades; above 450 g/m² the fold tends to fail through the full calliper instead.

Only one thing is actually controllable: specify grain on the sheeted order. In a roll the mill decides, and sheeting cannot change it. In sheets you can require long grain (parallel to the long edge) or short grain. That one line on a purchase order costs nothing; added after tooling is cut, it costs a new die.

2. Three Symptoms, One Mechanical Cause

Symptom Mechanics Spec fix
Stack curls on one edge CD absorbs moisture and swells more than MD; asymmetric coating on the two faces amplifies it Long grain; ship flat in shrink film with desiccant
White core splits at the fold When the fold runs across the grain the fibres snap instead of bending, and the coating cracks with them Run the fold parallel to the grain; if impossible, score before folding, corner radius 3 mm
Punched hole tears out The tear propagates along the grain, and stress concentrates when the hole sits close to the edge Keep at least 3 mm from edge, hole 3–5 mm; add an eyelet when pull is high

3. Three Tests You Can Run Without Instruments

On receiving sheets or finished tags, confirm grain before discussing anything else. None of these needs equipment, each takes about ten minutes on the warehouse floor, and they agree with each other:

  • Tear test: tear one strip each way. Along the grain it runs straight with little resistance; across it, the edge is ragged and drifts to one side.
  • Bend test: fold the sheet without scoring. The direction that springs back fastest and shows a whitened crease is across the grain.
  • Hole test: punch a 4 mm hole near the edge and hang 2 kg from it for five seconds. If the hole sits across the grain, the tear runs along the fibres.

Curl only means something if the conditions are stated; otherwise the number is noise. Moisture content follows the room, so a batch that sits flat in a dry warehouse starts to roll in an 80% RH container or a humid port. The disciplined method: condition the sample four hours at 23 °C and 50% relative humidity (the ISO 187 standard atmosphere for paper testing), then measure the maximum lift over a 100 mm span. Without a climate chamber, leave the samples overnight in one room and record the actual temperature and humidity in the report.

4. Writing Grain Into the Spec: Three Lines Are Enough

Spec wording you can paste into a purchase order: (1) sheeted stock, grain parallel to the 1,000 mm edge of a 700 × 1,000 mm sheet (long grain); (2) fold lines parallel to grain, otherwise score to 0.15 mm depth first, corner radius 3 mm; (3) hanging hole 4 mm diameter, centre at least 3 mm from the trimmed edge, tear strength 80 N or more. Three lines cover curl, fold cracks and hole tear for less than a paper upgrade.

There is a second decision at the imposition stage: which way the die runs. If all fold lines on an 8-up or 12-up layout can be kept parallel to the grain, waste drops noticeably. Rotating some units 90° to save paper turns those units into a permanent source of cracked folds, and the paper saved rarely covers a single customer rejection. Shaped tags, rounded corners and bookmark-style flaps need this settled even earlier, because die-cut tearing risk across the grain is already higher.

5. Incoming Checks: Three Numbers to Agree On

Check Method Pass limit
Curl 4 h at 23 °C, 50% RH, measured over 100 mm 3 mm or less
Fold line 180° fold, five cycles, inspect coating No white core, no fibre lift
Hole strength 2 kg static load for 5 s Hole holds shape, no tear

Put those three limits in the inspection sheet instead of writing "good appearance". Sample five tags per lot and take the worst of three readings per check: an average hides the one tag that is badly curled, and that is the one the customer photographs. One more constraint: mandatory information on the tag (composition, origin, responsible party, barcode) must not be squeezed or covered because of a paper problem. Taiwan retail goods must carry the product name and main composition in Chinese under the Commodity Labeling Act, and those fields are exactly what gets pushed to the edge when a die is re-laid to save paper.

6. When Grain Does Not Matter

Not every tag justifies paying for grain control. The effect is limited when the finished size is under 40 × 90 mm with no fold; on single-sided thin tags at 250 g/m² or below, where stiffness and stress are both low; on self-adhesive labels, where the adhesive and liner restrain movement; and on small rigid tags from heavy board, where lift over 100 mm is usually far below 3 mm. Always specify it in three cases: tags with a centre fold or bookmark flap, tags whose hole sits near an edge or carries real hanging weight, and any shipment to a high-humidity region (Southeast Asia, Central and South America, sea freight over 30 days).
