Odour Control for Apparel Trims and Packaging: From Tracing the Source to Preventing It

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 11, 2026

Odour in trims and packaging is the hardest complaint to argue about in the apparel supply chain — and the one most likely to end in a return. The garment itself is fine, yet the customer opens the box and says it smells. The source is rarely the fabric: it is usually hang tag ink, the laminating adhesive in a poly bag, dyeing and finishing auxiliaries on woven labels and care labels, or the varnish on a wooden tag. A sea container at forty to fifty degrees, sealed for two or three weeks, turns a faint solvent residue into a pungent smell; babywear and next-to-skin categories are the most sensitive, and once a batch is rejected the cost of rework and re-packing dwarfs the material saving that caused it. This guide covers the eight most common sources, how to define an odour grade, prevention at the source, pre-shipment self-checks and what to do after a complaint.

1. Why Odour Turns into a Return

  • Too subjective: the same batch is unbearable in the store and acceptable to the buyer; the argument usually comes down to never agreeing on a scale, a test environment or who makes the call
  • Sealed and hot amplifies it: containers, shrink-wrapped packs and hot warehouses concentrate residual solvents and low-molecular volatiles, so goods smell far stronger on arrival than at inspection
  • It also fails on compliance: China's GB 18401 lists odour among its basic safety requirements, with tighter expectations for infant products, and export customers increasingly write odour into their own goods-in standard
  • Costly to fix: unpacking a whole batch to air out, re-packing and missing a launch window dwarf the extra spend on low-odour materials

3. How to Judge: Odour Grade and Test Items

  • Agree the scale first: a common industry approach grades odour from 1 to 6, with 1 odourless and 6 strong, though some customers use 1 to 5; the scale matters less than using the same one for every batch across the year
  • Spell out five things: whether the sample is sealed (commonly 24 hours), at what temperature, how many assessors, which layer of packaging is sniffed, and whether the verdict is an average or the worst score — air inside the bag usually smells stronger than the product surface
  • Instrumental evidence: headspace GC/MS identifies which solvents remain, total volatile organic compounds give the quantity, and formaldehyde is tested separately to the relevant standard; the numbers should back up the nose
  • Blind-test against the customer's approved sample: sniff the approved reference beside the batch in question; it tracks the customer's actual perception far better than a number alone and settles arguments faster
  • Write it into the spec: odour grade, method, sample quantity and assessor belong in the specification and the order; every later batch is then checked the same way, and complaints have a basis for discussion

4. Prevention at the Source: Seven Practical Moves

  • Favour water-based or low-odour inks and adhesives: verify UV cure, since under-cured UV is a common source of pungency, especially on large solid areas and heavy ink laydown
  • Allow curing and breathing time: do not carton immediately after laminating, coating or foiling; one night of ventilated resting is the cheapest insurance available, so squeeze another step when time is short
  • Cut auxiliary load: add softeners and sizing only as needed and record the batch; less auxiliary means less odour risk and a lighter washing burden
  • Keep solvent varnish off wooden tags: use wax or water-based finishing and control moisture content; once a wooden tag gets damp, the musty note is very hard to remove
  • Ventilate where you can: punch or leave vents in bags when rules allow instead of sealing everything airtight, and size the barrier paper and desiccant to the destination season
  • Do not let stock bake in a sealed hot warehouse: rotate and air stock regularly in peak and rainy seasons, stand rolls and bags with a gap, and keep stacks off walls and roof panels
  • Trial new materials in small batches: when changing ink, adhesive or supplier, seal a sample with the finished garment overnight and assess before committing — far cheaper than discovering it after shipping

5. What to Check Before and After Shipping

  • Do not sample straight off the line: freshly printed or laminated goods smell strongest; sample after the resting time agreed in the order or you will fail good batches
  • Sniff before shipping: seal one finished item in a bag, open and assess after 24 hours, and record batch, assessor and verdict so there is a traceable note
  • Time the lab work: smell first to isolate the component, then test it; ordering a full panel straight away is expensive and hard to interpret
  • Keep sealed references: retain one finished item and its packaging material per batch so a complaint can be compared at once and traced to raw material or storage

6. What to Do Once a Complaint Arrives

  • Grade it before deciding: mild can be aired, moderate needs unpacking and re-packing, and clearly pungent means fixing the process before shipping — never ship hoping the customer will not notice
  • Airing and rework need method: open the cartons to ventilate, change to lower-odour packaging, add desiccant and barrier paper; never mask with fragrance, since the blend is harder to judge and customers spot it quickly
  • Isolate and trace in parallel: segregate and label the suspect batch, then work the batch number back to the component, the supplier and the machine so the same fault cannot repeat
  • Get ahead of the customer: present the scale and method you used, the corrective action and the re-test plan; being first is more controllable than being caught at incoming inspection and protects the order

7. Four Sentences to Put in the Purchasing File

  1. Odour is graded on an X-point scale, assessed by... (seal time, temperature, number of assessors, where the air is taken) — define it before arguing pass or fail
  2. No solvent-based laminating adhesive, or at least X hours of resting after lamination before cartoning — a process instruction in the order beats a verbal reminder
  3. Supply the auxiliary, ink and adhesive list for the batch with the goods — it is what lets you trace back layer by layer, and where most odour mysteries stall
  4. Odour complaints are re-tested against the retained sample and corrected according to the agreed plan — settle retention and responsibility in advance so both sides have a route out when it happens
