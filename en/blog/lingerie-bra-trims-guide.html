Lingerie and Bra Trims Guide: Hooks, Strap Elastic and Underwire Casing

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 16, 2026

The trims used on underwear and bras are the smallest components in a garment and the most demanding: hook-and-eye tape, strap elastic, underwire casing, tagless prints. Half a millimetre out, or a little recovery lost after a few washes, and the whole wearing experience changes. This guide breaks lingerie trim selection and acceptance into points you can put straight into an enquiry.

It works through four groups — closures, elastics, structural parts and labelling — and closes with a sampling and acceptance checklist you can use as a development reference.

1. Four Ways Lingerie Trims Differ from Ready-to-Wear

  • Fabric is very thin and highly elastic: trim thickness and weight are magnified — a fraction thicker shows through, a fraction heavier leaves marks, most visibly on straps and the underband.
  • Harsher environment: perspiration, frequent washing and chlorine or fabric softener stress elastics and printed markings far more than on outerwear.
  • Small parts, tight tolerances: hook spacing, strap width and underband length are toleranced in millimetres, so the spec sheet has to state measuring points and limits.
  • Consumable by nature: underwear is replaced far more often than outerwear, so trim durability drives repeat purchase — an option that goes slack in five washes is not cheaper.

2. Choosing the Closure: Hook-and-Eye Tape, Front Closures and Bonded Seams

ClosureTypical stylesMost common failure
Hook-and-eye tape (2–3 rows)Standard bras, shaping wearHook spacing tolerance, needles damaging hooks, hooks distorting after washing
Front closure (centre hook-and-eye)Easy-on, maternity and post-surgeryLoad is concentrated so a reinforcement panel is needed; tolerances are tighter and closing feel must be kept as a sample
Bonded / ultrasonicSeamless and T-shirt brasBond strength depends heavily on fabric and process — prove it by washing before setting a quantity

Extended and multi-row hook tapes are common on larger sizes and adjustable-fit ranges. Watch the elastic difference against the main fabric: when the two do not shrink together, rippling appears beside the tape after washing.

3. Elastics: Straps, Underband and Binding

  • Straps: width and thickness decide shoulder pressure, and adjuster grip has to be measured after repeated stretching; on detachable straps check hook interchangeability and anti-slip.
  • Underband elastic: recovery and permanent deformation are the key figures; ripples after washing usually mean poor recovery, and a light and a heavy style cannot share one spec.
  • Binding tape: must match the fabric in thickness and elongation — too thick crushes the edge, too thin curls; the added thickness counts toward the overall wearing thickness.
  • Test colour fastness separately: judge elastics on perspiration and chlorinated-water fastness, not only on washing; rule out staining from a dark underband onto a light outer layer at development stage.

4. Structural Parts: Underwire Casing, Boning and Padding

  • Underwire casing: the casing must fully enclose the wire ends with protection at the tips so they cannot pierce through, and stitching should be kept away from the fabric edge to reduce abrasion.
  • Boning: choose trimmable types with finished tips and test the finished garment for piercing and bending; on shaping wear the spacing between bones decides whether the edge rolls.
  • Padding and moulded cups: confirm fit, recovery and post-wash shape together; on removable pads the opening position and fixing decide whether they shift in the wash.
  • Metal parts: confirm plating and nickel release on hooks, adjusters and decorative hardware against the target market, since dark platings dull quickly under perspiration.

5. Labels and Marking: Invisible, Legible, and Survives Washing

  • Prefer tagless printing: use heat transfer or printed marking at centre back to reduce friction; where a woven label is essential, choose light satin, a hot-cut edge and edge-enclosing stitching.
  • Show two size systems: EU and US side by side (75B / 34B) clearly reduces enquiries, and placing the size next to the care symbols improves readability.
  • Get the composition right: underwear is usually a multi-fibre blend and the elastane share must match the tested result; a hang tag can carry marketing, but statutory information still belongs on the care label.
  • Verify after washing: test print adhesion with fabric softener in the wash, and on dark prints check whether they transfer onto light fabric.

6. Sampling and Acceptance Checklist

  • Wash cycles: run repeated cycles to the target market's care method and check hooks, recovery, print adhesion and colour fastness, with at least one round including softener and one with chlorine.
  • Measure every item on the list: hook spacing, strap width, underband length and casing width are measured at the points and limits written in the spec — never judged by feel on a single piece from the batch.
  • Real try-on: have at least two users with different body types test two size ranges, and record strap marks, underwire tip feel and whether the hook tape digs into the back.
  • Keep samples and control changes: retain a signed sample with photos and a batch reference; changing the elastic supplier, the hook brand or the thread all affect performance and need re-validation.
💡 Practical tip: complaints on lingerie trims cluster in two places — hooks distorting after five washes and straps losing recovery. Washing one sample five times during development (once with softener, once with chlorine) costs almost nothing and surfaces about nine out of ten of them in advance.
