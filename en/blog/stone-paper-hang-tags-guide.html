Stone Paper Hang Tags: Choosing and Printing Waterproof Mineral Paper

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 8, 2026

Stone paper hang tags — also called mineral paper — are made from calcium carbonate bonded with a small share of resin. No wood pulp, no water in production, and the sheet is naturally waterproof, tear-resistant and oil-resistant, which is why it turns up on hang tags and price tickets for outdoor, sports, swimwear and workwear. Its physical data, printing behaviour and recycling classification differ from coated paper and PP synthetic paper: much higher density, easy cracking along a fold, low-temperature short-dwell foil stamping, and a material that does not always belong in the paper recycling bin. This guide covers composition and key figures, a three-way comparison, what printing and finishing can and cannot do, hole punching and cord fitting, where it fits, and how to talk about the sustainability story without overclaiming.

1. What Stone Paper Is: Composition and Key Figures

Keep one sentence in mind: it behaves more like a very thin plastic sheet than like paper. Weight, calliper, stiffness and creasing therefore follow a different set of conventions.

Item Usual value What it means for a tag
Composition About 70–80 % calcium carbonate, 20–30 % resin No wood pulp; stiffer, cooler, slightly chalky feel
Density About 1.2–1.5 g/cm³ Heavier than paper at the same size and calliper; grammage does not convert like paper
Water resistance Soaking does not break, pulp or wrinkle it Suits outdoor and wet contexts, though cut edges still wick water
Tear resistance Clearly higher than paper at the same calliper Holes resist cord pull-out; suits heavier tags or tags with hardware
Folding and creasing Creases tend to crack and whiten Crease first, then fold; never fold flat under a platen
Heat tolerance Softens and distorts locally at high temperature Foil at low temperature and short dwell; keep hot tools off the sheet

2. Stone Paper, Coated Paper or PP Synthetic Paper

Criterion Stone paper Coated paper PP synthetic paper
Water resistance Excellent, short soaking is fine Poor: wrinkles and softens when wet Excellent
Tear resistance Good Average; edges notch easily Very good
Four-colour fidelity Good but needs dedicated inks and drying settings Best; the finest tonal gradation Fair; large dark areas mottle
Folding and creasing Crease first; the fold may whiten Easy; best for folded booklets Needs creasing; whitening is more obvious
Recycling route Varies by market; often treated as composite Paper stream, the most established Plastic stream, subject to local facilities
Typical use Outdoor, swim, workwear tags and waterproof tickets Standard brand tags, the widest finishing range Cards and labels needing high water resistance

3. Printing and Finishing: What Works, What to Avoid

  • Digital printing: the easiest route for short runs; first confirm the press feeds the sheet reliably and the fusing temperature does not soften it.
  • Offset: possible, but control fountain solution, choose the right ink and verify drying and adhesion; four-colour fidelity is slightly duller than on paper.
  • UV inkjet: strong adhesion; good for heavy local ink, white underlay and shaped pieces.
  • Foil stamping: possible with low temperature, short dwell and light pressure; too much pressure leaves shiny marks and crushed edges.
  • Embossing and texture: shallow relief works; deep relief whitens or cracks the reverse, so round the die edges.
  • Lamination: limited value since the sheet is already waterproof; a matt film for touch can lift at the corners.
  • Folded tags: crease before folding; for multi-page booklets, switch to paper or PP synthetic.

4. Holes, Cords and Eyelets: Machining Notes

  • Keep holes at least 6–8 mm from the edge; round holes beat square ones, whose corners start tears.
  • Metal eyelets can be pressed in, but keep the tooling cool to avoid local distortion; the swivel hooks common on wood tags do not suit this sheet.
  • Use flat cotton or polyester cord matched to the hole diameter; thin cord cuts the hole rim like a wire saw.
  • When the tag is heavy or carries hardware or stacked cards, load concentrates at the hole: add an eyelet and run a hang test.

5. Where Stone Paper Fits, and Where It Does Not

  • Fits: tags and price cards for outdoor and ski, swim and beachwear, workwear and cycling, tools and hardware; waterproof tickets and cards; labels that must survive humid retail environments without distorting.
  • Does not fit: multi-page folded booklets; brands whose core claim is paper-stream recyclability; premium tags needing photo-grade gradients and fine detail; products repeatedly flexed or exposed to heat.
  • A common conversion error: do not carry paper grammage across. Specify thickness in microns and ask the supplier for samples so you can feel stiffness and hand.

6. Sustainability Story and Compliance: Do Not Overclaim

  • No wood pulp and minimal process water is a claim that holds up; the resin share, however, means most markets treat it as a composite or plastic.
  • Avoid 100 percent recyclable or biodegradable; naming the recycling stream and the country where it applies is far safer than adjectives.
  • Mineral paper is generally outside the FSC system; if certification documents are required, say so early and do not promise the FSC mark.
  • For the EU, watch packaging and packaging waste rules on recyclability and material marking; agree the wording on the tag with the buyer first.

7. Quotation and Acceptance Checklist

  • Finished size and shape, plus die-cut forms and rounded corners if needed
  • Thickness in microns, stating whether you want stiffness or some flexibility
  • Printing method, number of colours, foil stamping and whether white underlay is needed
  • Hole position and diameter, cord material and length, eyelet reinforcement or not
  • Quantity, individual packing or retail header card, and destination market to set compliant wording
  • Acceptance criteria: size and thickness tolerance, colour deviation, rate of whitened or cracked folds, foil adhesion, hole strength sampling
