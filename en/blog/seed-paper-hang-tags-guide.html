A Seed Paper Hang Tag Gets Planted, Not Thrown Away

By TAGE Packaging · Updated November 27, 2026

Seed paper hang tags turn a disposable label into a second product: the buyer soaks the card, plants it, and gets basil or wildflowers three weeks later. For a brand that is a memorable unboxing moment. For a trims buyer it is a different specification problem, because a plantable tag has to survive printing, punching, stringing, freight and a warehouse without germinating early, and every claim printed on it has to hold up. This guide covers how the sheet is made, what it tolerates, which standards actually apply, and what we ask before quoting a custom run.

1. What Seed Paper Actually Is

The seeds are not glued on. They are mixed into the pulp while the sheet is being formed, so the fibres close around them and the seed sits inside the paper once it dries. The base is usually recycled post-consumer pulp, sometimes cotton rag or a blend. Hang tags sit at 200–300 g/m²: below 180 g/m² the sheet cracks at the punched hole, above 350 g/m² it behaves like board and the seeds are buried too deep for reliable germination. Seed load is typically 1–3 % of sheet weight; more seed means a rougher surface and more pinholes in print.

  • Single species (basil, marigold, tomato) lets you write a precise claim. A wildflower mix looks better but its species list changes between lots, which makes it the highest-risk SKU for cross-border plant health checks
  • Ask for the germination rate of the seed lot and the date the sheet was made. Those two numbers decide the outcome more than the grammage does
  • Stored dry, dark and below 25 °C, germination holds for roughly 12–18 months. A container crossing the tropics in August is not where seed paper should spend three weeks

2. What Printing Does to a Living Sheet

The sheet contains living seed, which fears heat, pressure and water. That single fact removes most of the finishes on a default spec sheet. We print seed paper with water-based or soy-based ink, letterpress or screen, at low pressure. The sheet is uncoated, so ink sits on the fibre and rubs more easily than on coated art card; a matte, slightly rough surface is not a compromise, it is the only honest look this material has.

  • Works: water-based or soy ink, letterpress, screen at low pressure, die cutting with at least 8 mm clearance from seed clusters, hand punching
  • Does not work: UV varnish or aqueous coating (seals the sheet so the seed cannot take up water), hot foil stamping (contact above 120 °C kills the embryo at that spot), laser cutting (charring), thermal transfer and toner digital (fuser heat)
  • Emboss and deboss only with care: fine on artwork edges, destructive where seeds cluster
  • Use cotton or paper twine; a plastic barb cancels the point of the sheet. If you need a QR code, put it on the reverse at 12 mm or larger: the visible fibre texture eats scanner tolerance

3. Which Claims Actually Apply

EN 13432 is the EU harmonised standard for packaging recoverable through composting. It tests a packaging item for disintegration (90 % within 12 weeks) and biodegradation (90 % within six months) under industrial conditions, plus heavy-metal and ecotoxicity limits. A hang tag with living seeds is not packaging in that sense and will not pass EN 13432 as a tag: the seeds are the whole point. So do not print “compostable to EN 13432” on a seed tag. Keep compostability claims for the poly bag or mailer, where the standard belongs.

  • Safe: “Plantable paper. Contains basil seed. Soak, cover with 5 mm of soil, keep moist.” That is a usage instruction, not an environmental certification
  • Safe: FSC Mix or FSC Recycled, but only if the printer holds an FSC chain-of-custody certificate and the job is registered under it
  • Avoid: “100 % biodegradable”, because the tag also carries string, ink and seed, and the string usually is not; and bare “eco-friendly”, a generic term banned in the EU from 27 September 2026
  • With care: “recycled paper” needs the post-consumer share stated separately from pre-consumer mill waste. The two are not interchangeable percentages

4. Seeds Cross Borders, and That Is a Real Constraint

Seeds are regulated plant material. In the EU, Regulation (EU) 2016/2031 on protective measures against pests of plants governs how seed and other plant material may enter the Union, and species listed under Regulation (EU) 1143/2014 on invasive alien species may not be moved at all. Australia, New Zealand, the United States and Japan each inspect or prohibit seed-bearing paper. A tag that is legal in one market can be held in another, and a wildflower mix is the riskiest SKU because its species list changes between lots. Before you print a species name, have your customs broker or forwarder confirm the import status, and put the species and lot number on the commercial invoice.

5. Seed Paper Against the Alternatives

Material Weight and print Claim that survives
Seed paper (recycled pulp + seed) 200–300 g/m²; water-based ink, letterpress; no coating “Plantable, contains X seed”; FSC Mix only with CoC
FSC Recycled kraft board 300–350 g/m²; offset, foil and emboss all fine “FSC Recycled 100 %, recyclable in paper stream” — the least risky combination
Cotton rag board 350–600 g/m²; letterpress and foil hold well, richest hand feel “Cotton fibre, no plastic”; buyers usually keep it, so recycling is not the argument
Stone paper (calcium carbonate + HDPE) 120–200 g/m²; digital print, tear resistant, water resistant Never call it “recyclable paper”: most mills reject it because of the resin content

On cost, seed paper is not a slightly dearer paper but a different material class: sheets are made to order, so minimums usually start at 3,000–5,000 tags per design; seed is priced by weight and moves with the species; summer shipments need air freight or insulated cartons; and at low volumes the punching and stringing are done by hand. It earns its place in three situations: a new brand's first drop, a limited collaboration, and press kits that need a photograph. For a season-long basic tag bought by the hundred thousand, FSC recycled kraft board costs less and passes a client's compliance questionnaire more easily.

6. What We Ask Before Quoting

  • Size and grammage: finished size in cm plus target g/m², a 4–5 mm punched hole, and at least 8 mm from hole centre to the sheet edge
  • Species and lot: single species or mix, and whether the destination country admits that species at all
  • Print method and artwork: water-based ink has a narrower gamut than offset, and large dark solids mottle, so a physical proof comes first
  • Quantity and lead time: 3,000 and 30,000 sit in different sheet-making slots, and peak season needs 25–35 days
  • Companion labels: should the care label carry the planting instruction too? We set the same sentence in six languages so tag and care label cannot contradict each other
  • Sampling: run a 50–100 piece germination test and plant one yourself. It tells you more than any certificate on the day
One line to keep: the claim follows the material. On seed paper, say plantable, not biodegradable. The second word makes you responsible for the string, the ink and the seed at once.
