Apparel Packaging Transit Testing: Drop, Stacking, Vibration and Humidity

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 24, 2026

Whether export packaging is strong enough cannot be judged by feel. On the same international lane and the same garment, different packaging formats can differ several times over in damage rate: burst bag seals, torn paper-bag handles, collapsed cartons, scuffed hang tags. Transit testing exists to quantify that risk before the goods ship — and it needs no expensive equipment, since most checks can be run in-house at the sampling stage.

This article covers the four basic test families (drop, stacking, vibration and temperature-humidity), how to set parameters for apparel, the five most common failure modes with countermeasures, a five-step in-house test anyone can run, and a pre-shipment checklist.

1. Why It Pays Off: Damage Costs Far More Than Packaging

Packaging is usually a tiny share of unit retail price, while damage costs arrive as a package: total loss of the goods, return or replacement freight, service hours — and on cross-border orders the added time of customs and a second delivery leg. Building packaging to just-strong-enough converts unpredictable after-sales cost into a one-off packaging spend.

  • Claims outrun the packaging premium: the payout and freight for a single damaged order usually exceed upgrading the whole run
  • Cross-border returns rarely come back: return duty and freight often exceed the value of the goods, so the real outcome is a discount or abandonment
  • Platforms and retailers measure arrivals: damage rates feed straight into ratings, listing eligibility and future order allocation
  • Peak season and long lanes are harder: more sorting volume and more transfers make one packaging spec perform worse in peak, so set standards per lane
  • A failed carton takes the contents with it: collapse, water ingress and crushing usually happen together, and the loss is a whole box, not one piece

2. The Four Test Families: Drop, Stacking, Vibration, Climate

Third-party labs usually work to transport-packaging methods such as the ISTA series or ASTM D4169, and e-commerce channels add their own requirements for ships-in-own-container shipments. The names differ, but the loads applied fall into the four families below. You do not need the full standard: doing the two or three most relevant to your own lane properly beats filing a generic report.

3. Setting the Parameters: Weight, Format, Lane

The same test method with different parameters can produce opposite conclusions. Fix the four points below first, and test results become comparable.

  • Unit weight: heavier items take a lower drop height but higher stacking demands — set standards by weight band, not one number for the whole range
  • Packaging format: flexible bags hinge on seals and puncture resistance, paper bags on handles and base gluing, cartons on compression strength and internal support
  • Lane: domestic e-commerce is mostly manual sorting, while cross-border sea freight faces long humid transit and repeated transfers — set a higher grade
  • Stacking duration: 30 days of storage is not 90 days, and carton strength decays over time in humid conditions

4. Five Most Common Failures and What to Do

  • Burst seals: check seal width and adhesive weight first, then whether the seal lands on print or a crease; leave corner clearance and avoid sealing exactly on the edge line
  • Handles pulling out: almost always a load point that is too small. Eyelets or reinforcement patches at the holes plus a reinforced handle base do more than a thicker cord
  • Bulging or collapsing cartons: work out real stack height and storage time, then set carton grade and burst strength; fill large voids with partitions or void fill rather than thickening the walls
  • Scuffed tags and labels: lamination or varnish is the cheapest protection, and separating hard hardware (zips, buckles) from tags with interleaving or separate bags visibly cuts scratch complaints
  • Moisture and mould: paper and fabric together mould fast. Dose desiccant by volume, add barrier bags, and for long sea voyages use container desiccant strips — far cheaper than remediation on arrival
💡 Tip: write the test conditions into the packaging specification — weight bands, drop height, stack height, climate and duration. Only with those numbers does a supplier know where to leave margin; without them each batch goes by feel and the damage rate swings order to order.

5. A Five-Step Test Without a Lab

  1. Pack it for real: load it the way production will fold it and check tightness, voids and shifting contents — most sizing problems show up here
  2. Drop: from about one metre, drop once on a corner, once on an edge, once flat — focus on seals, handles and corners
  3. Stacking: apply the real stack load for 24 hours and watch carton deformation and marks on the contents
  4. Climate: hold it at high humidity (say 30°C and 80% RH) for 48 hours, then check adhesive, paper stiffness and mould
  5. Open and document: photograph each layer and keep the sample as the baseline for the next run and for any claim
