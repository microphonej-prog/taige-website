Garment Trims Storage Guide: Moisture, Yellowing, Pests and FIFO

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 14, 2026

Plenty of trim failures are not made in production but spoiled in storage: hang tags cockling with moisture, dark and light woven labels bleeding into each other, elastic losing its snap, stickers losing tack. The loss never shows up on a quotation, yet it surfaces right before bulk production starts. This guide covers how six common trim groups fail, the three environmental numbers (temperature, humidity and light), rack placement and labelling, batch shelf life, and the order in which to deal with mould, pests and odour.

1. Each Trim Group Fears Something Different

One room, one temperature, one humidity level is not the optimum: paper, textiles, adhesives and metal differ widely. Group by material first, then set the conditions. The table below lines up the usual failure mode and the storage rule for six trim groups.

Trim groupMost likely failureStorage rule
Paper items (hang tags, backing cards, paper bags, belly bands)Cockling and distortion from moisture, yellowing in daylight, insect damage20–25 °C and 45–60% RH, off the floor and away from walls, out of light, no heavy stacking
Woven labels and care labelsColour transfer between dark and light shades when damp, snagging, pressure marks from long stackingRolls upright or flat in low stacks, dark and light in separate boxes, never mixed with other trims on one pallet
Coated and screen-printed care labelsCoating blocking, whitening along creases, residual odourCool, unpressured, sealed against moisture, strict FIFO by batch
Elastic, webbing and drawcordsHeat and UV age the rubber, elasticity fades, dyes migrateAway from light and heat, keep the original sealed bag, mark the production date on intake
Adhesive items (labels, stickers, hook and loop)Adhesive takes up moisture and loses tack, picks up dust, release liner hard to peel in the coldSeal against moisture, store flat without weight, use the earliest expiry first
Metal parts (eyelets, fasteners, rivets, tag pins)Oxidation and darkening, salt-spray spotting, scuffing from frictionKeep dry and sealed, wrap in anti-rust paper, store in divided bins, do not bulk-mix
Sewing thread, ribbon and poly bagsMould growth from moisture, fading in the sun, static picking up dustClosed cabinets, raised off the floor, out of light, FIFO

2. Three Environmental Numbers: Temperature, Humidity, Light

Environmental control does not need a climate chamber, but three numbers must hold. Most paper items and woven labels are comfortable at 20–25 °C and 45–60% RH; above 65% RH for long periods, paper picks up moisture, adhesives lose tack and mould risk rises together; below 40% RH, static becomes noticeable and paper and film attract dust and shift.

  • Record temperature and humidity instead of guessing: hang a thermo-hygrometer in the warehouse and log it twice a day; in the rainy season and in humid coastal regions a dehumidifier must run continuously, with the tank checked.
  • Light is the quiet killer: UV is the shared cause of paper yellowing, dye migration and rubber ageing. Keep opened boxes away from windows and outer walls, and either close the lids or fit a shade.
  • Off the floor, off the wall, with airflow: pallets at least 10 cm off the ground, racks 20–30 cm from the wall, aisles kept clear so air moves, and nothing tight against outer walls, condensate pipes or floor drains.
  • Manage the doors: frequent opening spikes local humidity, so batch the movements and fit an air curtain or plastic strip curtain — far cheaper than chilling the whole warehouse.
💡 Tip: the cheapest humidity alarm is not the gauge on the wall but the packaging itself — cartons gone soft, condensation inside poly bags, wavy edges on rolled webbing. Any one of those means check the dehumidifier and the stacking height at once.

3. Placement and Labelling: Making FIFO Actually Work

  • Zone by batch and colour: different batches of the same trim sit in separate left/right zones; colours are never boxed together, and dark next to light is the classic recipe for colour transfer.
  • Rolls or flat: woven labels, care labels and webbing are best kept on rolls standing upright; if stored flat, cap the number of layers, because a pressure mark rarely comes out.
  • Six items on every label: item name, specification, batch number, date received, quantity and the order number it belongs to. Miss one and every return or repeat order starts with re-matching samples.
  • FIFO has to be visible: tag the older batch “use first” and place it outside, low and near the aisle; design the picking route around the date received, not around what is easiest to reach.
  • Close the box after sampling: reseal the original packaging right away and never leave paper items or coated labels exposed to warehouse air for long.

4. Which Trims Have a Shelf Life

Not every trim survives a year or two in stock. Adhesive, coated, elastomer-containing and printed items are batch-sensitive: beyond the shelf life the supplier recommends, performance decays slowly and then quickly — everything looks fine when applied, and only shows up after washing or on the machine.

ItemIndicator to watchRe-check method once expired
Self-adhesive labels and stickersInitial tack, holding tack, release-liner peelTake 3–5 pieces, apply and peel, and look for residue
Coated and screen-printed care labelsCoating blocking, wash fastnessWash it, then check abrasion, flaking and colour change
Elastic and rubber tapeElastic recovery, permanent setStretch, let it rest, watch the recovery against the reference sample
Laminated and varnished hang tagsSurface adhesion, yellowingFold it and try a tape peel, then check for delamination
Sewing thread (including lubricated)Lubricant migration, musty odourCheck appearance and smell, then sew a trial and watch the thread breaks

5. Mould, Pests and Odour: the Right Order of Handling

All three follow one principle: isolate first, judge second. The worst move is to wipe it clean and put it back with good stock.

  • Mould: usually paper arrives damp and then meets a humid warehouse. Isolate the whole pallet, record batch, location and arrival date, then sample to see whether it is surface-level or through the whole box.
  • On paper items, mould marks and colour shift are essentially irreversible: wiping does not fix the colour difference or the hygiene question, so the batch is normally scrapped or downgraded. QC and sales should decide together rather than leaving it to the storekeeper.
  • Pests: paper, cotton thread and natural fibres attract them most; control comes from dry conditions, sealed packaging and routine checks. Never spray insecticide directly on trims — residual odour gets magnified in garment testing.
  • Odour: residual ink solvent, damp mustiness and sealed-up storage are three different causes with three different fixes. Move the goods out to ventilate, spread them in separate boxes, send a sample for testing if needed — and never mask it with fragrance.
  • Record and trace: every incident must trace back to batch, date received and location, so you know whether it was one damp box or a storage environment that has gone out of control. Only the second demands immediate action.

6. Reference Samples, Colour Cards and the Inspection Routine

Reference samples and colour cards are the lightest and most expensive assets in the warehouse: they decide whether you hold evidence in a return, a repeat order or a quality dispute. Keep sealed samples and colour cards out of light, sealed and flat, dated and tied to an order number, and retain them for as long as the customer or channel requires.

  • Daily: temperature and humidity readings, dehumidifier status, water or seepage on the floor, doors and windows closed.
  • Weekly: spot-check the racks near windows and outer walls, packaging softness, weight on rolls, and piles blocking the aisles.
  • Monthly: label completeness and how FIFO is actually being followed, a list of slow-moving batches against the supplier's recommended shelf life, and filing of supplier feedback.
  • Quarterly: review sealed samples and colour cards for fading, soiling or missing pieces, inspect for pest traces, keep exits and aisles clear, and service the dehumidifier filter and drain.
  • Every incident: isolation record, supplier feedback, corrective action and verification, closing the loop so the same problem does not simply move to another rack.
