Wash-Away Backing Does Not Disappear. It Dissolves, and That Is a Different Claim

By TAGE Packaging · Updated November 28, 2026

A water-soluble embroidery backing is the film that keeps a chest logo crisp on a light fabric and then leaves in the first wash. It is not paper, and in most cases it is not compostable either: the standard product is a polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) film, a polymer that dissolves in water without vanishing from it. That distinction decides whether a brand can print 'biodegradable backing' on a hang tag at all. Below: what the material is, which numbers belong in a trim specification so a factory can verify it, which backing route leaves the least waste, and how care-label and composition rules in the EU, Japan and Korea limit what you may claim.

1. What the Film Actually Is

Backing splits into two families: what stays on the garment (tear-away, cut-away) and what is meant to be washed out (wash-away). Wash-away splits again into cold-water and hot-water grades. A cold-water film starts to soften around 20 °C and breaks up within about 30 seconds, which suits wool and silk that cannot take heat; a hot-water film needs 40–60 °C to dissolve fully. Backing weight commonly runs 30–60 g/m² with a film thickness of roughly 25–50 µm. Heavier backing holds dense stitch counts better, but it also puts more solids into the wash water. A third option is water-soluble nonwoven: it looks like paper, costs less, but leaves short fibres behind and shows as a white haze on dark garments.

2. Where It Goes After the Wash: the Part Nobody Measures

Once dissolved, PVA does not vanish; it becomes dissolved organic matter in the wash water, which is a COD load the wastewater plant has to treat. Take a 12 × 8 cm logo on 40 g/m² backing with a 3 cm frame: roughly 1 g of film per garment, so ten thousand pieces put about 10 kg of polymer into the wash water. Biodegradability is the crux. PVA does not pass the OECD 301 ready-biodegradability screen with unadapted sludge; it sits in the OECD 302 category of inherent biodegradability, meaning an adapted microbial community can break it down slowly, given time and the right sludge. So a claim of 'biodegradable' can be true in a laboratory and still not be true at the discharge pipe of the factory that washes your garments.

Practical rule: when a buyer asks whether the backing is biodegradable, the honest answer is not 'yes' but 'it is inherently biodegradable under OECD 302 conditions, and here is the test report and ECO PASSPORT certificate number'. The supplier who can hand over the documents is the one who survives the question.

3. What Each Backing Route Leaves Behind

Route What it leaves Where it fits
Cold-water wash-away Dissolves into the wash water as COD load Wool, silk, heat-sensitive light fabrics
Tear-away Solid offcuts into general industrial waste Denim, heavy cotton, coarse knits
Cut-away The most solid waste, plus hand trimming Dense logos that must stay flat for years

From a waste angle, tear-away looks cleanest, but it leaves the backing on the garment, where fibres shed through wear and washing and still end up in wastewater. Wash-away concentrates the load in the factory and the household wash, which is at least a point that can be treated. The real reduction is in design: shrink a logo from 12 × 8 cm to 9 × 6 cm, or drop one stitch density step, and backing consumption and wash-water load fall in proportion. That beats swapping materials, and it does not change the look.

4. Five Numbers That Belong in the Specification

'Biodegradable backing' is not a specification; it is a wish. What can go on a purchase order and be re-checked by the factory is five items: weight in g/m², dissolution temperature in °C, the residue test method, the chemical certificate, and consumption per thousand pieces. Write those down and a second factory can reproduce the same result instead of a similar one.

  • Pick one value between 30 and 60 g/m² and allow ±5 %; never write 'about 40'
  • Dissolution temperature: cold-water film at 20 °C, warm-water film at 40 °C or above, and state whether that means 'breaks up within 30 seconds' or 'fully dissolves'
  • Residue test: three home washes, then visual check under 45° raking light, haze no more than 5 % of the embroidered area, with a photo on file
  • Chemistry: an OEKO-TEX ECO PASSPORT certificate number plus a ZDHC MRSL conformance level, both in date
  • Consumption: kilograms of backing per thousand pieces, which lets you cross-check wash-water COD and cost, and compare a second factory on the same basis

5. How Labelling Rules Limit the Claim

Backing is neither packaging nor a declared fibre: it is a process auxiliary that leaves the garment in the wash. That means its 'biodegradable' property cannot go in the fibre composition field of a care label. Under Regulation (EU) 1007/2011 the care label must state fibre composition, and slipping a non-fibre claim into that field invalidates the label. The right place for the backing story is the brand website, the back of a hang tag, or a B2B technical sheet, with the test method and conditions spelled out.

Each market has its own hook. France: the AGEC law pushes textile environmental disclosure in stages, so check which tranche is live before publishing a French claim. Spain: Ley 7/2022 covers packaging and the plastic tax, not backing, while textile labelling still follows Regulation (EU) 1007/2011 in Spanish. Japan: the Household Goods Quality Labelling Act requires composition and handling information, with care symbols following JIS L 0001; backing is a process material and outside the labelling duty. Korea: the Electrical Appliances and Consumer Products Safety Control Act requires quality and handling marking with fibre content in percent, and again backing is not covered. China: GB 18401 governs basic safety of textile products, and voluntary carbon disclosure is allowed under the dual-carbon policy but must not be presented as a certification.

6. Where the Cost Actually Lands

The premium on an eco grade is not in the film; it sits in three other places. First, certificate upkeep: ECO PASSPORT and MRSL testing are renewed annually, and that cost is heavy on small volumes. Second, a narrower dissolution window, because the workshop has to hold water temperature and dwell time or risk residue complaints that eat the margin. Third, stocking, since cold-water and hot-water films are not interchangeable and both must be held. By contrast, shrinking a logo by 25 % usually saves more in backing and labour than the eco film costs extra, which is why we look at the design before the material.

7. Four Questions We Ask Before Quoting

  • Must the logo area read completely clean after washing? If yes, specify cold-water film and name the residue acceptance criterion
  • Can the fabric take 40 °C or more? If not, do not choose a warm-water film, or the first bulk run will fail
  • Does the buyer mean a material property or a disposal route? The first is answered by certificates, the second by local rules and wash conditions
  • Will you keep the test report in the purchasing file? When a client runs due diligence three years from now, that sheet is what saves you
