Trim pH and Skin Safety: Why Woven Labels, Care Labels and Tags All Need Testing

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 4, 2026

Apparel trims need pH testing too. Woven labels, care labels, paper hang tags and poly bags all sit against skin or against the garment, and yarn sizing, coating neutralisers, paper fillers and film additives can all carry alkaline residue into the finished product. China's GB 18401 lists pH alongside formaldehyde, odour and banned azo dyes as a basic safety item for textile products, splitting them into classes A, B and C for infants, direct skin contact and non-direct contact. Most brands focus on fabric and only spot-check a couple of items on trims at inspection; pH is one of the easiest to skip and one of the most likely to fail on re-test.

This guide covers why trims need a pH check, how to read the limits, and where alkalinity comes from in five trim families; then the test method and its error sources, four routes to fix an out-of-spec result, and five acceptance clauses you can put straight into a purchasing document.

1. The Side Against the Skin: pH Speaks Before Hand-Feel Does

Healthy skin sits at a mildly acidic pH of roughly 4.5 to 5.5, and that acid film is part of how it keeps bacteria in check and the barrier intact. Textiles carrying alkaline residue — alkali from sizing, from fixation of reactive dyes, ammonia and amines in coatings, calcium carbonate filler in paper — push that film upward on contact. The result is not always a visible allergy; more often it is itching, tightness, a sting when the wearer sweats, and complaints concentrate in infants, eczema-prone wearers and close-fitting garments worn eight hours a day. Such complaints are hard to prove, because the skin reaction is delayed and by the time the customer writes in, the garment has been washed twice.

Two rule sets need separating. China's GB 18401 is a mandatory national standard in which pH sits alongside formaldehyde, odour and banned azo dyes, and it is cited on domestic sales and on orders placed in China. EU REACH sets no single textile pH limit; the real constraint comes from the buyer's chosen scheme — OEKO-TEX STANDARD 100, bluesign or a customer RSL — each of which sets pH ranges by product class. So the same lot of trims can be compliant with the national standard and non-compliant with the customer's requirement at the same time, which is why the specification must name the reference it is measured against.

2. Reading the Limits: Classes A, B, C and the Internal Margin

GB 18401 classifies by contact, not by material. Articles for infants up to 36 months fall in class A, direct skin contact in class B, and non-direct contact in class C. On one garment, different trims follow the garment: on an infant bodysuit the woven label, care label and hang tag are all judged as class A; on an outer jacket the hang tag can be class C, but the neck label and care label are usually still class B, because the wearer may have nothing between the label and the back of the neck.

Class Applies to Standard limit Suggested internal control
Class A Infants, 36 months and under 4.0–7.5 5.5–7.0
Class B Direct skin contact 4.0–8.5 6.0–7.5
Class C No direct skin contact 4.0–9.0 6.5–8.5

An internal margin is not caution, it is arithmetic. A standard limit is a pass-fail line, not a production target. Between the trim factory and the retail warehouse a lot may pass through humid sea freight, stacked storage, pressing and steam, and pH drifts upward a little; add alkaline detergent and poor rinsing at the consumer's washing machine and the measured value climbs further. Setting the target one to one and a half units inside the limit buys buffer for all of that.

💡 The standard gives a range, not just a ceiling. Too acidic — below 4.0 — also fails, and over-neutralising with acetic or citric acid creates its own problems: residual vinegar smell, dye hydrolysis, and continued acidification in storage. Aim for the 6.5 to 7.0 band, not for as low as possible.

3. Five Trim Families, Five Different Sources of Alkalinity

The same pH number can have entirely different causes, and each needs a different fix. Identifying the source beats sending samples out again and again.

  • Woven and neck labels: alkali left by yarn sizing — starch or PVA — and by mercerising and desizing. Deep-coloured jacquard labels with insufficient washing after dyeing are the classic case, and a stiff, slightly harsh cut edge usually shows up at the same time.
  • Care labels: polyurethane and acrylic coatings are usually pH-adjusted with ammonia or amines to keep the bath stable, and nylon tape bases are themselves slightly alkaline while polyester fares better. The thicker and glossier the coating, the higher the residue risk.
  • Paper hang tags: calcium carbonate filler and surface sizing make the water extract naturally alkaline — 8 to 9 is common — and recycled pulp or heavily filled coated board goes further. Hang tags rarely touch skin and are usually class C, but tags on childrenswear and infant products are judged as class A, a point that is routinely missed.
  • Poly bags and film: slip agents such as erucamide, anti-block and antistatic additives are largely amines or fatty acid amides, and PE film carries more of them out than OPP; amines in printing ink add to the total.
  • Self-adhesive labels and hot-melt adhesives: acrylic emulsion polymerisation is usually neutralised with ammonia and a little remains after the film dries; hot melts used in lamination can also release alkaline components at temperature. The self-adhesive woven label placed inside the neckline is the one to watch in this group.

A working rule: the deeper the colour, the thicker the coating, the cheaper the paper and the softer the film, the more likely the alkalinity. That is why the same product can suddenly fail on a new lot — not because the process got worse, but because the yarn supplier changed, the paper mill changed, or the coating recipe was switched to a cheaper version.

4. How the Test Is Run: Method Matters More Than the Number

The standard Chinese method is GB/T 7573, the water extraction method, corresponding to ISO 3071 internationally. The principle is the same: cut the specimen, shake it in a defined proportion of water — some versions use 0.1 mol/L potassium chloride solution — for a set time in a sealed vessel, then read the pH of the extract. Simple as it looks, four things must appear on the report or two labs' numbers cannot be compared: method version, extraction medium, temperature and time, and the part sampled.

  • Where to sample: for a woven label take both the cut edge and the middle of the weave; for a care label include the coated and printed face; for a paper tag take both printed and blank areas. Base fabric alone gives a clean, meaningless number.
  • Liquor ratio and specimen mass: a deviation changes the result directly and is the most common source of disagreement between labs. Write the specified ratio into the specification rather than simply citing the national standard.
  • Environment and handling: an open extract absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and reads low; a pH electrode responds slowly at the alkaline end, so calibrate with two or three buffers and rinse between readings. These details account for 0.2 to 0.5 of a unit — exactly the width of a marginal verdict.
  • Replicates: at least three parallel specimens per lot, judged on the median or on the rule stated in the standard. One specimen is not enough to reject a lot, nor to accept it.

5. After a Fail: Four Routes and What Each Costs

  1. Change the sizing or the substrate. Modified starch, low-alkali PVA recipes and sizing-free yarns cut residue noticeably, but require new sampling, and the stiffness, hand-feel and cut-edge fraying all shift, so a new approval is needed. Suits new styles that still have a sampling window.
  2. Wash and neutralise harder. A warm rinse after dyeing, then citric or acetic acid to bring the bath to pH 6.5–7.0, is the most direct process fix. The cost is water, shrinkage and lead time: an extra wash can add 0.5 to 1.5 percent shrinkage on a woven label, so the dimensional tolerance has to move with it.
  3. Reformulate the paper tag. Cutting the calcium carbonate filler and switching to low-alkali surface sizing can pull the water extract down by 0.5 to 1.5 units. A water-based barrier coating is the other route, at the cost of paper feel and ink adhesion, and it needs re-evaluation where recyclability or compostability is claimed.
  4. Grade and buffer. For an order already in production with no room to change the process, the options are sorting, widening the internal margin, letting finished goods rest in ventilated storage, and steering the care instructions away from strongly alkaline detergent. That stops the bleeding; it does not cure.
💡 Do not treat a wash as the fix. Washing does lower pH, but it also moves shrinkage, surface hairiness and shade, and infant products cannot rely on after-treatment: the class A window is already narrow at 4.0–7.5, so a lot can come down from 8.2 to 7.4, look compliant, sit right on the edge, and land in the same place next batch.

6. Putting pH Into the Purchasing File: Five Acceptance Clauses

  • State the class and the limit. The first line of the spec should read class A of GB 18401, pH 4.0–7.5, or class B, 4.0–8.5, and name the method — GB/T 7573 or ISO 3071. Mixing classes is the most common source of dispute.
  • State the sampling location. Require that the specimen include the coating, the print and the cut edge, and you close the escape route where a lab tests base fabric only.
  • State the decision rule. Something like: three parallel specimens per lot, the median must sit within the limit; if any single specimen falls outside, retest once, and if the retest also fails, reject the lot with the cost charged to the responsible party. With the rule written down, inspection stops depending on goodwill.
  • Require a report and retained samples. Each lot should carry a third-party report, or an in-house report plus a quarterly third-party cross-check; retain samples for at least 12 months with lot, machine and date written on the bag. When something goes wrong, the retained sample is the only thing that can speak for you.
  • Write the change-control clause. Any change of yarn, substrate, coating recipe or paper supplier must be notified in advance and re-tested. Nine out of ten pH failures happen on a lot where nothing changed — except that something upstream quietly did.

One last note: pH is an entry point, not the whole file. The same specification usually carries formaldehyde, banned azo dyes, heavy metals, phthalates for coatings and prints, and nickel release on metal parts. Write them with one sampling and decision rule and inspection stops turning into a series of one-off catch-ups.
