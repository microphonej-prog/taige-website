Swimwear & Activewear Trims Guide: Chlorine-Resistant Care Labels, Waterproof Tags and Stretch Labels

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 12, 2026

Swimwear and activewear trims are among the most demanding categories in garment accessories: the fabric is stretchy and body-hugging, while the trims must survive pool chlorine, sea salt, sweat and frequent hot washing. Standard cotton woven labels or paper care labels used on these garments fade, stiffen, curl or fall off within weeks. This guide covers care labels, woven labels, hang tags and packaging for swim and sportswear.

The core principle is simple: trims must be as wash-resistant and durable as the fabric itself, otherwise the garment's lifespan is decided by its weakest component.

1. Why Swim and Activewear Trims Are Harder to Make

  • Chlorine attack — pool chlorine attacks dyes and elastane, so ordinary polyester labels yellow and fade over time.
  • High stretch — fabric stretches repeatedly, pulling at seams and labels; stiff tags chafe the skin and crumple.
  • Hot machine washing — gym wear is often washed at 40–60 °C with concentrated detergent and softener, which can leach label inks and coatings.
  • Skin contact and sensitive skin — swimwear and sports bras sit directly on the skin, so label edges must be soft and non-itching, and the attachment method must not rub.

2. Care Labels: Materials That Resist Chlorine, Salt and Sweat

For swim and activewear, prefer nylon or polyester tape over pure cotton, and choose coated or heat-transfer printing rather than standard screen printing.

Care label material Chlorine / wash resistance Best for
Coated nylon tape Excellent: soft edges, crisp print that survives washing Swimwear, sports bras, compression wear
Coated polyester tape Good; cheaper than nylon but slightly stiffer Running and training wear in mass-market ranges
Satin polyester (heat transfer) Fine print and vivid colour; moderate chlorine resistance Yoga and training wear where feel matters
Pure cotton tape (screen printed) Poor chlorine resistance; stiffens, fades and pills Not recommended for swimwear or heavily washed sportswear

3. Woven Labels, Stretch Labels and Silicone Labels

  • Stretch woven labels — woven with elastic yarn so they stretch with the fabric; ideal sewn directly onto elastane without puckering.
  • Ultra-fine polyester woven labels — crisp lettering and a thin edge, ideal on the underband of sports bras to reduce friction.
  • Silicone and TPU labels — fully waterproof and non-absorbent to chlorine, available as cut-outs or high-frequency welded patches; common on swimsuit side marks and yoga waistbands.
  • Heat-transfer labels — applied directly to the fabric with no textile edge; common on competition swimwear and body-hugging sportswear, but confirm the chlorine-resistance grade.

4. Hang Tags: Waterproof Materials and Finishing

Swim hang tags hang in store with the garment, get handled constantly and may meet moisture and sunscreen — ordinary art paper softens and warps.

  • Synthetic PP paper — fully waterproof and tear-resistant, with print quality close to coated paper; the mainstream choice for swim tags.
  • PVC cards — thicker and more rigid, good for die-cut shapes or transparent effects, though eco-minded brands may reject PVC.
  • Cotton paper with full lamination — keeps the premium feel while adding a waterproof film; a good compromise for upmarket swim brands.
  • Switch to plastic or nylon attachments — metal eyelets rust with prolonged chlorine and salt exposure; plastic fasteners or nylon cord are safer.

5. Packaging Bags and Accessories

  • PE and CPE beat OPP — swimwear is damp and stretchy, and PE or CPE is more flexible and puncture-resistant, while OPP is crisp but brittle.
  • Zip-lock bags — standard for e-commerce fulfilment and handy for storing a wet swimsuit; choose a vented or heavier-gauge version for durability.
  • Avoid metal fittings — staples and metal clips corrode in salty, chlorinated environments and stain pale fabrics; use plastic clips or stickers instead.

6. Sampling and Testing Points

Before bulk production, run a real wash test on swim trims: soak a sewn sample in chlorinated water or simulated sweat, then machine-wash it several times at the stated temperature and watch for fading, delamination or curling. Sampling usually takes 3–7 days and bulk production 10–25 days; add a week of buffer for projects with testing. On compliance, EU markets should check REACH restrictions on azo dyes and phthalates, and children's swimwear has extra cord and drawstring rules — confirm these at the sampling stage.

💡 Tip: 'Chlorine-resistant' is not an absolute property. Ask your supplier for the test conditions (chlorine concentration, soak time, water temperature) and actual results, not just a label. Real wear conditions vary widely, so a stated test method is the only way to judge fit for your market.
