Care Label Standards & Washing Symbols Explained

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated August 16, 2026

The care label is one of the densest and most compliance-heavy trims on a garment. It tells consumers how to wash, dry and iron — and it directly affects whether your clothing can enter the EU or US market. Understanding care label standards and washing symbols is a must for every export-oriented apparel brand.

1. Why Care Label Standards Matter

When garments are exported to different countries, the content and symbols on the care label are governed by local regulations. Incorrect labeling can lead to delisting, fines or even recalls. Meanwhile, symbols that are blurry or that fade after washing cause consumer complaints when garments are ruined. A compliant, legible care label is both a legal requirement and a sign of brand responsibility.

2. The Main International Standards

Today's care label standards are built on ISO 3758, with each market adding its own local rules:

  • EU: EN ISO 3758 — washing symbols must follow the standard; generally mandatory for EU exports
  • US: FTC Care Labeling Rule (16 CFR Part 423) — requires a permanent care label with fiber content and country of origin
  • China: GB 5296.4 — instructions for use of consumer products, Part 4: textiles and apparel
  • Japan: JIS L 0001 series — symbol system broadly aligned with ISO

If one garment is exported to multiple markets, design the care label to the strictest standard to avoid rework.

3. The Five Groups of Washing Symbols

ISO 3758 groups washing symbols into five categories. Symbols must appear as a complete set in a fixed order; invented or simplified symbols are treated as non-compliant:

4. Fiber Content & Origin Labeling

Both the US and EU are strict about composition labeling. The US FTC requires fiber generic names listed in descending order by weight (e.g. '80% cotton, 20% polyester'), plus country of origin and importer details. The EU also requires specific percentages for inseparable fibers. Wrong composition labels are a common reason for customs seizures.

5. Materials & Printing for Legible, Durable Symbols

Common care label materials include satin ribbon, coated ribbon and heat-transfer film. Ribbon labels withstand washing and feel soft, ideal for sewn-in main care labels; heat-transfer suits lightweight next-to-skin garments and resists wear. Whichever material you choose, symbols must stay sharp at small sizes, offer clear contrast, and survive repeated wash tests. A professional factory samples first, tests, then mass-produces.

💡 Tip: For the US market, meet both the FTC Care Labeling Rule and fiber-content regulations; for the EU, follow EN ISO 3758. Share your target markets and fabric composition with your supplier — a professional care label factory will design a compliant solution.

6. Care Label Materials Compared: Four Common Constructions

Four care label constructions dominate the market: satin polyester tape, coated tape, heat-transfer film and cotton tape. They differ less on price than on how legible the print stays after washing and how the label feels on skin. Before choosing, ask two questions: how harsh is the laundering routine, and will consumers actually see the label.

7. Size, Placement and Sewing: Three Details That Get Shipments Rejected

There is no global size rule, but buyers check one thing: can the label be read without opening the packaging? Common sizes are 20×40 mm, 30×50 mm and 40×60 mm — go larger when the text is long or multilingual. Keep each symbol at least 5 mm on its side; below that, print blurs and inspection teams will call it illegible.

  • Placement: about 5 cm above the hem in the side seam, or centre back neck — it must be reachable without opening the packaging. For next-to-skin pieces, avoid the upper side seam where it rubs.
  • Sewing: fold both ends and lock-stitch; the seam must never cross a symbol or a word. If the care label and the composition label are separate, both must carry the same care instructions.
  • Consistency: the care label, hang tag and e-commerce listing must agree. Platform audits and consumer complaints start by comparing exactly these three.

8. Wash Testing and Incoming Inspection Checklist

Compliance is not proven by printing — it is proven by testing. Run a sample wash test before bulk, then inspect by lot during production. Only with both steps does the care label actually carry evidentiary weight.

  1. Sampling: sew the sample label onto the actual fabric (never a substitute) and run 3–5 standard wash-and-dry cycles per ISO 6330 or AATCC 135
  2. Check four things: blurred symbols or text, frayed edges, color migration from dark to light fabric, and curling or stiffening of the label
  3. Record the conditions: water temperature, detergent, drying method and number of cycles go on the sample approval sheet so you can trace back if a dispute arises
  4. Bulk inspection: pull 3–5 pieces per lot for a quick wash and compare against the sealed sample; color and legibility are judged against that sample
  5. Keep the paperwork: file the test report plus composition and origin data so you can hand them over for customs clearance or platform review

9. Selling One Garment in Several Markets: One Care Label Covering FTC, EN ISO 3758 and GB 5296.4

One label for several markets works if you follow the strictest intersection: permanent attachment (US FTC 16 CFR Part 423), a complete symbol set in fixed order per EN ISO 3758, fiber content in descending weight with country of origin (US TFPIA / EU Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011), plus GB 5296.4 sizing and care wording for China. The symbols themselves are a universal graphic language — one ISO set serves all three markets. Only the written instructions and size marking need separate treatment.

The split between care label and hang tag comes up often: the hang tag carries brand, price and marketing, the care label carries legal and care information. Conflicts between them are a frequent finding in customs and platform audits — a hang tag saying "machine wash" while the care label says "dry clean only" sends the claim back to the brand and supplier once a garment is ruined. Proofread both on one approval sheet before production; it costs far less than re-labelling.

10. Three Common Misconceptions That Can Sink a Shipment

Care label problems rarely come from having no label at all — they come from one detail being wrong. These three misconceptions show up most often in inspections and customs hearings, and all of them can be avoided at no extra cost.

  • Misconception 1: assuming the EU has one mandatory care-symbol law. At EU level there is no single mandatory care-symbol regulation — EN ISO 3758 symbols are industry practice. Some member states, France among them, impose their own care-marking duty, and GPSR (EU) 2023/988 adds traceability and responsible-person requirements. Buyer standards are usually stricter than the law.
  • Misconception 2: writing fiber names loosely. Use the standard generic names, in descending order by weight, with percentages. Marketing words such as "pure cotton" or "premium cotton" cannot replace the legal generic name, and a miscalculated blend ratio is a common reason goods are held at customs.
  • Misconception 3: an English-only label for global sales. Korea, Japan and China all require marking in the local language; platform audits ask whether local consumers can actually read it. An English-only label counts as no label in those markets.

None of these costs extra — they only require stating the target markets, fiber composition and required languages at the sampling stage. Hand your market list to the supplier and let them design to the strictest country: that is far cheaper than one version per market, and it removes a pile of part numbers and stock.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the minimum order quantity for care labels?

Typically 3,000 pieces for one spec and one content set; several sizes can share the same run, and trial orders can be discussed at lower volumes. The more content you pack in (multilingual, multi-composition versions), the earlier you should lock the layout to avoid separate plate runs.

How long does sampling take, and is the sample fee refundable?

Standard samples take 3–7 working days; new plates or multi-color printing add a few days. The sample fee depends on the process and is usually credited against bulk. For urgent orders, state your launch date first and the factory will quote a feasible schedule against capacity.

What information do you need for a quote?

Target markets (which set the regulations and languages), fiber composition and percentages, sizes, required symbols, label dimensions and color, quantity, and whether bilingual text is needed. A complete inquiry gets a price range the same day; an incomplete one only gets a generic figure.

The print came off after washing — who is responsible?

Start with three questions: was the garment washed as instructed, did the print pass the sealed-sample adhesion test, and did the fabric receive a water-repellent or softener finish (finishes affect adhesion)? Keep the sample, the lot number and the wash record — most disputes are settled against the sealed sample, which is exactly why wash testing before bulk matters.

Can the care label be bilingual, e.g. Chinese and English?

Yes — bilingual labels are standard for export. For the US the English text must be complete; for the EU and Japan, ISO symbols plus key words in the local language are enough; for China, care methods and sizing are marked in Chinese. Bilingual layout takes more space, so scale the label up accordingly.

Appendix: Korean Labeling Requirements and Care Label Practice

A care label has to be read together with the statutory marking items. For adult garments, confirm before selling whether the product falls under supplier conformity verification under the Electrical Appliances and Consumer Products Safety Control Act; for products for children aged 13 or under, decide first whether safety confirmation under the Special Act on the Safety of Children's Products, that is KC certification, applies. The test reports and label wording differ depending on the answer, so settling this before freezing the label spec avoids rework.

Match symbols and wording to KS K 0021, the Korean standard for care labelling and its symbols. Symbols alone without Korean wording weaken consumer guidance; wording alone without symbols tends to draw comments at store inspection. State the blend ratio as a percentage per fibre, and place origin and handling precautions so they do not contradict each other on the same label. If the marking differs from the actual test result it can become a problem under the Act on Fair Labelling and Advertising, so re-check the blend ratio every time the fabric lot changes.

Settle the sizing and measurement conventions at the same time. Childrenswear is marked by height as size 90, size 100, size 110, while adult women's wear is easier to explain at inspection and customs when the 55, 66 and 77 Korean sizes sit next to the cm measurement. Fabric weight is given in g/m², and label margins and trims in mm. Keeping the minimum care-symbol size at 5 mm or more makes the symbols reliably legible whether printed or woven.

  • Confirm supplier conformity verification for adult wear and KC certification for children's products before freezing the label spec
  • Follow KS K 0021 for care symbols and pair them with Korean wording; keep blend ratio, origin and handling precautions consistent on one label
  • Mark childrenswear by height in cm, adult women's wear with Korean size numbers plus cm, and fabric weight in g/m²
