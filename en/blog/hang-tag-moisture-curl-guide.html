Hang Tags That Curl: Moisture Content, Lamination Stress and Storage

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 3, 2026

The cartons arrive, you cut them open, and the hang tags have curled at the edges, the stack no longer sits flat, and once strung they hang crooked on the garment. The first reaction is usually that the paper is too thin or the printing went wrong, but the real cause is usually moisture content against ambient humidity: paper is hygroscopic, and the water it took up in the pressroom comes back out slowly in a container and a warehouse, taking the shape with it.

This guide runs through the causes, the moisture ranges that matter, control at press and finishing, storage and sea freight, and what to do once tags have already curled — closing with the four things worth stating in a request for quotation.

Three sources of curl

  • Unbalanced lamination tension: the film shrinks as it dries and cools, pulling the paper towards the film side. Single-sided lamination almost always creates a stress difference, only the degree varies — and matte film shows it more than gloss, being usually thicker.
  • Moisture against ambient air: the equilibrium moisture content of paper follows relative humidity, falling roughly one to two points for every ten percent drop in humidity, and the sheet shrinks with it. Expansion and contraction across the grain run about two to three times that along the grain, which is why a sheet bends in one direction rather than shrinking evenly.
  • Stress from converting and stacking: die-cutting, creasing and hole punching all leave local stress. Packing warm tags straight into PE bags and palletising lets the whole stack distort as it cools, and the waves are there when the carton opens.

The moisture range that behaves well

Relative humidity Paper moisture (approx.) Behaviour
Below 30 percent 4 to 5 percent, dry Brittle, edges crack, more die-cutting dust, static builds up
45 to 60 percent 6 to 8 percent, the comfortable zone Printing, lamination and die-cutting all stable; flat finished tags
65 to 75 percent 9 to 11 percent, damp Softer sheet, creases spring back, lamination blisters and edges lift
Above 80 percent 12 percent and up, wet Wavy edges from swelling, set hard once cooled, hard to recover

These are working ranges, and paper grades shift them: coated art paper has a dense surface and takes up moisture slowly; solid bleached board and offset paper are more sensitive; recycled stock, with shorter fibre and more porosity, reacts faster. Weighing those differences when choosing a grade does more than comparing grammage alone.

Press and finishing: six actions that work

  1. Let the stack acclimatise first. Paper arriving in winter or by air should stay sealed for twenty-four hours in the pressroom so temperature and humidity equalise before it goes on press.
  2. Control fountain solution and prefer less water, more passes. Large solids and dark shades carry water into the sheet fastest, and once the ink-water balance tips wet, moisture content is hard to bring back.
  3. Give laminated sheets a rest. Do not die-cut straight after lamination: lay them flat for twenty-four hours so film and paper release stress. Skip this and curl rates climb noticeably.
  4. Build symmetrical structures where possible. Laminating or varnishing both sides balances the stress against itself and is far more stable than one side; if only one side is possible, use a thinner film and face it inwards during assembly.
  5. Rest before packing. Let cut tags lie flat for eight to twelve hours under a weighted board, then bag them; never seal tags that still carry residual heat.
  6. Spot-check moisture before packing. A handheld resistance or capacitance meter gives a reading in seconds; three to five points beat judging by feel by a wide margin.

Storage and sea freight: where it actually goes wrong

  • Bag in PE with a heat seal and dose desiccant by volume. Sachets of tens to a hundred grams protect a carton; a few small loose packets usually are not enough.
  • Line cartons with moisture-barrier paper, wrap pallets to the base, and keep stock off the floor and away from outer walls. Floor and wall are where moisture concentrates; a damp carton base curls the whole stack from the bottom up.
  • Sea freight needs protection from container rain. Day-night temperature swings condense moisture on the roof, which then drips; damage usually concentrates in the top two or three cartons. Long voyages justify hanging desiccant bars.
  • Do not open the bags on arrival. A winter or air-freighted carton is cold, and cold paper takes up moisture the moment it meets warm humid air, waving within hours. Leave the carton in the warehouse for four to eight hours, then open. This is the most overlooked and cheapest step of all.

What to do once the tags have curled

Light curl, edges lifting no more than two or three millimetres: unbag them, lay them flat in a room at about fifty percent relative humidity under a five to ten kilogram board, and leave them for twenty-four to forty-eight hours; most recover seventy to eighty percent of the way. Moderate curl responds to a flattening press at forty to fifty degrees Celsius and low pressure, though watch the laminate — too hot and it blisters or loses gloss. Severe single-sided lamination curl is essentially irreversible: change the process instead, to two-sided film, spot UV, or drop the film and go up in paper weight.

Four things to state in a request for quotation

Information Why it affects curl
Size and grammage Bigger and thinner means stress wins more easily; small, heavy board resists
Lamination, one side or two Single-sided film is the leading cause; two sides largely cancel out
Destination and mode Sea freight means longer transit and wider temperature swings, so it needs more moisture protection
Storage conditions An air-conditioned, dehumidified warehouse and a metal shed call for different packing levels
One rule of thumb: hang tags almost never curl at the press, they curl on the road between the pressroom and the shop floor. Set the packing and shipping plan together with the printing process, not after the complaint.
