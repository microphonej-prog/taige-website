Certification Logos and Green Claims on Hang Tags: What You May Print

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 25, 2026

Certification logos and environmental claims on hang tags and labels are where brands trip up most: printing a logo their certificate does not cover, or claiming "eco" and "recyclable" without evidence. The EU Green Claims direction and the Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR) are tightening these statements, and the US FTC Green Guides require them to be specific and verifiable. This guide explains which logos only the certificate holder may print, which documents grant that right, how to word claims safely, and gives a checklist you can attach to an order.

The article runs in this order: why the pitfalls are so common, the main logos and their conditions of use, how to word claims safely, who carries the responsibility and how to write it into the order, a pre-press checklist, and which documents your supplier can provide.

1. Why This Is So Easy to Get Wrong

A certification logo is not decoration, it is a managed trademark; an environmental claim is not copywriting, it is a statement a regulator can audit. Most problems come from three roles being blurred:

  • The certificate belongs to the supplier: OEKO-TEX, GRS and FSC certificates are held by the producer or trader, so a brand is usually not the holder and cannot simply use the logo.
  • The printer prints what it is given: printers do not verify logo rights; if the artwork carries the logo, it goes on press. Liability stays with whoever sent the file — usually the brand or its supplier.
  • Platforms and retailers check: marketplaces, department stores and concept stores ask for certificates or transaction evidence during listing review; a logo without matching documents can get a product pulled.

2. Common Logos and Their Conditions of Use

Logo Covers Who may print it Frequent misuse
OEKO-TEX Standard 100 Harmful-substance testing of materials and components The certificate holder, with official label authorisation Testing only fabric, then printing the logo on tags and bags
GRS (Global Recycled Standard) Recycled content percentage and chain of custody The certified factory, and only with a transaction certificate (TC) for that shipment Printing it when only part of the item is recycled, implying the whole product is
FSC forest certification Sourcing and chain of custody for paper and wood-derived material The CoC-certified printer; promotional use needs prior approval Omitting the trademark mark (® / TM) or recolouring and distorting the official version
Recyclable / degradable claims Not a certification — a self-declared statement The brand itself, but it needs testing or an industry standard behind it A blanket "100% eco-friendly" without recycling conditions or applicable regions

3. How to Write a Claim Safely

  • Be specific: "60% recycled polyester (GRS certified)" is far stronger than "eco material" — and it is exactly what regulators and buyers want to see.
  • Define the scope: which components are covered? The tag paper may be recycled while the cotton string and metal eyelet are not — either limit the claim to the part, or use neutral wording.
  • Avoid absolutes and home-made badges: "100% degradable", "zero pollution" or "carbon neutral" need full life-cycle evidence, and inventing a green leaf badge that looks like a certification carries similar risk.
  • Match language and market: the same tag sold in the EU, UK and US needs claim wording and recycling symbols adjusted per market — one universal version rarely holds.

4. Responsibility and How to Write It Into the Order

The simplest safeguard is to settle four points at sampling or ordering stage, before plates are made:

  • Who supplies the logo file: the brand's official vector, or an authorised version obtained through the certification body.
  • Where the certificate number appears, at what size, and whether it must read the same across languages.
  • Signed proof: the printer goes on press only after the brand signs off the final file containing the logo; moving or recolouring it needs a fresh approval.
  • Keep records: order, certificate, transaction evidence and approved sample for several years, so an audit or platform review can be answered in one pass.

5. Pre-Press Checklist

  • The certificate is valid and covers the trims and processes in this order
  • This shipment has its matching transaction certificate (GRS TC) or batch test report
  • Logo use is authorised in writing, using the official artwork and correct trademark symbol
  • Every claim can be traced to a document or test
  • Wording matches across hang tag, care label and packaging bag, with no contradiction
  • Market versions are separated: EU, UK and US each use their own wording and symbols

6. What Your Supplier Can Provide

A competent trim supplier can provide all of this before the order is placed: the certificate scan with number and validity, the material supplier's declaration or transaction certificate number, third-party test reports (harmful substances, colourfastness, ink migration), and the logo licence or official artwork issued by the certification body. Note that the certificate belongs to the producer, so when a brand wants to print a logo the supplier usually has to arrange the authorisation — raising it at sampling stage saves the most time.

💡 Tip: treat certifications and claims as a technical field on the tag, not as marketing copy. Three questions — who will read it, which document backs it, where the boundary lies — need answers before it goes on press.
