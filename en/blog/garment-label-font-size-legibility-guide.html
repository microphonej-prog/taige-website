Care Label Font Size: Minimum Type Heights, Contrast and Legal Language

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 15, 2026

The regulatory requirement for care label text comes down to four words: clearly legible. On the factory floor that becomes three measurable numbers — type height, stroke width and contrast. Take one line, 65% polyester and 35% cotton: at 0.8 mm type height on a coated nylon label it smears into a black bar, and at 1.2 mm on a woven label it still needs a 0.20 mm stroke to keep from joining up. This guide puts our standard minimum type heights, care symbol spacing, a multilingual width budget and each market's legal language in one place, so the calculation happens before the artwork is signed off.

1. Minimum type height: same word, three times the difference

The table below is our working floor, not a clause in any standard: ISO 3758 defines the care symbols but says nothing about type height, and both Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011 and US 16 CFR 423 ask only for legibility. Control therefore rests on two things: set the floor at artwork stage, and after sampling have a colleague who has never seen the style read the label at 300 lux from 30 cm. A misread or a blank stare is a fail.

Process Min. type height Min. stroke Note
Woven label, shuttle 1.2 mm 0.20 mm The smaller the type, the harder and dearer it weaves
Woven label, needle 1.6 mm 0.30 mm Below this, O, A and 8 knit together
Coated nylon care label, thermal transfer 1.0 mm 0.15 mm The usual choice when languages sit side by side
Heat transfer film label 0.8 mm 0.12 mm Temperature and pressure must be fixed
Paper hang tag, offset 1.0 mm 0.10 mm Can go smaller, but retail readability drops
Laser or inkjet coding 0.9 mm 0.15 mm Edges bloom; avoid hairline serif faces

2. Care symbols: order, size and spacing

Five symbols, fixed order: wash, bleach, dry, iron, professional care. Europe and the US print the EN ISO 3758 figures, Japan uses the wash-symbol set defined in JIS L 0001, and Korea the KS K 0021 marking symbols. Legibility depends on two habits: symbol height at least 1.5 times the type height on the same line, and one symbol-width of space between neighbours. Print water temperature as 30 ℃ or 40 ℃ and skip the fine print underneath — small text is the first to fill in and the first to be misread.

3. Multilingual width budget: French and Spanish run 20–35% longer

Measured on the same block of composition, care and origin text: English 100 characters as the baseline, French 125–135, Spanish 120–130, Japanese 60–70 characters, Korean 70–80. With 40 mm of usable width and 1.0 mm type, a line holds about 70 characters, so the French version quietly needs an extra line. The practical order is longest language first — French, then Spanish and English, then Chinese and Japanese or Korean. Do it the other way round and French never fits, so the type height gets cut, which is exactly how labels drop below the readability floor.

Language Characters for the same block (English = 100) Lines that fit in 40 mm How to tighten it
English 100 (baseline) 4 —
French 125–135 3 Drop articles, use the standard abbreviations, no space before %
Spanish 120–130 3 Use que to open clauses, avoid stacked el que
Japanese 60–70 characters 3–4 Mix kanji with kana, never all kana
Korean 70–80 characters 3 Omit particles and use spacing to break the phrase

4. Legal language: a missing language is a missing label

Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011 requires labelling in the language of the member state where the goods are sold; France additionally requires mandatory wording in French under the Code de la consommation, and Spain sets both the method and the terminology of fibre labelling in Real Decreto 928/1987. Japan requires the fibre mix, care symbols and origin in Japanese under the Household Goods Quality Labelling Act, and Korea requires Korean marking plus KC certification information. The usual failure is not a bad translation but a half-finished one: composition in French, care text still in English fine print. The French consumer gets half the information, and a customs or marketplace audit scores it as non-compliant.

5. Contrast: three combinations that fail most often

  • Light type on a dark ground with metallic ink: gold foil on black glares at an angle and bleeds head on, so type height needs 30% more
  • White on black in a woven label: thick white weft fills the counters, and O, A and 8 close up first
  • Coloured type on white nylon: red and orange bloom at the edge fastest, so switch to navy or black
  • Matte or reflective coating: gloss turns patchy after printing and the label disappears under shop light, so use matte ink and wider tracking

6. Sign-off: 300 lux, 30 cm, three readers

  • After sampling, three people read the label at 300 lux from 30 cm; one misread sends the artwork back
  • Measure type height and stroke with a 10× loupe and a scale, item by item, and record the numbers
  • Barcode grade at least 1.5 (grade B) on paper hang tags, at least 1.2 on woven labels and fabric tags
  • Compare every care symbol against EN ISO 3758, JIS L 0001 and KS K 0021 figures — no near-miss glyphs
  • Check every language version for a complete legal label: French, Spanish, Japanese and Korean cannot stop at composition
