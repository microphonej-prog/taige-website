Woven Label Artwork Guide: Line Weight, Font Size & File Formats

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated September 3, 2026

Woven label plate-making follows a different logic from print: printing can reproduce photo-grade gradients, but a woven label is a grid picture built thread by thread — yarns have thickness, density has limits, and each color must be assigned to a specific yarn. When brands bring gorgeous artwork to have woven labels sampled, repeated rejections are usually not the factory's fault but the artwork's incompatibility with weaving physics. This woven label artwork guide explains how to submit files, where the minimum line and font limits sit, and which effects simply cannot be woven.

1. Why Woven Label File Requirements Are Stricter Than Print

A woven label's resolution is set by warp and weft density, commonly in the range of 30–50 yarns per centimeter. Any detail narrower than one or two yarns simply cannot be rendered: fine lines break, small text blurs, and gradient steps fall apart. A printer's ink dot is measured in microns, while the smallest unit of a woven label is a yarn diameter — which is why a design that looks perfect on screen can come out unrecognizable when woven. Artwork for woven labels must therefore be treated with weaving logic from the design stage: thicken lines, enlarge type, and convert gradients into color blocks.

2. File Formats and Basic Setup

  • Vector files first: AI, PDF and CDR are ideal — they scale without losing quality and give the most accurate color separation; if bitmap, supply 1:1 artwork at 300 dpi or higher
  • Design at 1:1 finished size: state the finished width (including fold style) and design text and graphics at actual finished scale — avoid designing large then shrinking, which distorts proportions
  • Convert text to outlines and remove strokes: avoids font-missing issues that scramble the layout; if keeping live text, name the fonts used
  • Mark color numbers and positions: label every color block with a Pantone reference and use numbered swatches to tell similar shades apart — plate separation and shade matching both depend on it

3. Practical Minimums for Lines, Fonts and Spacing

Here are practical minimums (they vary with yarn count and density, and sampling is the final judge): line weight at least 0.3 mm, thicker for reversed-out lines (light text on a dark ground); Latin font height above 1.5 mm and Chinese characters above 3 mm, larger still for complex stroke counts; gaps between adjacent color blocks of at least 0.5 mm, or yarns bleed into each other and edges look fuzzy. Small type cannot be fixed by trying slightly smaller — the yarn grid is what it is, and strokes narrower than the grid simply do not exist.

💡 Tip: when in doubt, ask your supplier for a test-weave sample using your thinnest lines and smallest type — see the real limits with your own eyes, then finalize accordingly. It saves far more time than repeated rejections.

5. Why Artwork Gets Rejected and How to Fix It

  • Gradients/photos: woven labels cannot blend smoothly — switch to a zoned design of main and accent color blocks, or simulate depth with lines and halftone dots
  • Type too small: do not reuse a print file — enlarge and reset the type against woven-label minimums
  • Too many colors: each extra color adds a yarn set and a shade-matching point — merge similar shades and stay within 4–6 colors for safety
  • Dense hairline patterns: more warp lines mean more broken yarns — use solid areas instead; if lines are essential, widen them and their spacing
  • Weak contrast: dark text on a dark ground disappears — use reversed-out white or light yarns on dark grounds

6. From File to Approved Sample

A well-prepared woven label file usually needs just one round of revision: artwork → digital color separation and process review (colors, density, fold style) → electronic mock-up for approval → weaving the sample (3–7 days) → physical shade matching and test sewing → approved sample. Once approved, put the color references, dimension tolerances and density into the contract and bulk production follows the sample. Hand your supplier a 1:1 vector file with Pantone references and the whole chain runs far more smoothly.
