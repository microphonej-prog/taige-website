Necktie and Bow Tie Trims: Where Labels Go and How to Pack Them

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated October 13, 2026

Neckties, bow ties and scarves use little material, sell at a high unit price and offer a narrow display area, yet they need just as many trims as a shirt: neck label, fibre content label, care label, size or length information, hang tag, gift box and backing card. Each one decides whether the product can be listed smoothly in a department store or on a cross-border platform. Neckwear has three specific difficulties: labels must be sewn onto thin, slippery fabric, composition is tightly regulated by textile fibre rules, and packaging has to prevent creasing while still looking like a gift.

This guide runs through five parts: the trim set itself, where each label is sewn, composition and material marking, sizing and system data, then packaging and children's neckwear safety, closing with a specification checklist.

1. The Trims a Neckwear Item Actually Needs

A tie looks like a short trim list, yet what blocks a delivery at the channel or the platform is nearly always in the right-hand column below.

2. Where the Labels Go: Ties, Bow Ties and Scarves Differ

  • Ties: the neck label usually sits on the narrow end at the back or along the centre seam, with composition and care either combined with it or on a separate label. The real risk is stitch marks and pulled threads on the face, so the work standard must fix needle, thread and tension.
  • Pre-tied bow ties: the brand label is normally sewn on the inside of the adjustable strap, without spoiling the feel of the adjustment or showing while worn.
  • Self-tie bow ties: the label goes below the knot or on the tail, away from the stress points, otherwise repeated tying wears it white.
  • Scarves and squares: usually a corner label or a folded label in the hem. The area is small, so the text must be tiny and limited to what the law demands plus the brand.
  • Common ground: silk, polyester and satin are all slippery. Sew one trial piece before setting bulk parameters, to confirm no needle holes, no pulled threads and no shine marks after pressing.

3. How to Mark Composition and Material

The rule for composition is to write little and write accurately: once it is on the label it becomes a promise to the consumer and the first thing an inspector reads.

  • Percentages must match the goods, blends go from highest to lowest, and whether decorative threads or interlining count follows the target market's rules.
  • Use standard fibre names: mulberry silk, polyester, wool, viscose and so on — never a nickname or a trade name instead.
  • Leather and imitation leather parts (leather bow ties, leather tabs, leather patches) are marked under leather labelling rules; bonded leather, PU and synthetic leather must never be marked as genuine leather.
  • Multilingual markets need the matching language versions; EU member states generally require composition and care information in their own official language.
  • Fibre content drifts slightly between batches, so keep raw-material declarations and test reports for later traceability and explanation.
💡 Tip: neckwear trims use so little material that they are treated as an afterthought. Sew, wash, fold and hang one finished piece first, then fix the bulk specification — far cheaper than reworking a full batch.

4. Size, Length and System Data

  • A tie is typically around 145–150cm long and 6–9cm wide at the blade, with slim ties narrower; exports often carry both length and width, or a style code instead.
  • Bow ties are mostly one-size adjustable; stating the adjustment range is more useful than a letter size and clearly cuts size-related returns.
  • A brand's own size logic must agree across hang tag, neck label, care label and stock system — one careless spot becomes a return or a warehouse exception.
  • Retail and e-commerce warehouses normally want a scannable barcode; settle the barcode-to-SKU mapping at the sampling stage so nothing has to be relabelled before receipt.

5. Gift Boxes, Backing Cards and Export Packing

  • Rolling saves space and resists creasing; laying flat on a backing card displays better. For cross-border shipping, a roll with an inner liner balances cost and appearance.
  • Test the fixing method (pin, clip, plastic fastener) on a finished piece: a pin is the quickest way to puncture satin, so use a clip or card wherever possible.
  • Gift box and carton must agree: checking carton marks, barcodes, quantities and pack specs before receipt costs less than explaining at the warehouse door.
  • Long sea routes mean humidity control: protect paper boxes from wrinkling and metal fittings from oxidising.

6. Safety Points for Children's Neckwear

  • Children's ties and bow ties fall under childrenswear rules: cord length and breakaway construction must follow the target market's drawstring and cord safety requirements (the EN 14682 family in the EU, US requirements and individual platform rules).
  • Clips, plastic fasteners and pins need enough pull strength, no sharp edges and no easy detachment; write the pull requirement into the spec sheet at sampling.
  • Age banding matters: products for children under three face stricter small-part rules, so confirm the target age before quoting to avoid redoing the specification.
  • Run one pull, appearance and edge review at sampling, record the conclusion in the spec sheet, and let bulk production and inspection follow that single standard.
