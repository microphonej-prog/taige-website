Hang Tag Changes: Reprint, Over-Sticker or a New Version

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 20, 2026

Learning that the hang tag has to change after it has been printed is the most common rework in trims procurement. The first instinct is to reprint everything; the second is to cover the old panel with a sticker. Both carry a price: a reprint pays the one-off tooling and proofing again, and an over-sticker can bury mandatory information under adhesive. This guide splits the decision into three routes, costs each one out, draws the legal line for stickering, and ends with six questions to settle first.

1. Four Triggers That Force a Change

In practice almost every change falls into four buckets: a price adjustment, a change of the legal entity behind the brand (English company name or registered address), a correction to compliance content (a fibre percentage entered wrongly, care symbols from an outdated edition, a new EU responsible person), and one style sold into several markets needing different language sets. The first two are commercial calls. The last two are compliance, and compliance cannot be postponed with a sticker, because an inspection reads the finished product, not the purchasing plan.

2. What Each of the Three Routes Costs

Route One-off cost Per piece When to choose it
Full reprint Plate and proof Base price plus 3–5% spoilage The change touches mandatory information, or old stock is under three months of use
Over-sticker Low sticker tooling, gang-run friendly Sticker cost plus 4–6 seconds of labour Only non-mandatory content: price, internal style code, campaign wording
New version, old stock counted or destroyed Tooling plus destruction witness Base price Several markets at once, needing a traceable version number

Run the numbers on a typical job: 5,000 tags in 350 g/m² coated board at 0.05 dollars each, with 40 dollars for plate and proof. Allocating the one-off over 5,000 pieces adds 0.008 dollars per tag, so the full reprint lands near 300 dollars including spoilage. The same 5,000 tags covered by stickers cost 0.006 dollars per sticker plus about 5 seconds of labour each, which at 6 dollars an hour works out near 0.008 dollars, roughly 70 dollars in total. Four times cheaper, provided that what changed was never on the mandatory list to begin with.

3. Three Red Lines for Over-Stickering

The first red line: never cover mandatory information. Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011 requires fibre composition to be indicated on the product and to stay visible through the chain, so a composition sticker on a tag that gets torn off is the same as no label; the FTC rule at 16 CFR 423 requires care instructions permanently attached to the garment; and the GPSR, (EU) 2023/988, requires manufacturer and responsible-person details to be traceable. Those three can only be fixed by reprinting or by remaking the durable label. The second: do not reduce legibility. Keep at least a 2 mm margin around the sticker, never shrink the type below the original size, and remember that a double layer over a barcode is unreadable to a scanner. The third: the sticker must not be less durable than the tag, or the first wash or rub takes you back to the old information.

One line divides them: price, internal style code and campaign wording can be stickered; composition, care, origin and responsible person must be reprinted or moved to a durable label.

4. Four Markets, Four Different Answers

France layers the Code de la consommation on top of the EU rules, and French-language marking is not optional. Spain regulates textile composition marking through Real Decreto 928/1987 and handles misleading marks under its unfair competition law. Japan's Household Goods Quality Labelling Act makes composition, care symbols and the labeller mandatory items, while advertising wording falls under the Act against Unjustifiable Premiums and Misleading Representations. Korea requires quality marking and country of origin under its Quality Management and Safety Control of Industrial Products Act, with promotional wording governed by the Act on Fair Labelling and Advertising. One shipment going to four markets cannot share one sticker set, least of all for origin and responsible person, where the required position and wording differ.

5. Version Control: Stop the Old Edition Leaving the Warehouse

  • Print the version number on the back or under the barcode at 1.5 mm or larger, matching the number on the purchase order.
  • The PO should name the version, the language set, the target market and an expiry date, not just one batch of tags.
  • Segregate old stock with a different tape colour or a separate rack, and mark the bin card as do not ship.
  • Keep a record of every sticker decision: which version, which field, what was applied, and who applied it. Two years later that record is the only answer to a traceability question.

6. Six Questions Before You Commit

  • Is the change mandatory or commercial? Mandatory content rules out stickers immediately.
  • How many months of old stock remain? Under three months of use, the amortised cost of a reprint is small.
  • One market or several? Multi-market versions need version numbers, not memory.
  • Will the sticker survive washing and rubbing? If not, it is out.
  • Will the sticker sit over the barcode? If so the layout changes, not just the sticker position.
  • Who signs off that the old version is retired? Without a named person, the old stock reappears on the line within six months.

A tag change is not a design question but a trade between cost and compliance. Classify the change, cost the three routes, keep mandatory content off the sticker, and name one person to retire the old version. Do those four things and one revision stops turning into two rounds of rework.
