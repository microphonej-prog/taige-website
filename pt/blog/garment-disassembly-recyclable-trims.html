Disassembly Design for Garments: Mono-material Trims and Labels That Come Off

By TAGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 2, 2026

When a garment reaches a recycling line, the first step is to cut away woven labels, care labels, zips, rhinestones and trims — small items that often weigh under five percent yet frequently decide whether the main fabric can enter a recycling stream at all. The EU textile strategy and ESPR have written disassembly and recyclability into design requirements, and brands are pushing the requirement upstream: trim suppliers now have to explain whether their product obstructs recycling.

This guide sets out what disassembly design asks of trims, the trade-offs of mono-material choices, why stitching matters more than material, the legal information that cannot be traded away, and how to prove the result with documents and data.

Which trims obstruct recycling

Trim Common choice Effect on recycling Disassembly route
Neck and woven labels Polyester jacquard with nylon edge Mixed with cotton body; near-infrared sorting struggles Cotton label for cotton garments, or a tear-away seam
Care label Coated nylon tape, multi-layer laminate Coating and film cannot be separated; incineration only Uncoated polyester tape printed direct, or a tear-away label
Sewing thread Polyester thread on cotton or wool Fibre mixing lowers recycled yarn quality Same-fibre thread, or water-soluble thread at key points
Tag fasteners and string PP fastener, nylon elastic cord Hand-cut; contaminates paper streams Paper fastener and cotton cord, recyclable with the tag
Decorative parts and hot-fix stones Metal hardware, glass stones, adhesive Damages shredders; adhesive residue Removable hardware; fewer bonded decorations

Mono-material: from blended to identifiable

The point of mono-material is not a hundred percent single composition but making identification and sorting at the recycling end unambiguous. The more concentrated the trims on a garment, the easier near-infrared sorting becomes and the higher the value of the recycled output. Three practical choices:

  • Polyester shell with polyester labels and polyester thread: all three sit in the same sorting channel without extra dismantling
  • Cotton shell with a cotton label, or a tear-away label that leaves the body single-fibre once removed
  • Keep care labels free of multilayer laminates: a three-layer PET film plus coating plus print cannot be separated, while a single-layer tape printed direct recycles far more easily

Easy removal: stitching matters more than material

However mono-material the trim, a label sewn in hard is still impossible to remove at the recycling end. Disassembly is delivered mainly through stitching; four approaches, ordered by how easy they are to put into production:

  1. Tack or interrupted stitching: fix one end of the label with a few stitches so it pulls free. Costs almost nothing extra and is the easiest to roll out.
  2. Tear-away labels: build the care label as a tear-away so it rips along a prepared line, leaving only a short stub and no fragments in the body.
  3. Water-soluble thread: a dissolvable thread holds the label temporarily and disappears in finishing or at first wash, useful for labels and decoration that need temporary locating.
  4. A separate label pocket: sew the label into a small pocket at the hem or side seam so one cut removes the whole strip, keeping looks and demountability together.

The costs must be stated too: tacked labels pull off easily during try-on and the customer has to accept that; water-soluble thread is sensitive to process temperature and can dissolve too early in a hot setting; the stub left by a tear-away must be positioned so it does not chafe. None of these are technical barriers, but each has to be written into the specification.

Legal content cannot be traded for recyclability

Fibre composition, care symbols, country of origin and warnings are legal duties that no sustainability trend removes. Disassembly design changes the material and the method of attachment, not whether the information exists. Blurring a composition table or dropping care symbols to claim mono-material trades compliance for image, and the risk far outweighs the gain.

One point often missed: if the label is designed to be torn away, the legal information must be readable before it is removed. The label has to carry the content in full, with no key field printed on the half that gets ripped off — otherwise the customer holds nothing and an inspector has nothing to check.

Cost and process impact

Mono-material usually costs a little more: cotton labels are less dimensionally stable than polyester, uncoated care tape prints less readily, and a tear-away structure adds a die-cutting step. The larger the volume, the more these differences amortise; on small trial orders the gap is more visible.

Conversely, disassembly design saves the recycler manual labour and raises the value of the main fabric, which is exactly why brands pay a premium. Making that argument explicit in a sourcing conversation is far more effective than saying sustainable.

How to prove it: documents and data

  1. Composition in the bill of materials: state fibre content, weight and coating type for every trim instead of writing one woven label.
  2. A disassembly statement: which parts come off by hand, with what tool and in how many seconds, as a table appended to the specification.
  3. Prepare digital product passport fields: material, recyclability and recycled content will end up in the DPP, so structure the data now and deliver it the moment a customer asks.
  4. Tie certification to batch numbers: GRS and RCS certificates are not wall decoration; they must map to a transaction certificate for the specific batch.
One acceptance principle: disassembly should not live as an arrow on a design drawing but as one person, one pair of scissors, thirty seconds to remove every non-body material. A standard you can measure is the only standard.
