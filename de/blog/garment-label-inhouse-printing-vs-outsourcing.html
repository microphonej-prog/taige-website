In-House Label Printing or Pre-Printed Labels: Cost, Wash and Compliance

By TAIGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 2, 2026

Printing labels in-house and buying them pre-printed are two different production models, not two price points. In-house means a thermal transfer printer, ribbons and blank stock, producing labels on demand inside the factory. Pre-printed means a trims plant weaving, printing or hot-stamping in bulk and delivering by carton or roll. The decision hinges on three things: order profile, wash requirements and how artwork versions are controlled — not on what the printer costs.

This guide works through four angles — cost structure, wash limits, compliance risk and hybrid models — to help factories and brands decide which labels to print themselves and which must be outsourced.

What actually differs between the two models

Dimension Pre-printed, outsourced Printed in-house
What arrives Finished rolls or cut-and-folded labels, sew-ready out of the box Blank stock and ribbons, printed sheet by sheet on demand
Unit cost versus volume Falls with volume; plate or loom set-up amortises Barely volume-sensitive; mainly consumables and labour
Changeover speed New plate and sample, usually several working days Edit the file, minutes
Finish ceiling Satin, jacquard, foil and embossing all possible Flat print; metallic and relief effects stay limited
Typical fit Main labels, care labels and hang tags in steady volume Labels with many variants, small batches or rush top-ups

Three order profiles where in-house printing really pays

  • Many variants, small quantities per style: multiple sizes and colours mean one set-up per colour, and pre-print plate costs cannot amortise, so the label price is dragged up by tooling.
  • Make-to-order and dropshipping: production starts with the order, labels cannot sit in stock in advance, and in-house printing keeps labels in step with sales.
  • Rush top-ups and rework: a customer adds a few dozen pieces, and pre-print minimums and lead times cannot respond, while in-house printing ships the same day.

Four hidden costs

  • Consumables and trial waste: ribbon is consumed by the metre, and blank stock used for set-up and test prints is scrapped outright, so real consumable cost runs well above the theoretical area calculation.
  • Stock compatibility: printers have a window for thickness, coating and liner, and the wrong stock produces faint print, rub-off or liner tearing.
  • Wash validation: printed labels need their own wash and abrasion testing, and a failure means reworking garments, not labels.
  • Version drift: files sit on several computers and chat groups, and nobody can say who changed a fibre percentage or whose Spanish file is out of date.

Wash limits: ribbon and stock decide what the label may claim

Wax ribbons are cheap and print at low temperature, but abrade and dissolve easily, so they suit hang tags and carton marks rather than anything washed. Resin ribbons bond hard and survive washing and dry cleaning, which makes them the choice for care and durability labels. Mixed ribbons sit in between and handle ordinary care information on cotton satin. The stock matters as much: coated nylon and coated polyester tapes take ink evenly and keep characters crisp, while uncoated cotton tape drinks ink and spreads strokes, punishing small type.

So before printing machine wash at 40 on a label, run a wash test to the relevant standard for the number of cycles you intend to claim: check edge lift, character blur and whether the barcode still scans. The trim combination tested must match production, and any change of stock or ribbon means retesting.

Compliance risk: fibre content, language and origin

With pre-printing, one wrong percentage means redoing the batch. With in-house printing, the error lives in the file: a few thousand labels may already be on the floor and sewn into garments before anyone notices. Fibre content and percentages, care symbols, country of origin and importer or responsible-party details must come from a single controlled master file, with every change logged. In multilingual markets, make sure no printer is still pulling an old Spanish or French file.

The hybrid model: keep the long tail in-house

At any real scale the answer is hybrid: main labels, care labels and hang tags — the core items with steady volume — are pre-printed for the best finish and the lowest unit cost, while size labels, temporary variants and rework top-ups are printed thermally in-house. Brand finish survives, and small batches stop holding the line hostage. The dividing line comes down to two numbers: annual volume per style and redesigns per year. High volume with a stable layout goes out; five changes a year at a few hundred pieces each stays in.

A decision checklist

Question Leans pre-printed Leans in-house
Annual volume per style Several thousand pieces or more A few hundred, frequently topped up
Redesigns per year Fewer than two Three or more
Finish requirement Satin, jacquard, foil, embossing Flat print is enough
Wash claim 20 machine washes or more Few washes, or none
File and version control No dedicated owner needed A single master file and an audit trail are mandatory
One judgement: a label is part of the garment, not office consumable. The printing money you save rarely covers one batch of rework.
