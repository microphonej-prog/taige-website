Trim Sampling Fees and Sample Policy: What You Pay and What Comes Back

By TAIGE Packaging  ·  Updated November 1, 2026

Almost every trim sample carries a charge, yet most buyers seeing the quotation for the first time cannot tell what they are paying for: the die, the material, the set-up time, or a presentation sample nobody actually costed. Separate the four parts and it becomes clear which you should pay, which is negotiable, and which comes back on the bulk order.

This guide breaks the sampling charge into its four components, draws the line between refundable and consumed cost, sets out the three cases where a free sample genuinely applies, covers who owns the sample and the retained reference, and lists the five lines that must appear on the quotation.

The sampling charge has four components

Component What it covers Credited on bulk
Die and plate Cutting die for shaped tags, jacquard plate for woven labels, print plate for care labels; a one-off asset you keep Usually credited once the agreed quantity is reached
Material Speciality paper, custom yarn, coated tape; charged on actual consumption No, it is consumed
Set-up and labour Short machine runs, colour matching, hand stitching and finishing No
Freight and packing Courier, sample bags, swatch and tag cards No

A useful rule: only what can be reused on your next order — a die, a plate — can be credited; what has been consumed — material, labour, freight — cannot. A quotation that lumps everything into one sampling fee guarantees an argument later.

When the sampling fee is refundable

  • The bulk order reaches the agreed quantity: common practice is to credit the whole fee, or at least the die part, with the threshold written into the quotation
  • A second sample of the same design: if the first failed through the factory's fault, the rework should not be charged again
  • Die reuse: a design that fits an existing die should not attract a die charge

The mirror case: revisions to artwork, size or material asked for by the buyer mean a new sample, and that cost sits with whoever changed the brief. Confirm it by email before sampling starts rather than explaining it when the invoice arrives.

A free sample only exists in three situations

  • A standard item from the material library: existing paper, yarn or bag format with only the print changed
  • A small evaluation quantity, typically one or two sets; beyond that it is charged at sample price
  • Freight paid by the receiver: a free sample sent freight collect is the normal convention

Anything needing a new die, a new jacquard plate or a special coating is almost never genuinely free. The money is recovered elsewhere, usually in the bulk unit price, so ask which design the words free sample actually refer to.

Who owns the sample and the retained reference

A buyer who paid for the sample owns it. The factory nevertheless keeps a reference piece for repeat orders and bulk colour matching. Keep that reference for six to twelve months, long enough to cover a selling season: matching a repeat against the retained piece settles colour arguments far faster than comparing photographs.

Five lines that must appear on the quotation

  1. The total sampling fee and its split across die, material, labour and freight
  2. The quantity threshold, how much is credited, and whether as cash or as a deduction from the bulk invoice
  3. The sampling lead time counted from artwork approval and receipt of the fee, so both sides count from the same date
  4. How many revisions are included free and how further rounds are charged
  5. Die ownership: on payment the die belongs to the buyer and can be transferred to another supplier

The fifth line is the one most often left out, and it decides whether switching supplier later means paying for the die twice. For brands running shaped tags and jacquard woven labels, a die can easily cost as much as several thousand tags.

One test: if a factory cannot split its sampling charge into a reusable die and consumed cost, its quotation system is not yet built for brand clients.
